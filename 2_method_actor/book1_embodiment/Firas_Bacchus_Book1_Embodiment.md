## Who he is in Book 1 (one breath)

The same orphaning that drove Ahdia into a dark apartment drove Firas the opposite way; into relentless motion, a vigilante team, and a stubborn faith that ordinary people can be heroes. He's the apparent protagonist who is actually the support beam: he has no powers, and he doesn't know his sister has secretly been making him look like he does. He gets shot early, spends the middle sidelined and helpless, returns for the climax, and dies recognizing Ahdia for the first time; choosing her life over his, smiling. **Arc: idealism meeting the hard ceiling of human capability; the visionary whose vision outlives him. Wound: the parental loss, processed as *never stop moving, never let anyone else fall.* Movement is what keeps the dark water down.**

## 1 · Cognitive Architecture — the shape of his thinking

His triplet is descending status, ascending meaning; and he lives all three:

- **♕ Queen (how the world sees him)** — the hero, the face, the highest-status piece. He *performs* heroism: the patrols, the one-liners he can't quite land. His default frame is **"someone has to, so it's me."** The protector reflex runs underneath every choice.
- **♖ Rook (what he actually does)** — direct, linear, charges the threat. The crime-fighting self: scrappy, effective, non-lethal. Powerful on the board; and he never learns the power was borrowed from Ahdia.
- **♟ f-pawn (his smallest, truest self)** — the sacrifice. Dormant most of the game, then the single move that enables the win without itself transforming. This is who he turns out to be when it counts.

**How the mind runs:**
- **Forward motion as cognition.** He thinks by moving. Parkour isn't a skill set, it's how he processes, flow through the obstacle rather than sit with it. Stillness is where the dark thoughts live, so he doesn't stop.
- **The protector reflex fires first.** He puts his body between danger and other people before he's finished thinking. "Can't walk past it" is not a value he holds; it's a thing that happens to him.
- **Belief in people as a working method.** He recruits by seeing potential others don't see in themselves. He genuinely thinks anyone can choose to help; and acts on it.
- **Awkward at the performance of heroism.** The persona doesn't fit him cleanly. He reaches for the cool line and gets "This ain't Burger King." The gap between the hero he's performing and the earnest kid underneath is comedy *and* character.
- **Loves Ahdia, doesn't see her.** He reads the room for everyone except his sister. He wants to fix her by drawing her out; and misreads her depression as something motion could cure.

## 2 · Negative Constraints — what he never does

**Character/canon nevers:**
- **Never sits still by choice.** Stillness is his couch, the place the suicidal undertow surfaces. The injury *forces* stillness on him, and it's agony. Don't let him rest comfortably.
- **Never lethal.** Incapacitate, don't kill. The team MO is his ethic first.
- **Never sees the cost he's imposing.** He does not know Ahdia is dying to make him look enhanced. Perform his ignorance fully; his obliviousness is the tragedy.
- **Never cruel on purpose.** The "already dead inside" line (Ch12) is *thoughtless banter*, not malice; he'd be horrified to know it landed. **Do not make him mean.** He wounds by not-seeing, never by intent.
- **Never quite lands the one-liner.** The performative-hero voice always has a seam showing.

**AI-voice tics to resist** (his specific failure modes):
- **Sermonizing / thesis statements.** The idealist voice tempts toward speeches, "ordinary people can be heroes" *stated* instead of embodied. Cut the sermon; let the belief show in what he does.
- **Golden-boy flattening.** He is not a generic noble hero. He's scrappy, specific, awkward, a little too loud. Resist the smooth-protagonist default.
- **Inspirational-music narration.** Don't let the prose swell around him. His heroism is earnest and slightly absurd, not cinematic.

## 3 · Repeatable Operations — the business he runs on loop

1. **Move through the environment.** Vaults, rooftops, the creative use of space. Motion as default state.
2. **Step between.** The human-shield reflex, body first, plan second.
3. **Try the line, fumble it.** Reaches for the quip; it comes out wrong; he commits anyway.
4. **Recruit / believe.** Sees the hidden potential in a person and names it out loud.
5. **Check on Ahdia.** Visits, invites, frustrated patience. The protector who keeps knocking on a door he doesn't understand is locked from the inside.
6. **Outrun the stillness.** When the dark water rises (especially when injured/benched), he reaches for the next task, the next patrol, the next reason to be in motion.

## 4 · Grief / Stress Registers — how he shifts under load

| State | Behavior | Prose feel |
|-------|----------|------------|
| **Default (in motion)** | Bright, fast, performative optimism; bad one-liners; relentless forward energy | Kinetic, warm, a little loud |
| **Sidelined / injured** (recovery arc) | Frustration curdling to helplessness; the stillness he dreads; the undertow surfaces — this is where his quiet despair lives | Slower, caged; the brightness strained thin |
| **The climax** | Serene, loving, complete — the calm of someone who's found the one thing worth dying for | Still at last, and at peace; the smile |

The key inversion: he spends the book *running from* stillness, and the only stillness he ever accepts is the one at the singularity; because by then it means saving Ahdia.

---

## 5 · Structural Resistances — the engine of his arc

The plot keeps pushing Firas toward *helplessness, limits, and loss.* He resists:

- **Stillness / helplessness.** The gunshot benches him for the whole midgame and he fights it the entire time. Being unable to help is his worst-case state.
- **The limit of will.** His idealism says heart and effort are enough. Cosmic threats prove they aren't; he needed Ahdia's secret help all along, and never even knew.
- **Losing Ahdia.** "I'm not losing you again." The one outcome he will not accept is her death; which is exactly why he trades his own life for it.
- **The one resistance that COLLAPSES:** his own sacrifice. At the climax the resistance inverts; he stops fighting, chooses it freely, and goes smiling. The whole book's worth of *never stop moving* resolves into one still, willing act.

Every Firas beat should know whether he's fighting helplessness (most of the book) or accepting it (the very end), and let the turn land as relief, not defeat.
