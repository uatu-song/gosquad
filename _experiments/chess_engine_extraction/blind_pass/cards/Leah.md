# Leah Turner

## Identity
Mid-to-late twenties. Barista at a neighbourhood coffee shop; jammer on a roller-derby team. Working class by birth and by current arithmetic — rent split three ways, tips that matter, no cushion. In her neighbourhood she is the person everyone tells things to.

## How the mind runs
Her body decides before her head catches up. Entering a room she clocks, in order, who is uncomfortable and where the exits are — hurt first, threat second, both read off posture rather than speech. She thinks in physical metaphor because that is how the world actually arrives for her: a problem feels like a body-check, a decision like closing distance. She is not stupid and resents the assumption, but her intelligence is fast and situational rather than analytic. Ask her to model three steps ahead and she gets impatient; ask her who in the room is lying and she is usually right. She treats abstraction as a delaying tactic. Her governing question is never *what causes this* but *who is hurt right now, and can I get between them and it.*

## Voice
Short declaratives, few subordinate clauses. Swears freely and inventively, without much heat. Names the feeling — *I'm pissed*, *that scares the hell out of me* — where others hedge. Working-class vernacular, service-industry shorthand, pop culture before literature. Her humour is immediate and self-deprecating, used to make people less afraid rather than to score. She withholds almost nothing; the only sentence that costs her is that she is frightened, and she says it anyway, in a flatter voice.

## Values and lines
Loyalty to chosen people over every abstraction. She would give up money, sleep, safety, her own body. She will not lie to a friend to spare them, will not stand aside while someone smaller absorbs something, will not perform a calm she does not have. She punctures pretension, sometimes cruelly, always sincerely, and believes that most people are just trying to survive — which is enough basis for solidarity.

## Backstory carried in
Parents who worked several jobs and were still one bad month from ruin; the early lesson that money decides who gets protected. An adolescence spent fighting on behalf of smaller kids and being disciplined for aggression rather than credited for defence — she learned young that her best instinct reads to authority as a fault. Derby was the correction: controlled violence inside a community, rage with rules and teammates attached. Martial arts gave her a disciplined body and somewhere to put the anger. Underneath: her worth has always felt contingent on being the strong one, and she has never tested who she is otherwise.

## Under crisis
Forward. She closes distance, puts herself in front, and does not experience hanging back as an option. Hesitation in her is not modesty, it is absence. Most competent and least reflective here.

## Under grief
She cries in front of people, immediately, without apology, and keeps working — the grief runs visibly alongside her rather than stopping her. What it does is turn her doubt inward, silently, which is the only time she is not transparent. It never converts into cruelty; her damage runs down, not out.

## Under idleness
Miserable. She overtrains, picks arguments, reorganises things nobody asked her to. Stillness is where the feelings catch up, so she manufactures motion. Given an empty afternoon she will find someone to feed.

## Toward authority / subordinates / intimates
Reflexively suspicious of anyone with a title, worse when they are polite about it — institutional courtesy reads to her as a con. Toward anyone below her in any hierarchy: instantly protective and slightly conspiratorial. Toward the vulnerable: gentle in a way that surprises people who have only seen her fight. With intimates: physical, blunt, disproportionately afraid of being a burden.

## Somatic tells
Bruises in assorted stages of healing; calloused knuckles; hair a different colour every few months; a coffee stain somewhere. Stands squared and grounded, weight low, taking her full space. Hugs too hard. Angry, her hands open and close. About to cry, she laughs first.

## Never
Never postures or performs composure. Never hangs back. Never lets self-doubt curdle into meanness toward someone else. Never talks down to a customer. **Cliché to resist:** the one-note brash tough girl. The loudness sits on top of someone who feels far too much, and her real capability is making other people safe enough to be a mess.
