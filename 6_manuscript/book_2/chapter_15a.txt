# Chapter 15A: Black Tie

---

Tess sat on the edge of Leta's bed with two dresses laid out and no intention of wearing either.

The gala invitation was on the dresser. Thick cardstock, embossed lettering, the kind of paper that costs more per sheet than the coffee she used to serve. *Chief Robert Whitford cordially invites you to the Annual Law Enforcement Appreciation Gala.* Her name handwritten on the envelope. His handwriting. She knew it from birthday cards, from permission slips, from the transfer order that put Webb back on active duty three weeks after he killed Isaiah Bennett.

She'd been planning to cancel. Had the text drafted in her phone: *Not feeling well, Dad. Next year.* Clean, simple, no follow-up required. She'd been planning to stay home with Leta, order Thai food, fall asleep on the couch during whatever true crime show they were pretending to watch. Normal girlfriend stuff. The version of her life that didn't require a cover story.

Then Ruth had forwarded the guest list.

Her father and the Secretary of Defense. Same venue, same night. Ruth's language had been careful, clinical: *Patterson confirmed attending. Your father is hosting. Institutional arrangements on the agenda — worth knowing what they're planning.* Tess had read between the lines: powerful men were making deals at a cocktail party, and her father was at the table.

She couldn't send the text.

The problem was the date. She couldn't bring Leta. Not to this crowd, not to this room. And she wouldn't walk in alone and perform straight for a ballroom full of men who'd covered up a murder, not without someone watching her back. She'd been turning it over for an hour, running scenarios, cataloguing options like exit routes.

The bedroom door was open. From the living room, the sound of Korede's laptop keys—he'd been at the kitchen table since five, helping Leta document harassment patterns. Evidence chains. The kid had a system. Color-coded spreadsheets. Timestamps.

Seventeen years old with a system for tracking death threats against his sister.

She heard his chair push back. Footsteps. He appeared in the doorway, leaning against the frame with his arms crossed, looking at the dresses on the bed.

"You're going," he said. Not a question.

"I'm deciding."

"You've been deciding for an hour. You're going."

Tess didn't answer. Korede watched her the way he watched everything—quiet, cataloguing, filing details for later. He'd been doing it more since the harassment escalated. Less of the kid she'd met at dinner months ago. More of something harder.

"Leta told me about the gala," he said. "Your dad's thing."

"It's not a thing. It's a work event."

"With the cops who cleared Webb."

Tess looked at him. He held the look.

"I can't bring Leta," she said. "And I'm not going alone."

"So don't."

"I don't have—"

"I'll go."

The words landed in the room before she could stop them. She studied his face. Serious. No bravado, no performance.

"Absolutely not."

"Why?"

"Because you're in high school. Because these are the people who covered up Isaiah Bennett's murder. Because—"

"Because it'll be a room full of white cops and I'm a Black teenager." He said it flat, like weather. "I know."

"Korede—"

"My sister gets death threats every day. I'm documenting them in spreadsheets with color-coded tabs. I'm already in this." He uncrossed his arms. "So either I'm in it, or I'm pretending I'm not. And I'm done pretending."

Tess heard Leta in that. The same stubbornness, the same jaw-set refusal to look away from the thing everyone wanted her to look away from. Genetic, maybe. Or just what happens to a family when the system puts a target on one of them.

"These aren't internet trolls," Tess said. "These are armed men in dress uniforms who killed someone and got away with it. If you lose your composure for one second—"

"I won't."

"You don't know that."

"I code-switch at school every day. AP classes, faculty meetings, hallway conversations. I know what room I'm in before I walk through the door." He shrugged. "This is just a bigger room."

Tess sat with that. He was right and he was wrong and both things were true at the same time.

"Fine," she said. "But we need a cover that holds."

---

They built it in twenty minutes.

Dating for three months. Met through mutual friends at a community cleanup. He was pre-engineering, honor roll. She was in security consulting—close enough to true that she wouldn't trip on the details. The story was simple because simple was what survived scrutiny.

Tess coached him on couple mechanics. Where to put his hand. How close to stand. The rhythm of two people who are comfortable enough to not perform.

"Don't hold my hand too tight," she said. "Relaxed grip. Like you've done it a hundred times."

"I have held a hand before."

"Not in a room where everyone's watching."

He practiced the grip. It was fine. Clinical, functional, the way siblings hold hands crossing a street—trust without chemistry. It would read close enough under ballroom lighting.

"My father thinks I'm straight," Tess said. "He's wrong about a lot of things and this is one of them. If he pushes, let me redirect."

"I get it. I've got relatives who still think I'm going to marry a nice Nigerian girl."

"Then you know the drill."

Korede looked at the rental tux on the back of the chair. Standard fit. Tess had grabbed it that afternoon—his measurements close enough to a stock size. He'd be one of maybe five Black people in the room. He'd be the youngest by a decade. He knew all of this. She could see him knowing it.

"One more thing," she said. "If it gets bad—if someone says something and you feel it starting to slip—find me. Touch my elbow. I'll get us out."

"It won't get bad."

"It will."

He looked at her. Nodded once.

They headed out.

---

The Caledonia Grand Hotel had been hosting police galas since before Tess was born. She knew the building the way she knew her father's precinct—every hallway, every service entrance, every blind spot in the security camera coverage. She'd been coming to these events since she was twelve, her father's hand on her shoulder, his voice introducing her to men whose names she'd forgotten and whose handshakes she hadn't.

The lobby was marble and chandeliers and the particular smell of expensive nothing. Tess adjusted Korede's tie in the reflection of the glass doors—performance, not necessity. Couple behavior. His tie was fine.

"Ready?"

"Yeah."

His voice was steady. His pulse was visible in his neck.

They entered.

The ballroom was exactly what she expected. Three hundred people in black tie. American flags flanking a stage. A string quartet playing something classical that the crowd talked over. White tablecloths, white flowers, white faces. The room was 95 percent white and the other 5 percent were service staff.

Korede's hand found hers. Relaxed grip. Like they'd done it a hundred times.

Heads turned. Not dramatic—the slow, swiveling attention of a room registering something new. Tess watched the reactions land: surprise, assessment, the particular tightening around the eyes that people thought they hid.

She squeezed his hand once. *I see it too.*

Her father materialized from the crowd the way he always did—big smile, pressed uniform, the command presence that had carried him from patrol to Chief in twenty-two years. He looked genuinely pleased.

"Tess." He opened his arms. She stepped into the hug, let it last the right amount of time, stepped back. Muscle memory. "And this must be—"

"Korede, sir." Hand extended, posture straight. "Pleasure to meet you."

Her father's handshake was the one he gave suspects and subordinates—firm, assessing, held a beat too long. "Good grip. What does your father do?"

"Logistics management, sir."

True. Warehouse supervisor at a distribution center. The kind of answer that mapped onto this room's vocabulary without revealing anything they'd look down on.

"Good, good. Hard work builds character." Her father's hand on Korede's shoulder. Proprietary. "Come on in. Let me introduce you."

The introductions started immediately. Her father's colleagues, their wives, the particular social architecture of a police department that socializes as a unit and closes ranks as a reflex. Tess catalogued faces, names, handshake pressure, eye-contact duration. Tactical habit. Every interaction was data.

The first one landed before they'd reached the bar.

An older woman, wife of someone senior, champagne in hand, pearls catching the light. She looked at Korede and her face did the thing—the smile that widened a fraction too much, the head tilt, the voice pitched up.

"Oh, how *exotic*! Where are you from?"

"Caledonia, ma'am. Born and raised."

"No, I mean—where are you *from* from?"

Korede's jaw tightened. Tess felt it through their linked hands—the micro-tension of a body holding itself in place. She squeezed. *Stay.*

"My parents are from Lagos. Nigeria."

"Fascinating! You speak English so *well*."

Korede said nothing. His hand in hers was rigid now. Tess steered them toward the bar.

"Baby, let's get drinks," she said. Girlfriend voice. The performance of ease.

It didn't stop.

Her father introduced them to a cluster of officers. Dress uniforms, medals, the practiced charm of men who knew how to work a room.

"My daughter and her friend Korede."

*Friend.* Not boyfriend. She let it pass.

"Korede? Interesting name. African?"

"Nigerian."

"Nigeria! Great country. Lagos, right? Heard it's wild down there."

Korede's smile was precise. Calibrated. Tess recognized the craftsmanship—he'd built that smile in hallways and classrooms and faculty offices, assembled it from years of practice until it looked effortless. It wasn't.

"What do you do, son? Student?"

"Junior at Caledonia High. Honor roll."

"Good for you!" A clap on the shoulder. Fraternal, condescending. "We need more success stories like yours."

Tess watched Korede absorb it. The *like yours*. The invisible qualifier that turned a compliment into a sorting mechanism.

Another officer, drink in hand, leaning in: "You know, you're one of the good ones. Not like those—" He caught himself. Smiled. "Well, you know what I mean."

Korede's free hand curled into a fist under the table. Tess saw it. The officer didn't.

The same officer, continuing, because alcohol and comfortable company erased whatever filter he'd briefly engaged: "That Bennett kid, now *he* was trouble. Resisting arrest, mouthing off—"

The table went quiet. Not because anyone objected. Because they were waiting for the rest, the way you wait for a punchline you already know.

"—play stupid games, win stupid prizes."

Laughter. Three officers. Loud enough to carry.

Korede stopped breathing. Tess felt it—the absence of his ribcage expanding, the stillness that comes right before something breaks. His eyes were locked on the officer who'd spoken. The officer who was laughing about Isaiah Bennett's death at a table with champagne flutes and linen napkins while Isaiah's name was still spray-painted on a memorial wall six blocks away.

Tess moved.

---

"Baby, you look pale. You okay?"

She was already pulling him up from the chair, hand on his arm, girlfriend concern painted over tactical extraction. Korede's legs worked because she guided them. His eyes were somewhere else.

"Think the shrimp isn't sitting right," she said to the table. Apologetic smile. Normal girl, normal problem.

"Hope your boyfriend feels better!" someone called after them.

She got him into the hallway. Quieter here—marble floors, muted lighting, the distant thrum of the quartet through closed doors. She let go of his hand and turned to face him.

"Breathe."

He was shaking. Fine tremor, jaw to fingertips. His eyes were wet and furious.

"They killed him." His voice was low, compressed, the sound of something being held in a space too small for it. "They killed him and they're *laughing* about it."

"I know."

"They called him a thug. Isaiah was an engineer. He was trying to *help people*."

"I know."

"He had research and a career and he was doing the right thing and they shot him and they're in there drinking champagne and calling him—"

His voice broke. Not crying—cracking. The sound of a composure that had been engineered to hold finding its load limit.

Tess gave him ten seconds. Counted them. Then:

"I need what they know. About CADENS, about the cover-up, about what my father and the Secretary of Defense are coordinating in there. If I don't get it tonight, Leta stays exposed. Do you understand?"

His breathing ragged. He wiped his eyes with the back of his hand.

"I don't know if I can go back in there."

"Then don't. Walk to the balcony, get air, wait for me."

"No." He straightened. The shaking hadn't stopped but his voice hardened around it. "Leta needs this."

"Korede—"

"I said no."

Footsteps. Someone coming down the corridor. Tess shifted instantly—hand on Korede's face, bodies close, the geometry of two people having a private moment. His skin was hot under her palm.

"Just breathe," she murmured. Girlfriend voice, but the words were real.

The footsteps slowed. Stopped.

"Miss Whitford, isn't it?"

Tess looked up. Expensive suit, no uniform. Silver hair, tanned, the particular grooming of someone who had people for that. Eyes that calculated before they smiled.

She knew the face from Ruth's briefings. Secretary of Defense Arthur Patterson.

"Your father speaks highly of you."

Tess adjusted. Dropped the intimate posture, stepped back half a step, kept her hand on Korede's arm. Flustered girlfriend, caught in a private moment. "Yes sir. Sorry—this is my boyfriend, Korede. He's not feeling well."

Patterson's eyes moved to Korede. Assessing. "The young man who's feeling ill?"

Korede straightened. The code-switch was immediate—one breath to the next, broken boy to composed young man. "Just needed air, sir. Gets warm in there."

"Indeed." Patterson's gaze stayed on him a beat too long, then shifted back to Tess. "Celebrating the department's resilience tonight."

"Resilience, sir?"

"Recent difficulties. That unfortunate incident with the Bennett boy."

Tess felt Korede's arm tense under her hand. She pressed her fingers down. *Hold.*

"But the investigation cleared our officers. System working as intended."

Tess made her face do the thing—open, slightly naive, the Chief's daughter who didn't follow the details. "That must be a relief. For everyone involved."

Patterson studied her. Weighing something.

"Your father was quite involved in ensuring... proper procedure."

She caught the phrasing. *Ensuring proper procedure* was not *conducting an investigation*. It was managing an outcome. She filed it.

"As were our partners at CADENS. Excellent cooperation between agencies."

CADENS. Just like that. Dropped into conversation like a name at a dinner party.

"CADENS?" Tess widened her eyes. Naive. "The superhero people?"

"Among other things." The faintest smile. "They have specialized capabilities. Forensic analysis that goes beyond what local departments can manage."

"I didn't know they worked with police investigations."

"They don't, typically. But this case had unique elements."

"Unique how?"

Patterson's expression shifted. Subtle—the smile thinning, the assessment sharpening. She'd pushed one question too far.

"Your father didn't mention? The witness situation was... complex."

Tess pulled back. "Dad doesn't really talk work much. Keeps it separate from family."

Patterson relaxed. "Smart man. Boundaries are important."

Korede spoke. Controlled, measured, the voice of a young man making polite conversation with an elder: "So CADENS helped clear the officers?"

Patterson's eyes snapped to him. Sharp now. "They provided technical analysis."

"What kind of technical analysis?"

"Ballistics, trajectory, threat assessment." A pause. "Beyond typical police forensics."

Tess heard what he wasn't saying. CADENS hadn't analyzed the evidence. They'd replaced it.

Patterson checked his watch. The gesture was performance—he knew exactly what time it was. "I should return. Your father will wonder where you've gone."

He turned to leave. Paused.

"He worries about you. Wants you safe."

The words hung in the hallway—ambient, fatherly, the temperature of a warning.

"I know, sir," Tess said. "I'm very careful."

Patterson held her gaze for one second longer than small talk required. Then he walked back toward the ballroom, footsteps measured on marble.

Tess and Korede stood in the corridor until the sound disappeared.

"He knows." Korede's voice was barely above a whisper. "He knows we're looking into it."

Tess replayed the conversation. The precision of his phrasing. The way he'd named CADENS casually, then watched for the reaction. The warning dressed as concern.

"Maybe," she said. "Or he's testing whether we know enough to be a problem."

"Is there a difference?"

"Yeah. One means we're flagged. The other means we still have room to move."

She looked at him. The shaking had stopped during the conversation—adrenaline overriding everything else. But his eyes were hollow. The cost of code-switching through a conversation with the man who'd paid for a murder cover-up was sitting in the set of his shoulders.

"You're done," she said. Not a question.

He opened his mouth to argue.

"Korede. You got the most important thing tonight. CADENS, Patterson, my father. The triangle. That's enough."

"What's your cover for me leaving?"

"Oysters. Stomach problems. I stay because I'm daddy's girl and this is his night."

He didn't move.

"What happens when you're in there alone?"

"I've been in rooms with these people since I was twelve. I know the drill."

"And if something goes wrong?"

"Then you're outside. You call Ruth."

He looked at her. She looked back.

"Don't tell Leta the specifics," Tess said. "Not what they said about Isaiah. She'll try to burn down city hall."

"She should."

"Maybe. But not tonight."

He nodded. The nod of someone accepting something he disagreed with because the person asking had earned the right to ask.

They walked back into the ballroom together. Korede's face was a mask she recognized—she'd worn her own version every time she walked into this building. He moved through the crowd with his shoulders straight and his eyes forward and his mouth set in the polite smile that cost him something every second he held it.

Her father intercepted them near the stage. "Son, you look terrible."

"Think the oysters, sir. Should probably head home."

"Take him, Tess. I'll call a car."

"Dad, I can't leave your event. This is important to you."

His face softened. The pleased expression of a father whose daughter chose him. Tess felt it like a blade.

"I'll be fine, sir." Korede shook her father's hand. The performance was flawless—grateful, slightly embarrassed, the good boyfriend who didn't want to ruin the evening. "Don't want to ruin Tess's night."

She walked him to the entrance. Through the lobby, past the marble and chandeliers, to the glass doors where the city waited outside.

"Park across the street," she said quietly. "Watch the entrance. If I text *headache*, something's wrong."

Korede pushed through the doors. Cold air hit them both. He stopped on the sidewalk and turned back.

"Tess."

"Yeah."

He looked at her. Seventeen years old, rental tux, eyes older than the rest of him.

"The officer who said *play stupid games*. Third from the left at the table. Silver hair, class ring. I want that name."

Tess held his gaze. "I'll get it."

He turned and walked across the street. She watched until he reached the car—Leta's car, borrowed for the night—and got in. Watched the interior light go on and off. Watched him sit there in the dark.

She turned back to the ballroom.

Her phone buzzed. Korede: *Parked. I'm here.*

She put the phone away and walked back in.

*End of Chapter 15A*
