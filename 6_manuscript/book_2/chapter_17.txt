# Chapter 17: Treatment

---

Earlier that morning.

---

The penthouse was quiet in the way expensive buildings are quiet—not silence but the specific absence of street noise, ambulance sirens, other people's lives. Ruth let herself in with the code Ahdia had given her months ago. The entry hall was dark. The living room beyond it was darker. Curtains drawn against a Saturday morning.

She found Ahdia on the couch.

Not sleeping. Not watching TV, though the TV was on—some nature documentary, volume low enough to be texture. Ahdia was sitting with her knees pulled up and her head against the cushion at an angle that said she hadn't moved in hours. Maybe longer.

"Hey." Ruth set the medical kit on the counter. "How are you doing?"

"Living the dream." Ahdia's voice was flat. Not deadpan—depleted. The humor was there but the engine behind it was idling.

Ruth looked at her. Really looked. The assessment started before she'd made the conscious decision to run it—sixteen years of medical training wired into her visual cortex like a second operating system. Weight loss visible in the collarbone, the wrists. Skin tone off—grayish undertone beneath the brown that meant cellular stress, not sunlight deficiency. Tremor in the hands, fine enough to miss from across the room, obvious from three feet.

"When did you eat last?"

"Define eat."

"Food. In your body."

"There was a granola bar at some point. Yesterday, maybe."

Ruth opened the kit. Didn't comment. She'd scheduled this check-in herself—outside Ryu's treatment rotation, because she wanted to see the numbers without his annotations softening them. The medical protocol was its own language and she let it speak for her—blood pressure cuff first, then the cellular baseline scanner Ryu had calibrated for Ahdia's specific degradation pattern. The readings would tell her what the conversation wouldn't.

"Arm."

Ahdia extended her left arm without looking up. The gesture was automatic—they'd done this enough times that the choreography had its own muscle memory. Ruth wrapped the cuff, pumped, waited. Blood pressure low. Not dangerous. Consistent with dehydration and exhaustion.

The baseline scanner was the one that mattered. Ruth pressed it to Ahdia's inner wrist and watched the numbers climb into focus on the small screen.

54.2%.

She kept her face still. Fourteen points down from the last reading she'd seen, which had been 68%. One week ago.

"How many missions this week?"

"One. Routine."

Ruth looked at the scanner. Looked at Ahdia's arm. The cellular damage distribution was wrong. A single routine mission produced a specific signature—concentrated along the translocation pathways, heaviest at the extremities, diminishing toward the core. What Ruth was seeing was something else. Diffuse damage across multiple muscle groups. Layered degradation, each layer at a slightly different stage of recovery. Like geological strata. Each layer was a separate event.

That wasn't one mission. That was five. Maybe more.

Ruth finished the reading. Logged it. Set the scanner down.

"Treatment will take about twenty minutes. Same protocol."

"I know the drill."

Ruth prepped the CR-7 infusion. The procedure was clinical—IV access, calibration, cellular integration monitoring. It was also the most honest physical contact she had with what Ahdia was doing to herself. The body didn't lie the way the mouth did. Under Ruth's hands, the damage was legible.

She worked in silence. Ahdia's eyes drifted to the TV. A snow leopard tracked something across a mountain ledge. The narrator said something about patience and cold.

Sixteen minutes. The infusion cycled through. Ahdia's baseline climbed two points—56.2%. That was less response than it should have been. The treatments were losing efficacy. The cells were too damaged to integrate fully.

Ruth disconnected the IV. Packed the kit. Stood up.

And didn't leave.

---

"What are you doing?" Ahdia said.

"We need to talk."

"We are talking. I'm being delightful."

Ruth pulled a chair from the dining table. Positioned it across from the couch. Sat down. The posture said: this isn't casual and I'm not going anywhere.

"Your baseline is 54 percent."

"I know."

"Down from 68 last week."

"I know that too."

"The cellular damage pattern doesn't match one routine mission. It matches sustained translocation across multiple sites over multiple days."

Ahdia's jaw tightened. She didn't look away from the TV.

"So I'll ask again," Ruth said. "How many missions this week?"

Silence. The snow leopard caught its prey. The narrator didn't celebrate.

"It doesn't matter how many—"

"It matters because at this rate you have days. Not weeks. Days."

"I know the math, Ruth."

"Then explain it to me. Because the math says you're choosing to die and I'd like to understand why."

Ahdia stood. The movement cost her—she caught herself on the arm of the couch, steadied, straightened. Performance of competence. Ruth saw the tremor run through her legs before she locked her knees.

"People needed help. I helped them. That's what we do."

"That's what the team does. Together. With support and medical oversight and someone tracking baselines so nobody burns out. What you're doing is something else."

"I'm doing what I can with the time I have."

"You sound like Firas."

The name landed in the room and stayed there.

Ahdia turned. "Don't."

"You're burning out the same way. Working until you die."

"He died in an explosion. I'm dying slowly from helping people. Different."

"Both of you chose mission over survival. That's the same."

Ahdia's mouth opened. Closed. She sat back down—not a choice, a failure of legs. The couch caught her.

"Firas didn't have time powers," she said. Quieter.

"He had the same impulse. Do more, do faster, don't stop, don't rest, because resting means someone else pays. He was going to propose to me and he couldn't even take a night off to buy the ring. He had a friend do it."

Ahdia looked at her.

"I found the receipt after," Ruth said. Flat. The flatness of a fact she'd carried so long it had worn smooth. "Dated three days before the explosion. He was going to ask me on Saturday. He died on Wednesday."

The TV played. The snow leopard had vanished from the frame. The mountain was empty.

"I'm sorry," Ahdia said.

"I know you are."

Ahdia didn't answer. Her hands were trembling in her lap. Not from emotion—from cellular exhaustion. Her body was telling the truth her voice wouldn't.

"I need you to stall the team," Ahdia said. "Buy me time. A week. I can—"

"No."

"Ruth—"

"No."

Ruth sat with the word for thirty seconds. She counted them. Not because the silence was performative—because she needed every one of those seconds to hold the line against the part of her that wanted to say yes. Thirty seconds of the caretaker in her screaming *fix it, help her, say yes, cover for her, keep her alive at any cost* and the doctor in her knowing that the cost was the problem.

"I won't provide treatment if you won't stop the secret missions."

Ahdia stared at her.

"That's not—you can't—"

"I can. And I am." Ruth's voice was level. Not angry. The anger had burned off somewhere in the thirty seconds and left something worse—the flat acceptance of someone who had already grieved the decision before making it. "Every treatment I give you buys time you spend destroying yourself. I'm done being the reason you can keep going to places that are killing you."

"Without treatment I have thirty-six hours."

"At 54 percent, yes. The eleven-day window is for a functional baseline. You burned past that weeks ago."

"So you're—what? Letting me die?"

"I'm letting you choose. Missions or treatment. Not both."

Ahdia's face moved through something Ruth couldn't fully read. Anger, disbelief, something underneath both that looked like the specific panic of a person who'd been running so hard they forgot what standing still felt like.

"You don't understand what I'm doing—"

"You're right. I don't. Because you won't tell me."

"I can't—"

"Then I can't help you."

"You're my best friend," Ahdia said.

"That's why I'm saying no."

The TV had gone to commercial. Something about laundry detergent. The transition from snow leopard to detergent was exactly the kind of thing Ahdia would normally comment on—*nothing says emotional catharsis like Tide Pods*—but she didn't. She sat on the couch with her hands shaking and her baseline at 54 percent and looked at Ruth the way Ruth had looked at Firas's name on the engagement ring receipt.

Like something she'd needed was being taken away by someone who was right to take it.

Ruth stood. Picked up the medical kit.

"Seventy-two hours," she said. "That's the minimum between treatments from now on. You coordinate with Ryu, you stay local, and you tell me the truth about where you've been. Those are the conditions."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I walk. And you handle this alone."

Ruth crossed to the door. Her hand on the handle. She didn't turn around because turning around meant seeing Ahdia's face and she'd already used every second of resolve she had.

"Ruth."

She waited.

"I'm scared."

Two words. No deflection, no redirect, no TV reference to diffuse the weight. Just the thing underneath everything Ahdia had said for the last six months, finally unclothed.

"I know," Ruth said. "Me too."

She opened the door and walked out and closed it behind her and stood in the hallway of a luxury building at six in the morning and pressed her back against the wall and breathed.

The elevator was twelve steps away. She counted them. She counted the breaths. She counted the seconds until her hands stopped shaking—eighteen, which was three more than the last time she'd walked away from someone she loved and known it might be the last time.

She took the elevator down. She drove to the academy. She set up the back room for a meeting she'd call that night, after Tess's gala, after whatever came next.

The medical kit sat on the passenger seat. She didn't open it again.

*End of Chapter 17*
