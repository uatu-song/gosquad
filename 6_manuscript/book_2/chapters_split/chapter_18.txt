# Chapter 18: Treatment
<!-- SOURCE: chapter_08.txt -->

---

The doorbell rang at 9 AM, exactly when it always did.

Ahdia pulled herself off the couch—same couch, same position, same pattern of indentation in the cushions from days of not moving. The apartment had accumulated a new layer of chaos since Ruth’s visit: more takeout containers, more streaming queues paused mid-episode, more evidence of a person not quite coping.

She opened the door. Dr. Ryu Matsuda stood in the hallway with his medical case, CADENS credentials visible on his lanyard. Same concerned expression he always wore. Same careful posture that suggested he was already cataloging her condition before she said a word.

“Ms. Bacchus.”

“You can still call me Ahdia. We’ve been doing this for months.”

“Ahdia.” He smiled—small, careful, professionally appropriate. “How are you feeling?”

“Like I’ve been watching TV for a week straight and forgot what sunlight looks like.” She stepped aside to let him in. “So, normal.”

Ryu set up his equipment on the coffee table—same spot Ruth had used, same spot his equipment always occupied. The routine was familiar now: scanner, synthesis device, vials that glowed in colors she’d stopped asking about.

“Treatment first,” he said, “then we can discuss your numbers.”

Ahdia settled onto the couch, rolling up her sleeve without being asked. The injection site was a constellation of small bruises—too many treatments, too close together, her body keeping score even when her mind tried not to.

The scanner hummed as he ran it over her arm. She watched his face, looking for the micro-expressions that would tell her how bad the news was.

“Sixty-eight percent,” he said. “Down from seventy-three last week.”

“That’s . . . not good?”

“It’s within expected degradation range.” He prepared the injection with practiced efficiency. “Your cellular integrity fluctuates based on power usage, stress levels, even sleep patterns. Five percent variance isn’t alarming.”

“But it’s not great.”

“It’s not great.” He met her eyes. “Have you been sleeping?”

“Define sleeping.”

“More than three hours at a time. In a bed. Not on this couch.”

Ahdia didn’t answer. The couch had become her default location—close to the screens, close to the remote, close to the particular numbness that came from watching other people’s stories instead of living her own.

The needle entered her arm. Cold first, spreading through her bloodstream like ice water. Then warmth—the cellular repair activating, her body recognizing the treatment and responding. Then nothing. Just the familiar numbness of being kept human by science she barely understood.

“Twenty minutes for full integration,” Ryu said, monitoring the readout. “You know the routine.”

She did. Sit still, let the treatment work, don’t use powers for at least an hour afterward. The rules of her existence, handed to her by doctors who cared whether she lived or died.

Ryu settled into the chair across from her, waiting out the integration period. This was when he usually made small talk—weather, city news, the particular brand of nothing that filled the space between medical procedures.

Today he seemed distracted.

“Everything okay?” Ahdia asked. “You seem . . . elsewhere.”

“CADENS facility news.” He shook his head slightly. “Politics. Nothing that affects your treatment.”

“Tell me anyway. I could use something to think about besides my own brain.”

Ryu considered this. “Southwest lab got requisitioned this week. Government project, everyone reassigned or loaned out.”

“What kind of project?”

“Fusion energy research. Stellarator prototype.” He said it like it was office gossip. “The President-elect’s big initiative.”

“Kain?”

“American Energy Independence Act. Announced it yesterday—artificial sun, American jobs, energy dominance.”

Ahdia processed this. Kain backing science felt wrong, like watching a cat suddenly advocate for dogs.

“Fusion is real science, right? Not a scam?”

“Very real. Same process that powers stars—hydrogen atoms fusing into helium, releasing enormous energy. A hundred million degrees, magnetic containment, controlled sun in a bottle.”

“That sounds . . .” She searched for the word. “Dangerous? Exciting? Both?”

“Both.” Ryu’s expression was complicated. “Fusion would be transformative. Clean energy, essentially unlimited. The kind of technology that advances civilization by orders of magnitude.”

“So why do you look worried?”

“Because the timeline is wrong. Scientists estimate three years minimum for safe prototype development. Kain promised ten months.”

“And CADENS has to help?”

“Government pressure. We’re required to collaborate.” He checked the treatment readout, watching her numbers climb. “You know about the Kardashev Scale?”

“The what?”

“Framework for measuring civilizational advancement. Type I civilization harnesses all energy available on their planet. Type II harnesses their star’s full output—Dyson spheres, stellar engineering. Type III harnesses an entire galaxy.”

“And we’re . . .”

“Type 0.72. Not even Type I yet.” He smiled slightly. “Cosmic toddlers, comparatively. Fusion would push us toward 0.8, maybe 0.9. Still far from planetary-scale, but significant progress.”

“So fusion is good.”

“The technology is good. The implementation . . .” He shook his head. “Kain’s rushing it for campaign promises. Safety protocols waived, environmental protections ignored. Good technology, dangerous approach.”

“Both at once.”

“Exactly.” Ryu checked the readout again. “Treatment complete. Your numbers are stabilizing at seventy percent.”

“That’s better than before?”

“Marginally. The compound is working.” He began packing his equipment. “Dr. Carter’s synthesis keeps improving. She’s dedicated significant research hours to optimizing your treatment.”

Ruth. Always Ruth. Keeping her alive even when Ahdia couldn’t be bothered to keep herself alive.

“Tell her thanks,” Ahdia said. “Next time you see her.”

“You could tell her yourself. She asks about you.”

The words hung in the air. Ahdia looked away, at the TV still playing on mute, at the city outside her windows, at anything except the concern in Ryu’s face.

“I know she does.”

“The team asks about you too. They’re worried.”

“I know.”

“Ahdia—”

“I know, okay?” Her voice came out sharper than intended. “I know they’re worried. I know I should call. I know I should show up, be present, be a teammate. I know all of it.”

Ryu waited.

“I just . . . can’t. Not yet.” She pulled her sleeve down, covering the injection site. “Every time I think about going out there, doing the hero thing, I see Geneva’s head exploding. I see the crowd turning into a mob. I see all the ways it goes wrong.”

“That’s trauma response. It’s normal after what you experienced.”

“Normal doesn’t make it easier.”

“No. It doesn’t.”

He finished packing, stood to leave. At the door, he paused.

“Same time next week?”

“Same time next week.”

“And Ahdia?” He turned back. “Consider answering when Ruth calls. She’s not checking on you as a doctor. She’s checking on you as a friend.”

The door clicked shut behind him.

Ahdia sat alone in her apartment, treatment coursing through her veins, numbers stabilized at seventy percent. Two percent better than when he’d arrived. Marginal improvement. Barely noticeable.

Her phone showed the missed calls. Ruth (3). Victor (2). Team group chat (47 unread messages). All of them reaching out. All of them getting silence.

She picked up the phone. Put it down. Picked it up again.

*Just text them. Tell them you’re okay.*

But she wasn’t okay. And telling them she was would be lying. And telling them the truth would mean explaining why she couldn’t leave this couch, why the treatment knocked her out for hours now, why she kept seeing Geneva’s head explode every time she closed her eyes.

The TV played on mute. News coverage she wasn’t watching. A city happening without her.

She pulled a blanket over herself. Didn’t answer the phone. Didn’t move from the couch.

The depression had been there before the powers, before the Hyper Seed, before any of this. It had just gotten worse. Or maybe the circumstances had caught up to it. Hard to tell the difference anymore.

Ruth texted: *Checking in. How are you feeling?*

Ahdia stared at the message for a long time.

*Tired*, she typed back. *Sorry for being MIA.*

*No apology needed. I’m here when you’re ready.*

She set down the phone. Closed her eyes. Let the treatment do its work while she did nothing at all.

This was her life now. Treatments and silence. Screens and solitude. The particular emptiness of being kept alive without quite knowing why.

Somewhere across the city, the team was doing hero things. Patrol routes. Community presence. The work she used to be part of.

She couldn’t remember why it had mattered.

Couldn’t remember why anything had.
