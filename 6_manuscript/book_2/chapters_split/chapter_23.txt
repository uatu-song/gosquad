# Chapter 23: Family Dinner
<!-- SOURCE: chapter_13.txt -->

Leah stood in the doorway with Victor, Thai food bags hanging from her wrists, taking in the place like she did every time. Three months she'd been crashing here and she still couldn't get used to it. Floor-to-ceiling windows. Kitchen bigger than her old apartment. Art on the walls that probably cost more than her car.

Ahdia's place. Ahdia's money. Ahdia's ghost.

"She been here?" Victor asked Ruth, who was already on the couch with her laptop.

Ruth shook her head. "Mail's piling up again. Three days at least."

"Traveling," Leah said, setting the bags on the kitchen island. "That's what her text said. Traveling."

"Traveling where?"

"Didn't say. Never does."

The penthouse was cold—Ahdia kept it that way, something about staying awake—and the cleaning service had been through recently. Everything spotless. Everything empty. Like a magazine spread nobody lived in.

Except Leah lived here now. Her stuff in the guest room. Her coffee mug in the cabinet. Her books on the nightstand: *The Dance of Anger*, *Radical Acceptance*, the workbook her therapist had recommended that she'd actually been doing.

She was a guest in a rich girl's apartment, and the rich girl was never home.

---

Ben came through the door with more food—pad see ew and spring rolls, because he always forgot someone's order and overcorrected. Tess showed up ten minutes later, apologizing about traffic, something about Leta expecting her home early.

"Work thing ran late," Tess said, and the lie sat there between them. They all knew what "work thing" meant. They all knew Leta didn't.

"Everyone's here," Ruth said. "Minus one."

Nobody said Ahdia's name. They didn't have to.

---

Victor started unpacking containers, and Leah fell into the rhythm of it—plates from the cabinet she now knew by heart, silverware from the drawer, napkins from the stack Ahdia had probably never touched. The motions of feeding people. The motions of making a place feel like home even when it wasn't.

"We should talk about the gear situation," Ruth said, laptop still open. "Medical supplies are low. Ben, can you do a run this week?"

"Ahdia said she'd handle expenses."

"She left a card," Leah said. "Told me to use it for team stuff."

Ruth's mouth did that thing it did when she was thinking something she wasn't saying. "That's generous."

"Benevolent dictator energy," Victor said, not looking up from the food. "Provides resources, doesn't collaborate."

"She's grieving," Tess said. "Her brother. Geneva. Give her time."

"It's been months."

"Grief doesn't have a timeline."

Leah watched them talk around it, the way they always did. Worried but not saying worried. Scared but not saying scared. Ahdia paid for everything—this penthouse, the gear, Leah's safe place to sleep when her apartment got too hot with harassers—and in exchange, she was never here.

Fair trade? Leah couldn't tell anymore.

---

Food happened the way food happens with people who've stopped being polite. Containers passed without asking. Forks stolen. Ben eating directly from the pad thai because nobody wanted to fight him for it.

Leah found herself wedged between Victor and Tess on the couch, plate balanced on her knee, watching the team settle into something that looked almost normal. Almost like a family.

"How's everyone actually doing?" Ruth asked. "Not mission status. Actually."

The question hung there. Leah felt it land in her chest—the weight of someone asking and meaning it.

"I'm still figuring out how to be useful," she started, but Tess cut her off.

"You're doing fine."

"Am I though?"

"You're here. You're trying. That counts."

---

"Remember when Ruth called 911 on herself?" Ben said, and the tension cracked.

Ruth groaned. "Why do you always bring that up?"

"Wait, what?" Leah hadn't heard this one.

"Month 2. First patrol." Ruth was already covering her face. "Trying to sound competent on comms. Hit the wrong button, broadcast 'Nightingale to all units' to police dispatch."

"You called 911 on yourself?"

"While coordinating a superhero team. Yes."

"My dad called her personally," Tess said, something complicated in her voice. "Asked if she needed psychiatric evaluation."

The laughter came easy after that. The kind of laughter that meant you'd seen each other fail and stuck around anyway.

"Ben keeps the recording," Ruth said. "Uses it when I'm 'too bossy.'"

"Leadership accountability," Ben said, not even trying to hide his grin.

---

"Your turn," Victor said, looking at Ben. "Fair's fair."

Ben leaned back, considering. "First week with powers. Thought I was hot shit."

"Oh no," Leah said.

"Practicing power jumps. Trying to land on a rooftop I'd scoped out." He shook his head. "Miscalculated. Overshot by thirty feet."

"Please tell me—"

"Landed in the middle of a rooftop yoga class. Twenty people mid-downward dog."

Victor was already laughing. "No."

"Crashed through their instructor's mat. Dominoes. Seven people down." Ben's face was completely straight, which made it worse. "Jump away in panic."

"But?" Ruth prompted.

"Left my gym bag. With CADENS ID badge."

"NO."

"Had to come back down the stairs like a normal person and claim I 'got lost.'"

Leah was crying. Actually crying, the good kind, the kind that came from laughing too hard. This. This was the thing. The embarrassments they gave each other, held without malice. The failures that became stories instead of shame.

This was what family meant when you didn't have one.

---

"Tess. Pigeon incident. Go."

"Absolutely not." But Ruth was giving her the look, and Tess caved. "Fine. Month 1. Big dramatic rooftop entrance. Flip off fire escape, land in crouch."

"Sounds cool so far," Leah said.

"Except I landed in fresh pigeon shit. Slipped. Full split. Groin pull."

"Oh my god."

"Had to be fireman-carried off the roof."

"By who?"

Tess muttered something.

"What?"

"Victor."

Victor, to his credit, kept his face neutral. "She apologized the entire way down."

"I was covered in pigeon shit!"

"How does Leta not know this story?" Ben asked.

Tess's face flickered. "Because I have dignity. And because—" She stopped. "She doesn't know a lot of things."

The room got quiet for a second. The weight of secrets sitting in the space between them.

---

Victor went next—the Spotify incident, Leah's playlist exposed to thirty community organizers, "Songs to Cry to While Researching Atrocities" in 40-point font.

"IT'S PRIVATE," Leah kept saying, but she was laughing too, mortified and alive with it.

"It was 'All Too Well (10 Minute Version)' and I stand by it."

"Someone asked if I needed a hug," Victor said. "I'm supposed to be the serious organizer."

This. Leah thought. This is how you get folded into a family. Your embarrassments become shared property.

---

"Anyone got an Ahdia story?" Ben asked. "Since she's not here to defend herself."

Ruth smiled, something sad in it. "Her first big translocation. She was supposed to land on a rooftop three blocks away."

"Supposed to?"

"Overshot. Landed in a fountain. Some corporate event on a rooftop patio."

"No."

"Soaking wet, purple lightning fading, fifty people in suits staring at her." Ruth shook her head. "Open bar. Catering. Someone giving a speech."

"What did she do?"

"Pretended she was the entertainment. Did a bow. Walked out like it was planned."

"That's the most Ahdia thing I've ever heard," Ben said.

The laughter was different this time. Warmer. Sadder. Two empty chairs at the table now—Ahdia and her brother both gone, one to whatever she was doing out there, one to the ground.

---

"Your turn." Everyone looking at Leah now.

"I'm—" She almost said "new" but that wasn't right. She'd been here since the beginning. She'd just felt new. "Fine. Last month, trying to fit in."

"We're listening."

"You all speak in code. Nicknames, references, inside jokes I don't get." She took a breath. "Someone says 'tank situation.' I assume military thing."

Ben was already wincing. "Oh no."

"I spend TEN MINUTES explaining tank specifications. Armor thickness, cannon types, urban warfare tactics."

"What were they actually talking about?" Ruth asked.

"Fish tank. For your apartment."

Ruth's face. "You explained anti-tank missiles when I asked about goldfish."

"I THOUGHT YOU WERE SPEAKING METAPHORICALLY."

"That's very white of you," Victor said, but he was smiling.

"I KNOW. I'm aware." Leah buried her face in her hands. "Did you at least get fish?"

"I researched koi for six hours."

---

The conversation shifted to Ahdia. Not the funny stories now—the pattern.

"She doesn't embarrass," Ruth said. "Just vanishes when things get awkward."

"Remember warehouse incident?" Ben said. "She just... froze time and left."

"That's the most Ahdia thing ever," Victor said. "'This is uncomfortable, bye forever.'"

"She bought this penthouse to avoid living with people," Leah said. Then, quieter: "And then offered it to me when I needed space. Ironic."

"She's generous from a distance," Ruth said. "Never up close."

They were all seeing it now. The pattern laid out in front of them.

"Should we be worried?" Tess asked.

"We are worried," Ruth said.

"But what do we do? She's never here to intervene on."

The conversation getting heavier. The mood shifting. The family dinner turning into something else.

---

Ruth noticed Leah's laptop on the coffee table. Her books spread out. The evidence of someone living here when the owner wouldn't.

"You basically live here now, huh?"

Leah felt herself tense. "Ahdia offered. My place was... not safe."

"Harassment?"

"After I got involved with organizing, stuff escalated." She didn't look at Victor. "Same tactics targeting other activists."

"Bot networks," Ben said. "Doxxing attempts. Coordinated campaigns."

"Yeah. Professional. Not random trolls."

Tess was quiet for a moment. Then: "Leta's getting it too. Worse than me."

Everyone's attention sharpened.

"Trans woman, Black, visible at protests." Tess's voice was flat. "They target her specifically."

"The compounded targeting," Victor said. "We've seen the pattern."

"She doesn't know I'm Go Squad. Can't tell her why I understand."

"That's hard," Ruth said. "Keeping that from someone you love."

"She thinks I work late. 'Freelance security consulting.'" Tess laughed, but there was nothing funny in it. "She trusts me. Which makes it worse."

"You're protecting her," Victor said. "Knowing puts her in more danger."

"Doesn't feel like protection. Feels like lying."

"Both can be true."

Leah watched the exchange. Tess carrying secrets from her girlfriend. Everyone carrying something.

Both can be true. Yeah. She was learning that.

---

"Victor's been helping me document the harassment," Leah said, changing the subject. "Evidence chain."

"She's a quick study," Victor said. "Methodical."

"We should coordinate our investigations," Ruth said. "Ben, you're working on—"

"Riot financing. And I'm seeing connections to the fusion project."

"And I'm seeing mining operations destroy communities I organize with," Victor added.

Tess was staring at her hands. "Everyone's investigating something except me. I'm just... processing."

"You're investigating your father," Ben said. "That counts."

"Isaiah Bennett." Tess's voice went hard. "He won't tell me anything about the case."

The room went quiet. The name sitting there. The dead man none of them could bring back.

"Tell them about the mining stuff," Leah said to Victor. Give Tess a break. Let the conversation move.

"Communities poisoned. Groundwater contaminated. Cancer clusters." Victor shook his head. "Company's rushing extraction. Rare earth minerals."

"Rare earths?" Ben leaned forward. "That's expensive. What are they building?"

---

"No idea," Victor said. "But the urgency is weird. Mining is usually slow."

"I've seen rare earth purchases in financial records I'm tracking." Ben was pulling up something on his phone. "From my riot investigation. Shell company buying reactor components."

"Reactor?" Victor's eyebrows went up. "Like nuclear?"

"Fusion. Kain's campaign promise."

Leah felt something cold move through her. Kain. The name that lived in her phone, in the audio file she hadn't played for anyone but Victor.

"The American Energy Independence thing?" she asked. "That's real?"

"Very real. Fusion facility in the Southwest. Rushed timeline, cutting corners."

"Cutting corners like... environmental protections waived?"

"Exactly. Permits fast-tracked, EPA pressured."

"That would explain the mining urgency," Victor said. "Need materials for the reactor."

Leah's brain was trying to make connections. Kain. Mining. Fusion. The harassment campaigns. The riots. She could almost see it—

"Who's financing the reactor?" Ruth asked.

"Shell company network. I've been mapping it." Ben looked up from his phone. "TRIOMF."

TRIOMF. Leah had seen that name somewhere. In her research, maybe? In the financial records she'd been pulling for the NDA investigation?

But before she could say anything, Tess's phone buzzed. Her face went dark.

"Shit. My dad's calling. Third time today."

"You still not talking to him?" Ruth asked.

"After he stonewalled me about Isaiah Bennett? No."

The moment passed. The name—TRIOMF—sitting there unexplored. Leah felt it slip away, replaced by the weight of Tess's father, Tess's investigation, Tess's pain.

"If you need CADENS access," Ben said, "I can try channels."

"Would you? I need to know what really happened. Who gave the orders."

"I'll see what I can find."

Leah stood. "More wine?"

The conversation moved on.

---

In the kitchen, opening bottles, Victor beside her.

"Thank you," she said quietly. "For the rides. The mentoring. Letting me crash your meetings."

"You're doing the work. I'm just connecting you to the right people."

"It's more than that." She kept her voice low. "You've been patient with me."

"Growth takes time."

"I still say the wrong thing constantly. Center myself. Miss the point."

"But you catch it faster now. Apologize quicker." He handed her the corkscrew. "That's progress."

"Is it enough?"

"Enough for what?"

"To be useful. To actually help."

Victor was quiet for a moment. "You're here. You're learning. You're staying uncomfortable. That's the work."

Leah's laptop pinged from the living room. She ignored it.

"You okay?" Victor asked.

"Fine. Just—Tess wrote me a decryption thing. For those files Ben couldn't crack from the mansion."

"The encrypted drive?"

"It's been running for three days. Keeps pinging when it finishes a batch." She pulled the cork. "Probably nothing. Financial records, corporate stuff."

Another ping.

"You should check it," Victor said.

"It can wait."

But she was already moving toward the living room, wine bottle in hand. Victor followed.

The laptop was on the coffee table, next to Ben's TRIOMF notes and Victor's mining research. The decryption progress bar: 100%. A folder had appeared on her desktop. No name. Just a date from three years ago.

She sat down. Opened it.

Spreadsheets. Flight logs. Scanned documents with legal headers.

"What is it?" Victor asked, standing behind her.

Leah didn't answer. She was looking at a list. Names in one column. Dates in another. Ages in a third.

Some of the ages were—

"Leah?"

"Don't." Her voice came out wrong. "Don't look."

She scrolled down. More names. More dates. Settlement amounts in the hundreds of thousands. NDAs. Confidentiality agreements. A folder labeled with a string of numbers that she knew, somehow, she should not open.

"Is that—"

"I need you to go back to the kitchen." She was still scrolling. "Please. Victor. Please go back to the kitchen."

He went.

Leah sat there for a long time, reading. When she finally closed the laptop, her hands were shaking.

They went back to the living room with wine.

---

Ruth was mid-story about a surgery rotation disaster—wrong chart, wrong patient, almost-catastrophe averted by a nurse with better attention to detail.

"How do you stay so calm under pressure?" Tess asked.

"Triage thinking. Assess, prioritize, accept losses."

"Military trains the same way," Ben said. "Save who you can, move on."

"That's dark," Victor said.

"It's realistic. Trying to save everyone means saving no one."

Leah thought about that. Triage. Prioritizing. Letting some things go to save others.

"Speaking of investigations—" Ben was looking at Leah. "You mentioned marketing firms earlier. Which ones?"

"Astroturf operations, mostly." She tried to remember. "One's called Synergy Solutions."

Something flickered in Ben's face. "Synergy... that sounds familiar. Is it a subsidiary?"

"Yeah, parent company is—" She reached for her phone. "Hold on, I have notes—"

But Tess was standing, abruptly. "I need air. Balcony?"

Ruth: "You okay?"

"Yeah, just... thinking about my dad. And Leta."

She was gone before anyone could respond. Ruth followed a few minutes later.

Leah watched them go, the conversation derailed. The name—Synergy Solutions—sitting there unexplored.

"She's struggling," Victor said quietly. "Keeping the secret from Leta. Investigating her father."

"If Ben can find information..." Ruth had left her sentence hanging.

"I'm trying," Ben said. "CADENS security is tight around the Bennett case."

"Bennett case?" Victor looked up.

"Isaiah Bennett. Environmental engineer killed by police months ago."

Leah knew the name. Everyone knew the name. "That's what the protests were about. Tess is investigating that?"

"Chief Whitford was involved. Her father." Victor's voice was careful. "She's trying to find out what he knew."

"Her father won't tell her?"

"Protecting someone. Or something."

Ben was looking at his notes. "Pattern I've seen before. Whistleblowers disappeared. Records scrubbed. Isaiah Bennett knew too much about... something."

"Your riot investigation?" Victor asked.

"Maybe. Or the fusion project."

The pieces were right there. Leah could almost see them—TRIOMF, Synergy Solutions, the mining, the fusion reactor, Isaiah Bennett. All connected. All pointing somewhere.

"Would Kain order a police killing of a whistleblower?" she asked.

"He's killed dozens through agent provocateurs," Ben said. "Direct police killing? Possible."

"If Bennett knew about environmental crimes. Safety violations."

"He'd be eliminated. Can't risk exposure during campaign."

"So Isaiah Bennett might have been trying to expose Kain?"

The question hung there. Ruth wasn't in the room to answer it.

---

Ruth found Tess leaning on the railing, city lights spread out below.

"Talk to me."

"Isaiah Bennett was innocent. I know he was."

"I know you do."

"Everyone does. That's why we protested." Tess's voice was flat. "But my father stonewalls. Won't give me anything."

"You're using his connection to investigate?"

"Trying. He's Chief of Police. He knows what happened." Tess turned to look at her. "And he won't tell me because he's complicit."

"That's not your fault."

"He's my father. I'm using his name, his position to investigate him." Tess laughed, hollow. "It feels like betrayal. Of him. Of the badge I used to respect."

"You're not betraying anything. You're choosing justice."

"And then I go home to Leta and pretend everything's fine." Tess's hands were gripping the railing. "She doesn't know I'm Go Squad. Doesn't know why I understand the harassment."

"You're protecting her."

"I'm lying to her."

"Both can be true. That's what makes it hard."

Silence. The city noise below. The weight of secrets neither of them could put down.

"Do you think I'm doing the right thing?" Tess asked. "Investigating my own father?"

"I think you're brave."

"Even if it destroys my family?"

"Your father destroyed it. Not you."

---

The party wound down the way these things do. Ruth's phone—hospital emergency, trauma patient, had to go. Ben with early morning surveillance. Tess needing to get back to Leta.

"Leah, you staying here?" Victor asked at the door.

"Yeah. Ahdia's place."

"Lock up. Be safe."

"I will. Thanks for everything."

One by one, they left.

Leah cleaned up alone. Takeout containers into the trash. Wine glasses into the sink. The motions of making a space livable when the person who owned it had abandoned it.

On the coffee table: papers everyone had left behind. Ben's financial documents with "TRIOMF" circled. Victor's mining notes—Titan Mining Corp, rare earth extraction. Her own research, still pulled up on her laptop.

TRIOMF. Synergy Solutions. Titan Mining. The fusion reactor. Isaiah Bennett.

The pieces were all here. She could see them.

But she was tired. It was late. She stacked the papers neatly, closed the laptop, headed for the guest room.

---

The guest room still felt borrowed. Three months, and she hadn't made it hers—just added layers of herself on top of Ahdia's expensive emptiness.

She opened the laptop again. Made herself look.

The settlement payments were routed through shell companies. She cross-referenced one against Ben's notes, still spread across the coffee table.

TRIOMF.

The same company funding Kain's campaign. The same company funding the riots. The same company paying hush money to—

She closed the laptop. Opened it again. Closed it.

One of the girls on the list would have been fourteen.

Leah sat in the dark for a long time. The penthouse silent around her. Ahdia's expensive emptiness. The team's scattered research. The puzzle pieces nobody had put together because nobody had looked at this drive, this folder, these names.

She thought about the women on that list. Girls, when it happened. Women now, scattered across the country, bound by NDAs and fear and money they probably needed to survive.

She thought about what it would take to find them. To ask them to trust a stranger. To build something that couldn't be buried, couldn't be dismissed, couldn't be spun.

She thought about Kain on television, shaking hands, smiling.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she'd figure out what to do.

Tonight she just sat there, laptop closed, unable to move, the weight of what she'd found pressing down on her chest like a hand.

---

She fell asleep surrounded by puzzle pieces she didn't know were connected.

On the coffee table in the living room, the papers sat in their neat stack. TRIOMF. Synergy Solutions. Titan Mining. Isaiah Bennett.

Somewhere in the city, Kain was campaigning. The fusion reactor was being built. The harassment networks were coordinating. The threads were converging.

But not tonight. Not yet.

Tonight was just a family dinner. Just people who loved each other sitting in a room, telling stories, not saying the things they were scared to say.

Tomorrow would be different.

It always was.
