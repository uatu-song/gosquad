## Scene 1: Team Debrief

The penthouse felt smaller in daylight.

Ahdia had spent the last eighteen hours on this couch. First watching Geneva Windrow die on live television, then not-sleeping through the night, then watching the sun come up through windows that felt too clean for a world this dirty. Now the team had gathered around her like she was furniture—which, honestly, fair. She'd been about as useful as furniture lately.

Ruth paced in front of the windows. Back and forth, six steps each way, the rhythm of someone who'd learned to move when she couldn't think. Her shirt still had the bloodstain from the debate stage—Geneva's blood, or maybe someone else's from the chaos after. Nobody had changed clothes. Nobody had showered. The coffee cups scattered around the room had been refilled three times but nobody was drinking anymore.

Ben stood by the kitchen counter, posture military-straight despite the exhaustion carved into his face. Tess sat cross-legged on the floor with her laptop balanced on her knees, fingers moving even when she wasn't typing. Victor and Leah shared the loveseat—his hand resting on her knee, her shoulder pressed against his arm. The kind of casual contact that said *we've been doing this for a while*.

The Hyper Seed in Ahdia's chest felt like a cold stone. Colder than usual. Like it was punishing her for yesterday's burns.

*Forty-seven minutes frozen. Sixteen people still dead.*

She'd learned something new about her powers: time manipulation doesn't fix bullets that have already landed.

"Alright." Ruth's voice cut through the fog—the triage voice, calm and clipped. "What do we know?"

Ben straightened. When he spoke, it was like he was briefing a general instead of a bunch of exhausted vigilantes who smelled like cold pizza and despair.

"Sniper nest on the parking structure. Roof access through a standard maintenance hatch—no forced entry, which means someone had codes or skills. Found a dust circle on the gravel where the tripod sat. Spent brass, 7.62 NATO." He pulled out his phone, projected something to the wall screen. "Eight hundred meters, moving target, headshot."

"Expert level," Tess said without looking up.

"Military or equivalent." Ben's jaw tightened. "But that's not the interesting part."

Ahdia watched him weighing what to say next. Ben was careful with information—doled it out in pieces, let you build the picture yourself. Probably an old habit from intelligence work. Or maybe he just didn't trust anyone to handle the whole truth at once.

"My ears were ringing when I got there," he said. "Ozone smell. Like standing next to a transformer during a lightning strike."

Ruth stopped pacing. "Translocation signature."

"That's my guess. Shooter was in position, took the shot, and then—" Ben made a gesture like smoke dispersing. "Gone. No exit route, no witnesses, no trace except the brass and the smell."

"Can we trace it?" Victor asked.

"Not without CADENS access logs. They track translocation signatures globally—authorization records, deployment timestamps, location pings. If someone used their tech, or something that mimics it, they'd have records." Ben's expression flattened. "But we're contractors, not family. We don't get Christmas cards, much less classified access."

"We're not going to CADENS with this." Ruth's tone left no room for negotiation. "Not yet. Not until we understand what we're actually dealing with."

Tess looked up from her laptop. Something had shifted in her face—the expression she got when she'd found a thread worth pulling.

"I found something too. The bot network that pushed the 'radical elements' narrative after Geneva went down?"

Victor leaned forward. "What about it?"

"It pivoted in under sixty seconds. Geneva gets shot at 7:23 PM. By 7:24, thousands of accounts are pushing identical messaging—'domestic terrorism,' 'radical elements,' 'this is what happens when you platform extremists.'" She turned her laptop so they could see the timestamps. "Coordinated. Simultaneous. Pre-loaded."

Silence. Leah's coffee cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"One minute?" she said.

"One minute to pivot thousands of accounts to a narrative about something that just happened." Tess's voice was flat with the kind of anger that came out cold. "That's not reactive spin. That's infrastructure. Someone built a machine designed to weaponize any crisis—sitting ready, waiting for something to exploit."

"So the bots and the shooter..." Victor started.

"Might not be the same operation," Tess finished. "The bot network was already running fear narratives before anyone died. 'Radical elements gathering.' 'Potential for violence.' Priming the audience. When Geneva went down, they just... pivoted the message."

"Which means serious money," Ruth said. "Building that kind of infrastructure."

"Yeah. But the funding trail dead-ends at shell companies. I need financial forensics I can't do from my laptop."

Ruth had stopped pacing. Ahdia could see her doing the math—resources, risks, who could be trusted with what. The same calculation she'd probably done a thousand times in the ER, deciding which patient got the surgeon first.

"So we have: professional sniper, translocation tech, and a pre-positioned narrative." Ruth's voice was steady but her hands weren't. "This wasn't random violence. This was an operation."

"And we can't trace any of it," Ben said, "without access we don't have."

"Then we investigate quietly. We use what we have. We don't tip anyone off that we're looking."

Ahdia sat through all of it. Watching. Listening. Her body felt like it belonged to someone else—heavy in ways she couldn't explain, wrong in ways she couldn't name. But her brain was doing something it hadn't done in months.

It was actually paying attention.

Ben needed CADENS access logs. Tess needed financial forensics capabilities. They were hitting walls that Ahdia couldn't freeze, couldn't punch through with time manipulation or purple lightning or any of the other cosmic bullshit that had colonized her life.

But Ryu was coming later. For treatment.

And Ryu worked for CADENS.

"Ahdia." Victor's voice, gentle. "You okay?"

She looked up. Everyone was watching her. She hadn't spoken in—how long? The whole briefing? She'd just been sitting here like a piece of furniture that occasionally breathed.

"Fine," she said. "Just tired."

"You should rest." Ruth's concern was real but practical—the kind that came with a checklist attached. "Ryu's coming later for your treatment."

"I know."

She didn't move. Didn't offer to help with the investigation. Didn't make a *Homeland* reference or a dark joke or any of the other deflections she usually deployed to fill uncomfortable silences.

Just sat there, heavy and wrong, while her brain kept turning over what she'd heard.

Ben needed translocation logs.
Tess needed financial access.
Ryu had both.

## Scene 2: Treatment

The doorbell was the first sound Ahdia had registered in hours.

She'd been watching CNN with the sound off. Talking heads discussing Geneva Windrow's "legacy"—which apparently meant arguing about whether she'd been too radical or not radical enough, too loud or not loud enough, too Black or not Black enough. Nobody on screen seemed to remember that she'd been a person twenty-four hours ago. A person who'd stayed three extra hours in a grieving mother's apartment because she couldn't make herself leave. A person who'd held hands and looked at model trains and meant every word she said.

Now she was a talking point. A symbol. A cautionary tale about women who speak too loud.

Ahdia pulled herself off the couch. Every joint ached with the deep-tissue exhaustion that came from burning frozen time. The Hyper Seed felt like ice lodged behind her sternum.

She opened the door.

Dr. Ryu Matsuda stood there with a medical case that probably cost more than her first car. He was younger than she'd expected when they first met—late twenties, maybe early thirties—with the kind of face that looked perpetually concerned. Like he'd emerged from the womb already worried about someone's cellular integrity.

"Ms. Bacchus."

"You can call me Ahdia. You've seen my cellular integrity numbers. We're past formalities."

He smiled—small and careful, like he wasn't sure if smiling was professionally appropriate. "Fair enough. How are you feeling?"

"Like I watched someone's head explode on live TV and then couldn't do anything about it."

Ryu paused in the doorway. Set down his case. Something in his expression shifted—not pity, exactly. Recognition.

"That's... a lot."

"It's fine. I'm fine. Everything's fine."

Neither of them believed it. She stepped aside to let him in.

Ryu surveyed the apartment as he set up—TV paused on a frozen news anchor, takeout containers stacked by the sink, the detritus of someone who hadn't left these rooms in days. He didn't comment. Just unpacked equipment with the practiced efficiency of someone who'd done this a hundred times.

"Where would you be most comfortable?"

Ahdia gestured at the couch. "This is basically my entire life, so."

She settled into the cushions. "Do I sit or lie down? I've only seen this in medical dramas. *Grey's Anatomy*. *House*. That one where the doctors are also ghosts."

"Sitting is fine." Ryu arranged instruments on the coffee table—portable synthesis device, scanner, vials that glowed in ways she'd learned not to ask about. "This will take approximately twenty minutes. Scan, then injection, then monitoring."

"More sci-fi than I expected."

"Dr. Carter's breakthrough required adapting isotope delivery systems. Quite elegant, actually."

Ahdia noticed: Ryu respected Ruth's work. That mattered.

He ran the scanner over her chest. The Hyper Seed registered as a cold spot on the thermal, surrounded by stress markers she couldn't read but knew weren't good.

"Baseline at 71 percent," Ryu said. "Down from 78 last week."

"The riot. The assassination. I burned a lot."

"You burned fifty minutes in three days."

"People were dying."

"I'm not criticizing." His voice was precise, careful. "Just noting. Each use costs lifespan. Therefore, each use requires justification."

"I helped investigate an assassination—"

"And burned 7 percent cellular integrity for information gathering that conventional analysis could have achieved."

The hit landed. He wasn't wrong. She'd frozen time at the crime scene to look at things she could have examined in normal time. Habit from training. The instinct to use the power because she had it.

"Your HUD functions in normal time," Ryu continued. "Thermal overlay, electromagnetic scanning, trajectory mapping—all without temporal manipulation."

"So I'm wasting my life. Cool. Thanks for the update."

"I'm trying to help you understand the economics." He prepared the injection—clear liquid in a syringe that caught light wrong. "Dr. Carter and I can extend your timeline. But you determine how much timeline we have to work with."

"So this is like... *Crank*? The Jason Statham movie?"

Ryu's hands paused. "I'm not familiar."

"Guy has to keep his adrenaline up or his heart stops. Except mine is 'use powers less or cells stop.'"

"That's somewhat reductive but not entirely inaccurate."

"Wait—you HAVE seen it?"

A slight smile crossed his face. "I reviewed your media consumption history. Contextual understanding aids communication."

Ahdia stared at him. "You studied how I talk?"

"I studied how you think. You process reality through narrative frameworks—television, films, genre conventions. It's not ineffective. Merely... indirect."

She couldn't decide if she should be touched or creeped out. Landed somewhere in the middle.

Ryu held up the syringe. "This will feel cold. Then warm. Then nothing."

The needle entered her arm. Cold spreading through her bloodstream—foreign, synthetic, not quite pain but not quite anything else. Then warm—her body recognizing, accepting, cellular repair activating. Then nothing.

But something fundamental had shifted. She could feel it in her chest—the Hyper Seed responding to whatever he'd injected. Like giving water to a plant that had been dying slowly.

"So that's keeping me alive?"

"That's extending your cellular viability by approximately 18 to 24 months."

"You're very careful not to call it 'keeping me alive.'"

"Language precision is important in medical contexts."

"But I AM dying. Just... slower now."

Ryu paused. Weighed his words with the care of someone defusing ordnance.

"Your human cellular structure is degrading from temporal stress. The treatment counteracts this degradation."

"That's a very fancy way of saying 'yes, dying.'"

"Degradation and death are not always synonymous."

Ahdia caught it. Weird phrasing. "What does THAT mean?"

"It means the treatment is working as designed." He resumed packing, not meeting her eyes.

Before she could press, he continued:

"I need to make you aware of something. CADENS monitors temporal distortions globally. When you use your powers, we register the signature."

"You're tracking me?"

"We're tracking temporal anomalies. Yours happen to be... distinctive."

Every time she used her powers, CADENS knew. The realization settled into her chest next to the cold of the Seed.

"This is not surveillance," Ryu said. "This is protection. If WE can track temporal signatures—"

"Others can too."

"A flare we can triangulate to within ten meters. If someone else has that technology, they know where you are and when you're most vulnerable."

The weight of it landed. She wasn't just dying faster when she used her powers. She was advertising her location to anyone with the right equipment.

"This is why we come to you," Ryu said. "House calls instead of facility summons. Protection."

Ahdia understood: the house call kept her location private from most CADENS staff. A small kindness. Or a leash disguised as kindness. She couldn't tell anymore.

"You're valuable, Ms. Bacchus. Please act accordingly."

He resumed packing. Monitoring complete. But Ahdia's brain wouldn't stop turning.

The cage was becoming visible now. Bars she hadn't noticed forming.

Freedom costs life. Using powers burns baseline.
Life costs freedom. Treatment keeps her dependent on CADENS.
She can't use powers without being tracked.
She can't survive without treatment.
She can't matter without burning the life she needs treatment to extend.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"CADENS tracks translocation signatures, right? Authorization records, deployment logs?"

Ryu's hands stilled on his equipment. Just for a second. Then he resumed packing.

"That's classified operational data."

"I'm not asking for state secrets. Ben found a translocation signature at the sniper nest. He can't trace it without access logs."

"And you want me to...?"

"I don't know." She heard her own voice—flat, tired, the sound of someone who'd stopped pretending. "I just want to help. The team's investigating and I'm sitting here getting juice boxes and naps while they do the actual work."

"Treatment isn't optional. Without it—"

"I know. Eleven days. You told me."

The number hung in the air. Eleven days without treatment. That's how long she had before her cells gave up entirely.

"Geneva was trying to build something." Ahdia's voice had gone quiet. "And someone erased her mid-sentence. While I stood there and watched."

"You couldn't have known—"

"I have powers that stop time and I couldn't save one person who actually mattered."

Ryu stopped packing. He stood there, medical case half-closed, looking at her with an expression she couldn't name. Concern. Guilt. Something else underneath.

"The team's hitting walls," Ahdia said. "Ben needs translocation access. Tess needs to trace the bot network funding. And I'm just... here. On this couch. Watching news I can't hear about a woman I couldn't save."

"You're recovering. That's not nothing."

"It feels like nothing."

Long pause. Ryu's fingers drummed against the case—three taps, then stop. She'd noticed he did that when processing something difficult.

"I could look into a few things." His voice was careful. "Off the record."

Ahdia looked at him. "Really?"

"I'm not promising anything. But if there's information that could help the investigation... you're not wrong that CADENS would have it."

"That would be... yeah. That would help."

"I'll see what I can find."

He finished packing. Paused at the door.

"Get some rest, Ahdia."

The door clicked shut behind him.

Ahdia sat alone in her penthouse, treatment coursing through her veins, keeping her human for another few weeks. The cage visible now—dependent on CADENS to survive, trackable whenever she used powers, isolated by depression, wasting the life she needed treatment to extend.

On the muted TV, a newscaster was saying something about a strongman in Eastern Europe. Some dictator who'd vanished without explanation. Unrelated. Background noise.

She didn't notice it yet.

But she would.

## Scene 3: The Kettle

The kettle wasn't boiling because Ruth hadn't turned it on.

She knew this. Had known it for—how long now? Three minutes. Five. The blue pilot light glowed but the flame wasn't lit, and her hands rested flat on the counter like they belonged to someone else.

Everyone had gone home. The penthouse was too quiet. She'd been lying awake for hours, staring at the ceiling, and finally thought: *just start. One drawer. You can do one drawer.*

Eight months of walking past his jacket on the chair. Eight months of not opening his side of the closet. His books still stacked on the nightstand—her nightstand now, just hers, except it wasn't because it still smelled like him when she let herself notice.

She'd opened his dresser. First drawer. Socks. Just socks. She could fold them, box them, donate them. Normal task. Manageable.

Then her hand hit the box.

Small. Velvet. Hidden under wool socks he'd never worn because Caledonia wasn't cold enough.

She'd known what it was before she opened it.

She'd opened it anyway.

Simple ring. Silver band. Small diamond. He'd known she didn't want anything flashy. He'd known her taste better than she did.

She'd closed it. Put it back. Closed the drawer. Walked out here. Put the kettle on.

Forgot to turn it on.

Had been standing here since.

"Hey."

Ahdia's voice, soft from the doorway. Ruth didn't startle—old ER habit, always know who's behind you—but she didn't turn around either.

"Can't sleep?"

"Treatment makes me jittery." A pause. Shuffling footsteps. "You?"

Ruth didn't answer. Just looked at the kettle that wasn't boiling.

Ahdia moved into the kitchen. Got herself water. Leaned against the counter a few feet away—present without crowding. For a while neither of them spoke. The pilot light flickered blue. The microwave clock said 2:17 AM.

Ruth reached out. Turned the dial. The flame caught, licking the bottom of the kettle.

"I tried to clear his things tonight."

She heard Ahdia go still behind her.

"First time." Ruth's voice came out flat, too controlled. "Eight months and I finally thought I could handle it."

Pause. Swallow.

"I found a ring. In his sock drawer."

Silence. Ruth could feel Ahdia processing.

"He was going to propose." The words tasted like ash. "I wasn't supposed to find it. He was waiting for the right moment. After things calmed down."

She watched the flame heat the water. Watched bubbles start to form at the bottom of the kettle.

"And then there wasn't one."

Her hands were shaking now, pressed flat against the counter like she could hold herself together through the granite.

"I put it back. Closed the drawer. Came out here." Her voice got smaller. "I can't do it. I thought I could and I can't."

"Ruth—"

"I was going to tell him I loved him." The words came faster now, eight months of holding them back. "I kept waiting for the right moment. Kept thinking there would be time. And then there wasn't any time and now there's a ring in his sock drawer that I was never supposed to find and I never said it, I never—"

Her voice cracked. Broke.

"He knew, right?" Almost begging. "He must have known."

"He knew." Ahdia's voice was quiet but certain.

"How? If I never said it?"

"Because you were you. With him."

Ruth turned around. Looked at Ahdia—really looked—and something in her chest finally gave way.

She was crying. First time since Firas died. First time since the singularity took him. First time since she'd started holding everything together because someone had to.

"His things are still everywhere." Sobbing now, couldn't stop. "His jacket on the chair. His books. I walk past them every day and I can't—I thought if I just started I could—"

The kettle started to whistle. Neither of them moved.

Ahdia stepped closer. Not touching. Just there. Present. Witnessing.

Ruth didn't know how long she cried. Long enough for the kettle to scream and then go quiet when the water boiled down. Long enough for the shaking to subside into something duller, heavier.

She turned off the burner. Wiped her eyes with the back of her hand.

"I'm sorry." Her voice was wrecked. "You didn't need to see that."

"Stop."

Ruth looked at her.

"You're allowed to fall apart." Ahdia's voice was awkward, uncertain, like she was reading from a script she'd only half-memorized. "And lead. Both things."

Ruth almost laughed. Wet, broken. "Both things."

Silence. But different now. Less like drowning.

"You don't have to clear it all tonight," Ahdia said.

"I know. I just thought if I started—"

"You started. You opened a drawer. That counts."

Ruth nodded. Barely.

"And you should actually make tea this time." Ahdia gestured at the empty kettle. "Before you boil it dry again."

Ruth filled the kettle. Turned it on. Hands still shaking, but moving. Ahdia stayed—didn't fill the silence with words, didn't try to fix anything. Just present.

The drawer in the bedroom was still closed. The ring was still there, hidden under wool socks he'd never wear.

But Ruth had told someone. After eight months, she'd said it out loud.

That was something. That was a drawer opened.

Tomorrow she would be the leader again. Hold everything together because someone had to.

Tonight she was a woman who'd found a ring and couldn't finish clearing a sock drawer. And someone had witnessed it. Someone who cared.

She didn't know yet what that trust would cost her. What Ahdia was already hiding, would keep hiding, would hide for months while Ruth defended her absences and made excuses to the team. She didn't know that the woman standing in her kitchen was lying about everything except this moment.

But that was later. That was a different kitchen, a different conversation, a different kind of breaking.

Tonight, there was just tea, and silence, and the small mercy of not being alone with a velvet box in a sock drawer.
