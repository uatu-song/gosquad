# Chapter 1: The Riot

## Scene 1: Downtown Caledonia - Peaceful Protest (Evening)

### Beat 1
**Rolls:** Environment (12), Ahdia State (14)

The sky on Feed 3 couldn't decide if it was evening or just giving up. Gray on gray, the kind of overcast that swallowed the sunset without announcing it.

Ahdia shifted in her chair, pulling her hoodie tighter around herself. The penthouse was cold—she kept it that way, easier to stay awake—and the glow from her wall of screens didn't help. Four drone feeds tiled across the main display: north approach, south approach, main plaza, crowd density overlay. Two thousand people, maybe more. Signs bobbing like a sea of cardboard: *Justice for Isaiah. Say His Name. Black Lives Matter.*

She flicked through the team's HUD feeds with a gesture.

Night Knight on the northwest rooftop, prone behind his scope. His feed showed the crowd from above, crosshairs drifting methodically across sections like he was reading a book. Old habits.

Gloom Girl and Battlea at street level, east side. Tess had her hood up, slouched against a wall like she was waiting for a bus. Leah beside her, arms crossed, trying too hard to look casual.

Nightingale was on the south rooftop, elevated position with full sightlines. Her feed showed the whole plaza laid out below—crowd, police line, side streets. The view of someone who needed to see everything to call the plays.

Crimson Sable's feed was the only one without a HUD overlay. Victor had insisted on civilian gear tonight—no suit, no mask, just a discreet earpiece. His camera angle came from his phone, half-pocketed, showing a cluster of young organizers he was talking to. Teaching them something, from the way they were nodding.

No one was breaking anything. No one was even shouting that loud.

"This is weird, right?" she said into comms. "We're just... watching people not commit crimes?"

### Beat 2

Nightingale's voice came through steady. "Protective presence, Auerbach. We're here if they need us."

"Unlike the fifty cops in riot gear who showed up to 'protect' them last time," Battlea muttered.

### Beat 4

She watched the feeds cycle through. Candles. Linked arms. A woman at the microphone talking about her son.

It looked like every protest montage she'd ever seen on cable news—the peaceful kind they always showed in retrospect, after something went wrong. *See how calm it was before?* Organized. Emotional. Zero molotov cocktails.

She waited for the other shoe to drop.

### Beat 5

"Clean so far." Night Knight's voice, clipped and professional. On his feed, the crosshairs panned across the crowd in slow sweeps. "Families, elderly folks. Not seeing agitators."

### Beats 6-8

"Sable, you good down there?" Night Knight asked, scope still sweeping.

"All quiet." Victor's voice was low, casual—blending in. "These kids know what they're doing. Better organized than half the nonprofits I've worked with."

"How are those boots holding up, Battlea?" Nightingale asked.

Battlea's sigh was audible. "I'm pretty sure CADENS sized these for someone with hooves."

"Mag-lock soles take adjustment. Give it another week."

"Easy for you to say. You got the medical sensors. I got clown shoes."

Gloom Girl's snort came through clear. "You literally asked for wall-climbing boots."

"I asked for *functional* wall-climbing boots. There's a difference."

### Beats 9-10

On the main plaza feed, someone started a call-and-response. *What do we want? Justice. When do we want it? Now.* The rhythm spread through the crowd like a wave. Fists raised. Candles flickering. A little girl on her father's shoulders, holding a sign she probably couldn't read yet.

This wasn't a riot waiting to happen. It was a community deciding to be seen.

Ahdia leaned back in her chair. Pulled her knees up. Watched.

First mission where nothing bad happens, she thought. Weird. But nice.

### Beat 11

6:47 PM.

"Nightingale, north perimeter." Night Knight's voice had changed. Flatter. "You seeing this?"

A pause. Then Ruth: "I see it."

Ahdia flicked to Feed 2. The police line on the north side—riot gear, batons, the whole theater of force. They'd been standing there for an hour. She didn't know what she was supposed to be seeing.

"What am I looking at?" she asked.

"Their hands," Nightingale said. "Watch their hands."

Ahdia watched. One cop adjusted his grip on his baton. Another shifted his weight forward. A third leaned toward the officer next to him, said something.

"The crowd hasn't changed," Night Knight said quietly. "They have."

### Beat 12

Ahdia glanced at the vitals overlay in the corner of her screen. Heart rates across the board were creeping up. Leah at 94—she was a derby jammer, usually sat in the low 60s. Tess at 89. Even Ben, prone and still behind his scope, was at 82.

"You guys all pre-game with espresso or something?" She meant it as a joke. "Ben, you're supposed to be the calm one."

"Maybe focus on the feeds, Auerbach." Night Knight's voice came back clipped. Not mean. Just... tight.

Ahdia blinked. Ben didn't snap. Ben was the steady one.

No one else said anything.

### Beat 13

She pulled back from the screens.

*Focus on the feeds.* Like she wasn't. Like she was just sitting here making jokes while they were out there actually doing something.

Firas would have been out there with them. Not watching from a bank of monitors in a cold penthouse, making dumb quips about espresso. He'd have been on a rooftop, or in the crowd with Victor, or—

She was the reason he wasn't. The reason he couldn't be.

The thought settled in her chest like it belonged there. *You're not a teammate. You're a replacement. A bad one.*

She shoved it down. Focused on the feeds.

### Beat 14

From somewhere in the murk of her own head, she almost missed it.

A man shoving someone on Feed 4. A woman yanking her kid back by the arm on Feed 1. Two teenagers squaring up where a banner used to be.

A minute ago these people were singing. What did she miss? Had she zoned out again?

"I'm seeing it too." Nightingale's voice, sharp now. "Something's moving through the crowd. Not physical—behavioral. It's spreading."

"From where?" Victor, low and urgent.

"I don't know. But we're not waiting to find out."

### Beats 15-17

"Nightingale, call it." Ben, done waiting.

"Battlea—north side, now. That police line's about to do something stupid."

Leah didn't need to be told twice. On her feed, Ahdia saw her plant her back foot, saw her mouth open—

The battle cry came through comms half a second before Ahdia felt the cold hit her chest.

She was already there. The Seed flaring, frost spreading through her ribs, and on Leah's feed the world went syrup-slow around her—just her, just that sliver of space—while FAERIS locked on and *pushed*.

Leah *moved*. Street level to the north barricade in the time it took a cop to blink. Sixty feet in maybe two seconds, if you were being generous with the math.

The cops didn't see her coming. That was the point.

Ahdia's fingers trembled on the console. The cold in her chest didn't fade—it settled. Made itself at home.

### Beat 18

"Knight—high ground, west side. I need overwatch on the crowd, not just the cops."

Ben was already in his crouch. That coiled, athletic stance that said *I'm about to do something physics doesn't approve of*.

She didn't wait for him to jump. Reached for the Seed before his feet left the rooftop—felt it answer, felt the cold sink deeper into her bones this time, settling in like it planned to stay.

On his feed, the air around Ben went thick and strange. FAERIS did the rest.

He launched off the northwest rooftop like gravity had personally offended him, arcing forty feet across the gap to the western tower. Landed in a roll, scope up before his knees touched concrete.

"Overwatch established." Cool as ever. Like he hadn't just jumped a canyon.

Ahdia flexed her fingers. They were slower to respond than they should have been.

### Beat 19

"Gloom Girl—rooftop across from Knight. I need a second angle on that police line."

Tess locked her gaze on the rooftop Ruth called. Then winked. Left eye.

The FAERIS drone pulsed once—reading her iris, calculating depth—and Tess was just *gone*. Reappeared on the rooftop half a second later, stumbling slightly on the landing.

"In position." A little breathless, but grinning. You could hear it in her voice.

"Still getting used to the new gesture," Ahdia said. "I miss the finger."

"Of course you do."

"Flipping the bird was a real statement. Now you're just coming on to the world."

"Very funny." Tess's voice had an edge. "Saying screw you is more your thing than mine, Auerbach."

### Beat 20

Silence on the line. Ahdia could see Tess's heart rate tick up another few points on the overlay.

"Auerbach, I didn't mean—" Tess started.

A sharp chirp cut her off. On Tess's feed, Ahdia watched her pull a handheld device from her utility belt—bulky, home-modded, covered in electrical tape and solder points.

"Hold on," Tess said. "I'm getting weird EM interference. The whole plaza."

Before Ahdia could ask, Battlea's voice cut through.

"Feed 4, northwest corner. Behind the dumpster."

Ahdia switched feeds. A figure in black bloc gear, face covered, crouched low. Hands busy with something.

"He's got a bottle," Battlea said.

"It's a protest," Ahdia said. "People bring water."

"He's stuffing a rag in it," Night Knight said.

"People bring... rags?"

"Lighter," Tess said.

"People bring—" The flame caught. "Shit."

"*Move,*" Nightingale said.

### Beat 21

The bottle arced high over the crowd—trailing fire, trailing screams—and shattered against the police line.

Glass and flame. Someone's shield. The cop stumbled back, and for half a second nobody moved.

Then the line charged.

### Beat 22

"They're not dispersing," Ben said. "They're *attacking*."

On Feed 2, riot shields slammed into the front row of protesters. Batons swinging. No warning, no bullhorn, no *this is an unlawful assembly*. Just violence, immediate and coordinated.

"That response time," Tess said. "They were waiting for an excuse."

"They didn't need one," Victor said, voice tight. "They just wanted one."

### Beat 23

Ahdia's fingers flew across the console, cycling feeds. Northwest—two more black bloc figures, moving through the chaos with that same wrong-footed grace. Not running *from* the violence. Moving *through* it. Like they knew where the gaps would be.

"I've got two more," she said. "Same gear, same movement pattern. They're not panicking."

"Because they're not protesters." Ben's crosshairs tracked one of them. "Professional agitators. I've seen this before."

"Great," Leah muttered. "So we're fighting cops *and* fake protesters. Anyone else want to join? Maybe some clowns?"

"Don't jinx it," Tess said.

### Beat 24

"Sable, get out of there." Ruth's voice cut through. "You're civilian cover, not combat."

"I've got people down," Victor said. On his feed, an elderly woman on the ground, a teenager trying to shield her. "I'm not leaving them."

"Victor—"

"Thirty seconds. I've done more with less."

"That's not the flex you think it is," Leah said.

### Beat 25

On Victor's feed, Ahdia watched him haul the woman up, pass her to the teenager, point them toward an alley. The kid nodded, pulled the woman with him, disappeared into the gap between buildings.

A cop broke from the line, baton raised. Victor saw him coming—shifted his weight, let the swing pass, caught the arm and redirected. Krav Maga. Economy of motion. The cop stumbled past and Victor was already moving, putting distance between himself and the line.

A second cop. Victor ducked the first swing, blocked the second, drove an elbow into the man's ribs. But a third was flanking—he didn't see the baton until it caught him across the shoulder. He rolled with it, came up in a crouch, but a fourth cop was already there. Boot to the chest. He went down hard, phone skittering across concrete. The feed spun—sky, pavement, boots.

"Sable's down!" Leah's voice.

The feed went dark.

### Beat 26

"Sable!" Ruth's voice, sharp. "Status!"

Nothing. The feed stayed dark.

Ahdia's hands were already moving, pulling up Victor's last known position, his vitals—heart rate 112, elevated but steady. He was alive. He was down. He wasn't responding.

"I've got his position," she said. "Northwest corner, behind the community center. He's mobile but his comms are out."

"Mobile how?" Ben asked. "Walking mobile or crawling mobile?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters for extraction."

### Beat 27

On Feed 1, the police line hit the crowd.

Not pushing—*hitting*. Batons swinging at heads, at arms raised in surrender, at people already falling. Tear gas blooming in thick white clouds. The screaming was everywhere now, coming through every feed, layered and raw.

"They're not trying to disperse them," Ben said, scope tracking. "They're trying to hurt them."

"Ben." Ruth's voice was tight. "I need solutions, not commentary."

"Working on it."

### Beat 28

Two more black bloc figures on Feed 3. Same coordinated movement, same wrong grace. One threw something—not a bottle this time. Bigger. Heavier.

The canister hit the ground and erupted in a wall of smoke so thick it swallowed the plaza's center whole.

"Military-grade," Ben said. "Someone's funding this."

"What, you think protestors are getting care packages from Lockheed Martin?" Tess's voice crackled through comms.

"I think someone wants this to look like a riot," Ben said. "And they brought receipts."

### Beat 29

The stampede started in earnest.

Two thousand people, maybe more, all trying to move at once. Ahdia watched the feeds fracture—bodies colliding, falling, trampling. An elderly man went down on Feed 2. A woman lost her grip on a child's hand on Feed 4.

The math was happening whether she wanted it to or not. How many would die in a crush this size? How many were already hurt? How many could she save if she—

The cold pulsed in her chest. Waiting.

### Beat 30

"Nightingale," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I can stop this."

A pause. Ruth knew what that meant. They all did now.

"Ahdia—"

"I know what it costs." She was already standing, already reaching for the Seed. "FAERIS, paint me for translocation. Center of the plaza."

The drone acknowledged. The cold spread through her ribs.

"Forty-seven seconds," she said. "That's all I need."

She didn't know if that was true. She said it anyway.

### Beat 31

The FAERIS drone locked on. She felt it—that strange pull behind her sternum, the world getting ready to fold.

"Ahdia, wait." Ruth's voice. "We don't know how bad—"

"I'm looking at the feeds, Ruth. I know exactly how bad."

### Beat 32

Feed 2: A child, maybe six, separated from her father. Frozen mid-scream, about to be trampled.

Feed 4: An elderly man on his knees, hands over his head, a cop's baton raised above him.

Feed 1: A pregnant woman pinned against a barricade, crowd crushing in.

"I see at least twelve critical," Ahdia said. "That's just what's on camera."

### Beat 33

"If you burn out in the middle of that crowd—" Ruth started.

"Then I burn out saving people instead of watching them die from a penthouse." She pulled the HUD headset tighter. "I've done the math. I don't like it either."

### Beat 34

"Battlea, Knight—cover her arrival point. Gloom Girl, extract Sable the second she freezes."

Ruth was already pivoting. Already planning around Ahdia's choice instead of fighting it.

"Copy," Ben said.

"On it," Leah said.

Tess didn't answer. She was already winking, already gone.

### Beat 35

The translocation hit like a cold wave breaking over her skull.

One second: penthouse, screens, the safe distance of watching.

Next second: plaza center, smoke and screaming, the crowd a living thing pressing in from all sides.

### Beat 36

The smell hit first. Tear gas, sweat, blood, fear—fear had a smell, sharp and animal. Then the sound: not screaming anymore, just a roar, thousands of voices compressed into white noise.

She couldn't see the feeds anymore. She was *in* the feed now.

### Beat 37

A man slammed into her shoulder, didn't even look, kept running. Someone else's elbow caught her ribs. The crowd wasn't a collection of people—it was a current, and she was drowning in it.

*Focus.*

The cold in her chest. The Seed. The only thing that could stop this.

### Beat 38

She saw the child from Feed 2. Real now, not pixels. A little girl in a yellow dress, fallen, curled into a ball as feet churned around her.

Three seconds until someone stepped on her. Maybe less.

### Beat 39

Ahdia reached for the cold. Let it spread. Felt it climb through her ribs, her spine, her skull.

*Forty-seven seconds,* she'd said.

The world began to slow.

### Beat 40

Time stopped.

The roar cut off like someone had hit mute. The bodies around her froze mid-stride, mid-fall, mid-scream. Tear gas hung in the air like fog that forgot how to move. A baton suspended six inches from a protester's skull.

Ahdia stood alone in the silence.

She had work to do.

## Scene 2: Frozen Time - Crowd Control

The little girl in the yellow dress was still falling.

Ahdia walked toward her, each step crunching on gravel that didn't move. The girl's face was frozen in that specific terror kids get when they know an adult can't save them—mouth open, eyes squeezed shut, waiting for impact.

"Okay," Ahdia said to no one. "You first."

She crouched, slid her arms under the girl's suspended body, and lifted. Lighter than she expected. The girl didn't weigh anything in frozen time, or maybe Ahdia's sense of weight was as broken as everything else right now. She carried her ten feet to the left, away from the stampede's path, set her down in a crouch that would resolve into a stumble instead of a fall.

One.

The cold in her chest pulsed. Not painful yet. Just present.

She turned back to the crowd. Two thousand people, frozen mid-panic. Tear gas hanging in white curtains. A cop's baton six inches from a skull. A woman's hand reaching for a child she'd already lost.

"This is fine," she muttered. "This is totally the Quicksilver scene from X-Men, except I'm bleeding and probably dying. Cool. Cool cool cool."

Her HUD flickered in the corner of her vision. *TEMPORAL SUSPENSION ACTIVE. CELLULAR INTEGRITY: 89%.*

She started walking.

---

The elderly man on his knees took thirty seconds. She repositioned him behind a concrete planter, out of the crush zone. The woman reaching for her child took another twenty—she reunited them, posed the mother's arms around the boy's shoulders so when time resumed they'd be holding each other.

Three. Four.

A teenager frozen mid-fall, phone still clutched in his hand, about to hit concrete face-first. She caught him, turned him, set him down in a controlled sprawl that would bruise instead of break.

Five.

*CELLULAR INTEGRITY: 86%.*

Blood dripped from her nose. She watched it fall in real-time—the only thing moving besides her—and hit the frozen ground without a splash.

She kept working.

---

Minute eight.

A cop was about to bring his baton down on a protester's skull. The protester—a man in his sixties, gray beard, "Justice for Isaiah" sign fallen at his feet—had his arms raised in surrender. The cop's face was twisted into something that didn't look like fear or anger. It looked like nothing. Just a job being done.

Ahdia stood between them, studying the angle.

If she moved the protester, the baton would hit air. The cop would stumble, maybe fall. The protester would live.

But that felt like choosing sides.

Ruth's voice in her memory: *Protect civilians. All of them.*

She adjusted the protester's position—two feet to the left, arms still raised, so the baton would catch his shoulder instead of his skull. Painful. Not fatal. Then she tilted the cop's trajectory, just slightly, so the follow-through would put him off-balance.

Neither of them would know what happened. They'd both walk away.

Both/and.

*CELLULAR INTEGRITY: 81%.*

Six. Seven.

---

Minute fifteen.

A brick hung in the air, trailing fragments of mortar like a comet's tail. Its trajectory would end at a cop's face—young guy, couldn't be more than twenty-five, frozen mid-flinch because he'd seen it coming and couldn't move fast enough.

Ahdia reached up and nudged the brick. Just a few degrees. It would hit the ground now instead of his cheekbone.

She didn't know if he was a good cop or a bad one. She didn't know if he'd been in the line that charged without warning, or if he'd been trying to de-escalate. She didn't know anything about him except that he was twenty-five and about to lose an eye.

"You're welcome," she said. "Or not. I don't know. Just—don't be an asshole when you wake up."

Eight.

*CELLULAR INTEGRITY: 74%.*

The nosebleed was worse now. She could taste copper in the back of her throat.

---

Minute twenty-three.

Her legs were starting to shake.

The HUD had stopped giving her percentages. It just flashed red now: *CRITICAL. CRITICAL. CRITICAL.*

She'd saved fifteen people. Repositioned another eight who were about to be trampled. Redirected four projectiles—two bricks, a bottle, something that might have been a rock or might have been a phone, she couldn't tell and didn't care.

Twenty-three.

The crowd still stretched in every direction. Frozen faces. Frozen screams. Frozen violence.

She couldn't save them all. She'd known that going in. But knowing it and *feeling* it were different things.

"Ryu would tell me to stop," she said. Her voice sounded strange. Wet. "Ruth would tell me to stop. Everyone would tell me to stop."

She kept walking.

---

Minute thirty-one.

She was in the northwest corner now. The smoke was thickest here, tear gas frozen into a solid wall of white that she had to push through like curtains. Her eyes burned even though the gas wasn't moving. Some things didn't care about physics.

AR-Ryu flickered into existence beside her, his lab coat too clean for a riot, his expression calibrated to "concerned but professional." He wasn't really there. He was never really there. Just her HUD projecting what it thought she needed.

"Cellular integrity at forty percent," he said, falling into step with her. "Ahdia, you need to stop."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because it's true. You're approaching irreversible damage thresholds."

She waved her hand through his chest and kept walking. He flickered, reset, followed.

"Recommend immediate suspension cessation."

"Yeah," she said. "I heard you the first time."

A figure ahead. Black hoodie, jeans, sneakers—not a protester in the traditional sense, no sign, no linked arms. Just a kid who'd gotten caught in the wrong place.

She got closer.

Korede.

Leta's little brother. Seventeen, maybe eighteen. She'd seen him in the feeds earlier, filming the speeches, looking proud of his sister. Now he was frozen mid-sprint, heading directly into a tear gas canister that would detonate six inches from his face the moment time resumed.

"Oh, you absolute *idiot*," she breathed. "Why are you running *toward* the gas?"

She grabbed him. Her arms felt like they were made of wet paper. The cold in her chest had spread to her spine, her skull, the backs of her eyes.

*CELLULAR INTEGRITY: 31%.*

She dragged him backward. Ten feet. Fifteen. Far enough that the canister would miss him, the gas would disperse before reaching him, he'd be scared but not blinded.

Twenty-four.

But he was the one who mattered. He was the one she'd recognized.

"Leta would kill me if I let you die," she said to his frozen face. "And then Tess would kill me again, somehow. And Ruth would figure out a way to kill me a third time. So you're welcome."

She let go. Her hands were shaking so badly she could barely feel them.

*CELLULAR INTEGRITY: 28%.*

---

Minute forty-seven.

She couldn't stand anymore.

The world was blurring at the edges—not the frozen world, that was still sharp, still terrible in its clarity. Her vision. Her eyes were failing. Blood ran freely from her nose, her ears, and when she touched her face her fingers came away red from her tear ducts too.

She was crying blood. That seemed bad. That seemed like the kind of thing a person should be concerned about.

AR-Ryu was still there, flickering at the edge of her vision. His mouth moved but the words came delayed, broken: "Cellular integrity... critical... immediate cessation..."

"Shut up," she whispered. "I know. I *know*."

Twenty-four people. She'd saved twenty-four people who would have died in the next thirty seconds of real time. Protesters and cops and kids and old men and a woman who'd been reaching for her son.

It wasn't enough. It was never going to be enough.

But it was what she had.

She let go.

---

The world exploded back into motion.

Sound hit her first—the roar of the crowd, the screaming, the tear gas canisters popping, all of it slamming into her ears like a physical force. Then the movement, bodies surging around her, the chaos she'd been walking through suddenly alive and violent and *loud*.

She tried to take a step. Her legs buckled.

The ground came up fast. She had time to think *this is going to hurt* before her cheek hit concrete and everything went sideways.

"AHDIA DOWN!" Ruth's voice, somewhere far away. "Northwest corner! Someone get to—"

The rest of it faded into static.

The last thing she saw, before the darkness took her, was a little girl in a yellow dress. Running. Alive.

Worth it, she thought.

And then nothing.

## Scene 3: Aftermath — CADENS Medical

The ceiling was wrong.

Too white. Too still. No smoke, no frozen faces, no purple tint. Just acoustic tiles and recessed lighting and that specific beeping rhythm that said *you're alive but we're monitoring the situation*.

Her mouth tasted like copper and something chemical.

"...Ruth?"

"Hey. You're at CADENS." Ruth's fingers were on her wrist—taking her pulse, not holding her hand. Doctor first. "Pupil response is good. Can you tell me what day it is?"

"The day I watched a riot from my penthouse and then decided to teleport into it like an idiot?"

"Close enough." Ruth's mouth twitched. "You've been out about four hours. How's your vision? Any blurring at the edges?"

Four hours. CADENS. Medical. Okay. Okay okay okay.

The riot. The freeze. The little girl in yellow. Korede running toward tear gas like he was trying out for a Darwin Award—

"The crowd." She tried to sit up. Her arms didn't cooperate. "How many—"

Ruth's hand pressed her shoulder back down. Firm. Not negotiable.

"Don't move yet. Your cellular integrity dropped significantly—we're still running numbers." A pause. "Ryu's working on it."

Ryu was at a console across the room. His fingers were drumming on the edge of the keyboard—that rhythmic pattern he did when he was deep in data, processing something he didn't like. He hadn't turned around.

"Ryu?"

The drumming stopped. Started again. "Your baseline dropped to sixty-seven percent. That's—sorry, let me just—" He pulled up another screen, scrolled through something. "Twenty-three points in under an hour, which is, I mean, the cellular degradation curve is—" He caught himself, shook his head. "Sorry. Ruth's calculating revised treatment intervals. The short version is: not great."

That wasn't what she'd asked. She'd asked *Ryu?*—which, okay, wasn't technically a question, but the subtext was pretty clear.

Movement in the doorway. Director Bourn stepped into the room, tablet in hand, already reading something on the screen.

"Ms. Bacchus. Good to see you conscious." She didn't look up from the tablet. "We're going to need a full debrief on the agitators you observed. Their movement patterns suggest professional coordination—I'd like your assessment when you're stable."

Not *calculating asset value*. Just... bureaucratic. Efficient. The voice of someone who managed cosmic threats like quarterly reports.

"Looking forward to it," Ahdia said, not meaning it even a little.

Ruth still hadn't answered the question. How many.

"Ruth. How many didn't make it?"

Ruth's hand found hers now—the pulse-check shifting into something warmer. "One. A man near the south barricade. Trampled before you reached that section."

One.

One person died while she was freezing time for forty-seven minutes.

"Who?"

"Daniel Farah. Thirty-one. Accountant. He was there with his girlfriend—she's in surgery, but she'll make it."

Daniel Farah. Thirty-one. Accountant.

She tried to picture him. Couldn't. He was a name and two facts now. An age and a job title. His girlfriend was in surgery and would wake up alone.

Ryu still hadn't turned around. The finger-drumming had stopped entirely, which was somehow worse.

"Ryu. You're being weird."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm reviewing your cellular data, which requires—" Another stop. His hand came up to adjust his glasses, the slightly crooked ones he never quite fixed. "I got the upload."

There it was.

AR-Ryu had been with her the whole time—walking beside her through frozen chaos, telling her to stop, watching her wave her hand through his chest and keep going. Which meant Real-Ryu had received forty-seven minutes of—

Oh.

Oh, *shit*.

"You got the upload."

Ryu finally turned. His eyes were red-rimmed. Too bright.

"I received the full temporal suspension record, yes. All forty-seven minutes of it." The words started coming faster. "You were at eighty-nine percent when you started, and by minute thirty-one you were bleeding from your eyes—from your *eyes*, Ahdia—and your hands were shaking so badly you could barely lift that kid, Korede, and I watched you drag him to safety when you could barely stand, and I told you to stop, I told you *fourteen times*, I counted, and you just—" He made a gesture like he was waving through something. Through someone. "You kept walking. Like I wasn't there. Like what I was saying didn't—" He stopped. Took a breath. "Sorry. I'm doing it again."

Because he *wasn't* there. Because he was a projection. Because she'd known that if she stopped, Daniel Farah wouldn't be the only name on the list.

But he didn't remember it as a projection. He remembered it as *him*. Forty-seven minutes of watching her kill herself in slow motion, unable to touch anything, unable to stop her.

Ruth stood. Moved to the IV cart. Picked up the autoinjector—sleek, medical-grade, the click-hiss kind.

"We need to start the treatment now. Your cells are destabilizing faster than projected."

She positioned it against Ahdia's arm. Thumb on the trigger.

*Click.*

The sound found her before she could brace for it.

*Hiss.*

Red light. Blue light. Her hand passing through the injector because she wasn't solid anymore. Firas's fingers on her wrist—the parts of her that were still *her*. The reversal. The flood. His lips moving over the roar: *Oh hey. I knew you were in there somewhere.*

Then Ruth's voice, and the ceiling, and the beeping.

She was gripping Ruth's wrist hard enough to leave marks. Her eyes were wet.

"Ahdia—"

"Sorry." Her voice cracked. "Sorry, I just—"

She let go. Saw the white pressure marks on Ruth's skin. Added *bruised my best friend* to the list of things she'd done today.

Ruth didn't pull away. Didn't flinch. Just watched her with that steady diagnostic gaze—reading her symptoms, cataloging her reactions, caring about her anyway.

"It's the sound," Ruth said quietly. Not a question. "The autoinjector."

Ahdia nodded. Couldn't speak.

Ryu had gone very still. "I didn't know," he said. "About Firas. What he did. The upload, it—I saw it. The memory."

He'd seen it. The one she'd been trying not to touch. Her brother's lips moving over the roar.

Firas was twenty-three. She was supposed to protect him. That was the deal—you're the older sibling, you protect them from bullies and bad decisions and singularities that want to eat them. She'd failed the only job that actually mattered.

"Now you do," she said. Barely audible.

Bourn's voice cut through from the doorway. "Dr. Ryu. Treatment status?"

Ryu blinked. Turned back to his monitors, fingers already drumming on the keyboard edge again.

"Cellular integrity stabilizing at sixty-eight percent. She'll need daily treatments for the next week instead of the usual three-day interval. I can have a revised protocol ready by—" He checked something. "Tomorrow morning. 0800."

"Noted." Bourn was already leaving. "Ms. Bacchus, Briefing Room C when you're mobile. The sooner the better—those movement patterns aren't going to analyze themselves."

The door hissed shut behind her.

Daniel Farah. Thirty-one. Accountant.

Firas Bacchus. Twenty-three. Her little brother.

"Ahdia." Ruth's voice. "Look at me."

She looked.

"Twenty-three people are alive because of you. A little girl in a yellow dress went home to her family. Korede went home to Leta. You did that."

"And Daniel Farah didn't go home to anyone."

His girlfriend was in surgery. She'd wake up and find out she came to a protest with her boyfriend and left alone.

Ruth held her gaze. Didn't flinch.

"No. He didn't. And that's not your fault."

*Isn't it?*

She didn't say it out loud. She didn't have to.

## Scene 3.5: County Lockup — Morning After

Leah had been sitting in the release area for three hours.

The plastic chair was designed by someone who hated human bodies. The fluorescent lights hummed at a frequency that made her teeth itch. A woman two seats down was crying quietly into her phone, and Leah was trying very hard not to eavesdrop while also memorizing every word because what else was she supposed to do with her brain at 7 AM in county lockup?

*He's fine. He's going to be fine. They would have called if he wasn't fine.*

Except they hadn't called. They hadn't called because Victor's phone was evidence, his earpiece was confiscated, and Leah had spent the last twelve hours working the system—calls to lawyers, calls to bail bondsmen, calls to Marcus from the ACLU who came into her coffee shop every morning for a cortado and always tipped five dollars on a four-dollar drink.

The system was designed to make you wait. To make you feel small. To remind you that your person was in a cage somewhere behind those doors and there wasn't a damn thing you could do about it except sit in a plastic chair and wait.

She hated waiting.

The door buzzed. Leah was on her feet before it finished opening.

Victor came through slowly. Moving like someone had rearranged his internal organs and he was still figuring out the new layout. His left arm was in a sling—improvised, looked like torn shirt fabric. His face was wrong: swollen around one eye, a cut on his temple that had been cleaned but not properly treated, and that specific pallor of someone who'd spent the night on concrete.

"Hey." His voice came out rough. Sandpaper on gravel.

"Hey yourself." She wanted to run to him. Wanted to grab him and check every inch for damage. Instead she walked—controlled, steady, the way you approach someone who might flinch.

He didn't flinch. He leaned into her when she got close, just slightly, his weight shifting toward her like gravity had opinions.

"Shoulder?" she asked.

"Dislocated. They popped it back." A pause. "Eventually."

*Eventually.* She filed that away in the part of her brain that was keeping a list. The list was getting long.

"Can you walk?"

"Been walking all night. Standing's the hard part."

She got her arm under his—the good side—and took some of his weight. He let her. That scared her more than the bruises. Victor didn't let people carry him.

They made it to the parking lot. Her car was in the far corner because of course it was, because nothing about this night was designed to be easy. The morning sun was too bright, too cheerful, completely wrong for the occasion.

"The others?" Victor asked. First words since they'd started walking.

"Ahdia's at CADENS. Medical. She burned hard." Leah kept her voice neutral, reporting. "Ruth's with her. Ben's doing Ben things—running down the provocateurs."

"Tess?"

"Tracing the bot networks. She's convinced someone orchestrated the whole thing."

Victor made a sound. Not quite a laugh. "They did. Professional job. I've seen it before—agent provocateurs, coordinated escalation. Someone wanted that protest to turn violent."

"And the cops?"

"The cops did what cops do." His voice went flat. "Some of them were waiting for it. You could see it in their eyes—finally, permission. The others just... followed orders."

They reached the car. Leah helped him into the passenger seat, watched him wince when his shoulder moved wrong.

"Hospital," she said. "Now."

"Not yet."

"Victor—"

"I need to see something first."

She knew where he meant before he said it. The same place she'd been avoiding since Firas disappeared. The place that held both their histories in its walls.

"The academy."

He nodded. Just once.

She started the engine.

---

The Bacchus Academy of Movement Arts looked wrong in daylight.

Leah had only ever been here at night—Tuesday and Thursday evenings, 7 to 9, Firas's self-defense classes in the back studio while the parkour kids ran the main floor. The building was supposed to be alive with bodies in motion, the thump of landing mats, Firas's voice calling corrections across the space.

Now it was just a building. Dark windows. Chain on the door. A handwritten sign that said CLOSED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE in Ruth's careful handwriting.

She parked across the street. Neither of them moved to get out.

"When's the last time you were here?" Victor asked.

"The night before." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "The night before the singularity. Thursday class. He was teaching knife defense."

Victor was quiet for a moment. "He used to let me use the community room for organizing meetings. Free of charge. Said it was good for the neighborhood to see people gathering for something other than exercise."

"That sounds like him."

"He asked me once why I kept doing it. The organizing. After all the setbacks."

Leah looked at him. He was staring at the building, not at her.

"What did you tell him?"

"That the neighborhood would be pissed if I stopped." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "He laughed. Said that was the right answer."

They sat in silence. The morning sun crept higher, turning the academy's windows into mirrors.

"I never told him why I came," Leah said. The words surprised her—she hadn't planned to say them. "To his classes, I mean. He asked once. Why self-defense specifically."

"What did you say?"

"City safety." She shrugged, the same shrug she'd given Firas two years ago. "You know how it is."

Victor didn't push. Didn't ask what "city safety" meant, didn't press for details she wasn't offering. Just like Firas hadn't.

"He never asked twice," she said quietly. "I always wondered if he knew anyway."

"Firas knew a lot of things he didn't say out loud."

"Yeah."

More silence. A jogger passed on the sidewalk, glanced at them, kept moving.

"I've seen what happens when communities don't have spaces like this," Victor said. "Five years doing this work, you see the same playbook over and over. Provocateurs, escalation, chaos." He touched his injured shoulder absently. "People trying to help each other, getting caught in the crush."

Leah didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say.

"That's why the work matters. Why places like this matter." His voice was quiet but firm. "Anger keeps the work going. But you need somewhere to channel it."

Leah looked at the shuttered academy. At Firas's name still on the sign, fading in the sun.

"Someone should do something with this place," she said.

Victor turned to look at her.

"He'd hate seeing it dark," she continued. "All those mats just sitting there. The community room empty."

"You thinking what I'm thinking?"

"I don't know what I'm thinking." She kept her eyes on the building. "I just know he saw something in both of us. And now he's gone and the place where he saw it is just... sitting here."

Victor's hand found hers on the gear shift. Squeezed.

"Not yet," he said. "But maybe. Someday."

"Someday."

They sat there a while longer. Two people who'd lost something they couldn't name, looking at a building that held the shape of who they used to be.

"Okay," Leah said finally. "Hospital now."

"Hospital now."

She pulled away from the curb. In the rearview mirror, the academy got smaller, then disappeared around a corner.

But she'd remember where it was. They both would.

---

The hospital took four hours, two X-rays, and one extremely judgmental nurse who clearly knew a protest injury when she saw one.

Victor's shoulder was stable but needed rest. His ribs were bruised, not broken. The cut on his temple got proper stitches this time.

"The memorial's in three days," Leah said as they walked back to the car. "Geneva's speaking. Kain too."

"I know."

"You going to be functional by then?"

"I'll be functional." He looked at her—really looked, for the first time since the release door. "Leah. I'm okay."

"You're not okay. You're held together with stubbornness and scar tissue." She kept her eyes on the road. "But yeah. You'll be functional. You always are."

They drove in silence for a while. The city fully awake now—coffee shops crowded, commuters in full rush, everything pretending last night hadn't happened.

"The cops who did this," Leah said finally. "The ones who beat you."

"What about them?"

"I want their names."

Victor's hand found hers again. Squeezed.

"Later," he said. "Right now—rest. Then we figure out who wanted this riot and why."

She squeezed back.

Later worked. For now.

---

## Scene 4: The Penthouse

There were people in her apartment.

She'd invited them. This was her idea. "Use the penthouse," she'd said, like a person who has people over. Like someone who does that regularly and doesn't find it deeply weird to have other humans breathing the same air as her.

Leah had her boots on the coffee table. Victor was leaning against the wall where Ahdia usually stood when she was dissociating at 3 AM. Ruth was in *her* spot on the couch—the spot with the perfect view of all four screens, the spot where the cushion had molded to the exact shape of Ahdia's depression.

This was what she wanted. Teammates. A team. People who showed up.

It was just... a lot. A lot of people. In her space. Existing near her.

She'd get used to it. Probably.

One of the screens was tuned to the news—she'd left it there, a habit, background noise that usually didn't require attention. But Kain's face kept pulling focus. Press conference. The riot still smoking in the B-roll behind him, all that chaos packaged into a campaign moment.

"—what we saw tonight was the failure of leadership to protect our citizens. The violence, the chaos—this is what happens when we let radical elements dictate the terms of public discourse—"

"I swear," Tess said, "I thought we murdered that guy."

Nobody laughed. Nobody disagreed. The words just sat there, heavy and true.

"He's campaigning." Ben's voice was flat. "He's literally campaigning on a riot he orchestrated."

"Can we mute that?" Ruth asked.

Ahdia turned it down but didn't kill it entirely. Kept Kain in her peripheral vision. Know your enemy.

Ben was pulling up something on his tablet, swiping through images. "The provocateurs weren't activists. Look at this—same boots on four different guys. Tactical footwear, not protest gear. They moved in formation. Entry points, dispersal patterns, timing on the gas—this was coordinated."

"So someone paid professionals to start a riot at a memorial for a dead kid." Tess's voice had that edge it got when she was too angry to sound angry.

"Someone with resources. Training protocols." Ben looked up. "And access to police response schedules."

Victor was quiet for a moment. He'd been quiet since they arrived—not his usual presence, the calm that anchored a room. Something smaller tonight.

"Isaiah deserved better than this," he said.

Nobody argued with that either.

Ruth leaned forward. "This isn't crime. This is political operation."

"So what, we're fighting... what?" Leah pulled her boots off the coffee table, finally. "A campaign?"

"We're fighting someone who can orchestrate a riot, control the police response, and turn it into a campaign ad within hours." Ruth's voice was steady, but Ahdia could hear the weight underneath. "That's not a criminal. That's—"

"Infrastructure." The word came out of Ahdia's mouth before she'd decided to say it. "Resources. People who follow orders."

Ruth nodded. "Okay. New parameters." She looked around the room—the room that wasn't hers, the room Ahdia had offered up like a normal person who did normal things. "Ben—keep pulling on the provocateur thread. I want names, funding sources, anything that connects back."

"On it."

"Tess—social media. Someone's controlling the narrative. Find out who's pushing the 'violent protesters' framing and trace it back."

Tess had her phone out before Ruth finished the sentence. "Already seeing bot patterns. Give me a few hours."

"Leah—activist networks. They need to know they're being infiltrated. Warn them without burning our own access."

"I know people." Leah was already standing. "I'll reach out."

"What about the community?" Victor's voice was quiet but firm. "Isaiah's family, the organizers—they're going to be blamed for this."

"That's on all of us." Ruth met his eyes. "We protect them how we can. But we can't fight the framing if we don't understand who's doing it."

Ruth paused. That pause—Ahdia knew that pause. It meant *there's more, and you're not going to like it*.

"There's something else." Ruth's voice shifted. Still steady, but different. The way she sounded when she was about to deliver bad news she'd already made peace with. "I heard from Bourn this morning."

The room went still. CADENS. Director Bourn. The shadow organization they'd stolen their gear from six months ago, back when "borrowed without asking" felt like the right phrase and not the lie it was.

"She could have called it theft," Ruth continued. "Federal charges. Asset recovery. All of it. Instead she's offering repairs. Upgrades. Legitimacy." A beat. "All she wants is for us to show up places she can't."

"That's a hell of an 'all she wants,'" Tess muttered.

"What kind of places?" Ben asked. His voice was neutral, tactical. Already calculating.

"Events where Geneva Windrow and Harding Kain intersect. CADENS has been logging anomalous EM readings every time they're in the same room. The riot. Campaign rallies. Press conferences." Ruth's jaw tightened. "They don't know what's causing it. They want eyes on the ground. Deniable eyes."

"And we're the deniable eyes." Victor's tone was flat. "Mercenary work for spooks."

"Contract work," Ruth corrected. "We stay independent. We choose which jobs we take. But yes—we're now on retainer for an organization that operates in the shadows and wants us to be their public face when things go sideways."

"Why us?" Leah asked. "They've got their own people."

"Because their own people can't be seen. CADENS is a shadow. If something goes wrong at a presidential debate and CADENS assets are involved, that's an international incident. If Go Squad is involved?" Ruth shrugged. "We're already vigilantes. We're already politically aligned with Geneva. We're already—"

"Expendable," Ahdia said.

Ruth didn't deny it.

"There's a debate in three days. Geneva versus Kain. First major face-to-face since the riot." Ruth looked around the room. "That's our first deployment. Security presence, officially. But really—"

"Really we're sensors." Ahdia felt the words come out flat. "I'm a sensor. My Seed reacts to whatever's causing those readings. That's why Bourn wants me there."

Ruth met her eyes. Didn't look away.

"Yes."

The silence stretched. Ahdia could feel the others looking at her—not with pity, not with judgment. Just that specific weight of people who knew they were about to ask you to do something dangerous and couldn't figure out how to feel about it.

"And Ahdia's treatment?" Ben asked quietly.

"Part of the deal." Ruth's voice was careful now. "Ryu continues her care. CADENS provides the resources. In exchange—"

"In exchange I show up and let them use my chest as a Geiger counter." Ahdia laughed, short and humorless. "Great. Love that for me."

"You don't have to—" Ruth started.

"Yeah, I do." Ahdia cut her off. "Because my treatment is non-negotiable, and you already said yes, and this is the price." She looked at Ruth—really looked. Saw the exhaustion underneath the leadership mask. The guilt she was trying not to show. "You made a deal with the devil to keep me alive. I'm not going to make you feel bad about it."

Ruth's expression flickered. Something like gratitude, something like pain.

"New gear is already on the way," she said. "CADENS-issued. Better than what we stole. Should arrive before the debate."

"Wonderful," Tess said. "I'm sure they won't install tracking devices or anything."

"They definitely will," Ben said. "I'll find them."

They were planning. Good. That's what teams did. They sat in each other's apartments and made deals with shadow organizations and assigned tasks and moved forward.

Ahdia should be part of this. She should be paying attention, contributing, being a teammate.

But her eyes kept drifting to the window.

The city at night, all those lights. All those people who went home safe because she'd held time still for forty-seven minutes.

Minus one.

Daniel Farah. Thirty-one. Accountant.

She moved toward the window without really deciding to. The others were still talking—timelines, check-ins, next steps. She was half-listening.

The balcony doors were floor-to-ceiling glass. Best view in Caledonia. Her parents had paid for it, back when her parents were alive to pay for things. Now it was just hers—the view, the penthouse, the money, all of it bought with the price of people who didn't come home.

There was someone on that rooftop.

The building across the plaza—shorter than hers, but not by much. And on the roof, a figure. Standing at the edge. Looking this way.

Purple light. Flickering around the figure like lightning that forgot to strike.

She blinked. Moved closer to the glass.

That's not—

Who stands on a rooftop in the dark with purple lightning?

"Ahdia?" Ruth's voice, behind her. "You okay?"

She looked back. Just for a second.

Looked back at the rooftop.

Empty.

"Yeah." Her voice came out almost normal. "Just... tired."

The rooftop was empty. No figure. No lightning. Just the city, indifferent, going about its night.

She was seeing things. Forty-seven minutes of frozen time, one dead accountant, and now she was hallucinating watchers on rooftops.

Great. Very normal. Very stable.

"You should rest." Ruth had that tone—the medical one, the one that wasn't really a suggestion. "We've got the assignments covered. Ryu's got your treatment schedule—0800, don't skip it."

Rest. In her apartment. That currently had six people in it.

"I'll rest when you all leave."

"Hint taken." Leah was already heading for the door.

They filed out. One by one. Victor paused at the door, looked like he wanted to say something—probably something kind, something about Isaiah or the riot or the fact that she'd saved twenty-three people tonight. He didn't say it. Just nodded and left.

Tess gave her a look on the way out. Not comfort. Acknowledgment. *I see you. You're still standing. Good enough.*

Ben was already on his phone, pulling threads, the job more important than goodbyes.

And then they were gone.

And it was just her and the screens.

Kain's face, still talking. She'd muted him at some point, but she could read his lips. *Leadership. Safety. Order.* Words that meant nothing, words that meant everything, words that would be on campaign signs by morning.

The rooftop across the plaza. Empty.

Daniel Farah. Thirty-one. Accountant.

She didn't turn off the screens. She sat on her couch—her couch again, finally, the cushion remembering her shape—and watched Kain's mouth move.

She didn't sleep.

# Chapter 2: The Assassination

## Scene 1: The Memorial

Three days since the riot. Three days since Daniel Farah didn't go home.

Ahdia stood in the wings of the Caledonia Civic Center, watching two thousand people file into velvet seats for Daniel Farah's memorial. The hall was one of those old buildings that tried very hard to look important—columns, gilded trim, a ceiling mural that probably meant something to someone who'd taken art history. She'd never taken art history. She'd taken computer science until she dropped out, and now she was standing backstage at a memorial for a man she couldn't save.

She did not belong there.

"Everyone in position?" Ruth's voice in her earpiece, calm and professional.

"North balcony clear." Ben. "Good sightlines on the crowd."

"Stage left," Ahdia said. "Feeling extremely useful. Definitely contributing."

"Blending in," Leah said from somewhere in the audience. "Also feeling extremely useful."

"Protesters are three blocks out," Victor reported. "Peaceful. Signs, candles, the usual."

"Eyes on audience," Tess added. "No obvious threats. Lots of pearls and blazers, though. Very threatening fashion choices."

This was what Go Squad did now, apparently. Contract work for shadow organizations. Standing around in new gear—CADENS-issued, not stolen this time, which meant tracking devices Ben hadn't found yet—waiting for something to go wrong.

Geneva Convention. That's what Bourn called it. The internal CADENS designation for events where Geneva Windrow and Harding Kain intersected. Every time they were in the same room, CADENS instruments went haywire. EM anomalies spiking off the charts. The riot had been the worst—readings so extreme they'd fried two monitoring stations—but it happened at campaign rallies too. Press conferences. Fundraisers. Anywhere the two of them breathed the same air.

Nobody knew why. That was the problem. CADENS had theories—Kain's campaign had access to something, Geneva was being targeted, cosmic interference, plain old equipment malfunction—but theories weren't answers. Answers required data. Data required sensors.

Ahdia was a sensor now. Her Seed reacted to whatever was causing those readings. That's why Bourn wanted her here. That's why Ruth had agreed to the deal. That's why she was standing backstage at a memorial for a man she couldn't save, waiting to feel something wrong in her chest.

The official cover was simpler: protect Geneva from harassment. She'd been vocal about police violence, about the riot, about the systems that let Daniel Farah die. That made her a target—not for assassination, Ruth had said, but for intimidation. The kind of slow-burn threat that came from angry people who'd been told their anger was righteous.

So here they were. Watching for thrown tomatoes and angry protesters. And also watching for EM spikes that might mean something worse.

Ahdia hadn't told anyone about the other reason she'd volunteered.

Since the riot, something had been wrong. Not the baseline drop—that was quantifiable, manageable, a number Ryu tracked on his tablet. This was different. Pressure in her chest at random moments. Dreams she couldn't remember but woke from with her heart racing. The Seed felt... restless. Like it was waiting for something.

She needed to know if it was real or if forty-seven minutes of frozen time had finally broken something in her head that couldn't be fixed.

"Ahdia," Ruth said. "How's your chest?"

Not *how are you feeling*. Not *how's the baseline*. How's your chest. Because that's why she was really here.

"Pressure," Ahdia admitted. "Low-grade. Started about an hour ago."

Silence on comms. Everyone knew what that meant.

"Same as the riot?" Ruth asked.

"Lighter. But yeah. Same flavor."

She didn't mention the dreams. The restlessness. The sense that something was building toward a moment she couldn't see yet. That felt too much like crazy, and she was already pushing the limits of what the team would tolerate from their resident time-freezing liability.

"Stay sharp. If it spikes—"

"You'll be the first to know."

She didn't add: *that's assuming I know before whatever happens, happens.* At the riot, she'd felt the spike three seconds before everything went sideways. Three seconds wasn't much warning.

It was more than Geneva Windrow would get.

Ahdia scanned the hall. VIPs in the front rows, press in the back, plainclothes security doing their earpiece-and-sunglasses thing near the stage. Everything looked exactly like a political memorial was supposed to look—somber lighting, flowers on the stage, a blown-up photo of Daniel Farah smiling in a way that made her chest hurt.

The pressure in her sternum pulsed. Just once. Like a heartbeat that didn't belong to her.

"Off like 'someone might throw a tomato,'" she said, "or off like 'assassination attempt'?"

Ruth didn't answer. Which was an answer.

---

The memorial started at 7 PM sharp.

Geneva Windrow took the podium first—tall, composed, the kind of presence that made you sit up straighter without knowing why. She wore a navy suit that probably cost more than Ahdia's monthly food budget, but she didn't wear it like armor. She wore it like work clothes. Like she had a job to do.

"Three days ago," Geneva said, "Caledonia witnessed tragedy."

The hall went quiet. Not the polite quiet of an audience waiting for applause lines. The heavy quiet of people who knew what was coming.

"A man named Daniel Farah died in a protest turned violent. He was thirty-one years old. An accountant."

She looked down at her notes. Touched the edge of the paper. Didn't read from it.

"I have a speech prepared. My team worked very hard on it. Polling data, policy proposals, a really excellent pivot to my infrastructure plan." A small, tired smile. "They're going to be upset with me."

She stepped out from behind the podium. The stage lights followed her, harsh and unforgiving.

"I visited Daniel's mother yesterday. Gloria Farah. She lives in a two-bedroom apartment in Eastwick with photographs covering every wall. I was supposed to stay fifteen minutes. Photo op, condolences, back to the motorcade. That's how these things work. You learn to schedule grief in increments."

Geneva stopped at the edge of the stage. Closer to the audience than any handler would have approved.

"I stayed three hours."

Someone in the crowd shifted. A cough. Otherwise, silence.

"Gloria showed me Daniel's room. He still lived with her—was saving up for a down payment on a condo, she said. Wanted to stay close. She showed me his desk where he did freelance accounting on weekends. Showed me his bookshelf. He liked science fiction. Asimov, Butler, Le Guin. His copies were worn soft at the spines."

Ahdia felt something in her chest that wasn't the Seed. Something older. More ordinary.

"And then she showed me his trains."

Geneva's voice caught. Just slightly. She didn't try to hide it.

"Daniel built model trains. Had a whole layout in his spare bedroom—mountains made of papier-mâché, tunnels he'd carved himself, a little town with a bakery and a church and a barbershop with a tiny striped pole. Twelve years he'd been working on it. Since college. His mother said he'd come home from work and spend hours in that room. Said it was the only time he really relaxed."

She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Took a breath.

"Gloria asked if I wanted to see it run. So I sat in her dead son's bedroom, in his desk chair that still had his jacket hanging on the back, and I watched his trains go around the track. The little town. The mountains. The tunnel he carved when he was twenty years old. Around and around."

The hall was absolutely still. Ahdia had stopped scanning for threats. Everyone had stopped everything.

"We sat there for twenty minutes. She held my hand. We didn't say anything. There was nothing to say."

Geneva looked up at the lights. Blinked. Looked back at the audience.

"My advisors told me not to talk about this. They said it's not strategic. They said voters want to see strength, confidence, solutions. They said—" She laughed, short and humorless. "They said 'don't cry on stage, it polls badly with independents.'"

A murmur in the crowd. Not disapproval. Recognition.

"Here's what I'm not supposed to tell you."

She moved back toward the podium but didn't stand behind it. Leaned against it instead, like she was tired. Like the performance had become too heavy to carry.

"I don't know what policy would have saved Daniel Farah. I've been doing this for twenty-two years. I've written legislation, I've chaired committees, I've given more speeches than I can count. And I sat in that bedroom with Gloria and I didn't have a single answer for her. Not one."

Ahdia's throat was tight. She thought about the riot. The forty-seven minutes. The math that would never balance.

"Harding Kain is going to stand at this podium in a few minutes and tell you Daniel died because we don't have enough police. Because protesters are dangerous. Because order must be maintained." Geneva's voice hardened, but it wasn't politician-hard. It was the hardness of someone who'd seen something they couldn't unsee. "He's going to use that man's death—that quiet man who built model trains and read science fiction and lived with his mother because he loved her—as a talking point. A pivot. A line in a stump speech."

She shook her head.

"I've done it too. I've stood at podiums and said names of dead people and pivoted to policy. That's the job. You learn to metabolize tragedy into messaging. You learn to—"

She stopped. Something crossed her face. Decision.

"I'm so tired."

The words came out soft. Unguarded. Not meant for a microphone, but the microphone caught them anyway.

"I'm so tired of being strategic about dead people. I'm tired of calculating how much grief to show. I'm tired of—" She gestured vaguely. "This. All of it. The performance."

Ahdia felt frozen, and not in the way she could control. This wasn't a speech anymore. This was someone unraveling at a podium, on live television, in front of two thousand people and however many million watching at home.

Geneva straightened. Smoothed her jacket. When she spoke again, her voice was steadier, but the rawness was still there underneath.

"Daniel's girlfriend is still in the hospital. Maya. She has a broken collarbone and three fractured ribs from being trampled. She's twenty-nine years old. They were going to get engaged next month—Gloria showed me the ring. He'd been carrying it around for weeks, waiting for the right moment."

She looked directly into the camera. Not performing for it. Looking through it.

"Maya's going to wake up and Daniel won't be there. There's no policy for that. There's no legislation that fixes that. There's no five-point plan that brings him back to finish his trains or give her that ring or—"

Her voice broke. She let it.

"I don't have answers tonight. I know that's not what you want to hear. I know you came here for a debate, for zingers and soundbites, for someone to tell you they've got it all figured out."

She wiped her eye with the back of her hand. Didn't try to hide it.

"I don't have it figured out. What I have is Gloria Farah's phone number in my pocket because I promised I'd call her tomorrow. What I have is a lot of doubt and not enough sleep and the memory of watching a dead man's trains go around a track while his mother held my hand."

Geneva looked at the photograph of Daniel behind her. Then back at the audience.

"But I have something else too."

Her voice changed. Not harder—deeper. Like she was reaching for something she didn't usually say out loud.

"I have a vision. And I'm going to share it with you tonight, even though my team is going to kill me, even though it's not polling-tested or focus-grouped, even though it sounds—" She paused. "Even though it sounds impossible."

She stepped forward again. Away from the podium entirely now. Standing at the very edge of the stage, close enough that people in the front row could have touched her.

"I see a world."

The words hung there. Simple. Strange.

"I see a world where Gloria Farah doesn't have to choose between paying rent and paying for her diabetes medication. Where Daniel didn't have to work weekends doing freelance accounting just to save for a down payment on a modest home. Where Maya doesn't wake up to medical bills that will bankrupt her before she's finished grieving."

Geneva's eyes were distant now. Focused on something the audience couldn't see.

"I see a world where everyone has enough. Not the same—we're not the same, we don't want the same things—but *enough*. Enough food. Enough shelter. Enough medicine. Enough security to build model trains in your spare bedroom without wondering if you'll lose everything to one bad month."

Ahdia felt something stir in her chest. Not the Seed. Not grief. Something like... hunger. Like hearing someone describe a meal when you'd forgotten you were starving.

"Some people call that socialism. Some people call it naive. Some people call it—" Geneva smiled, a little sadly. "—unrealistic. But I've sat with Gloria Farah, and I've sat with families in Appalachia and the South Side and rural Oregon, and I'm telling you: they're not asking for the moon. They're asking for *enough*."

She began to pace. Slow. Thoughtful. Like she was working something out in real-time.

"But enough isn't enough. I know that. Because I've also sat with artists who feel like their work doesn't matter. With entrepreneurs who feel suffocated by bureaucracy. With young people who want to build something new and keep being told to wait their turn."

Her voice lifted. Found a new register.

"I see a world where we have enough AND we have freedom. Where the baseline is security, but the ceiling is infinite. Where you're not one medical bill away from ruin, so you can actually take risks. Start that business. Make that art. Say what you actually think without worrying about losing your health insurance."

Ahdia noticed the security detail shifting near the exits. Restless. Like they felt it too—that something was off.

"The lie they tell us," Geneva said, "is that we have to choose. Security or freedom. Equality or excellence. Taking care of each other or letting people fly. But that's a lie told by people who benefit from us fighting over scraps."

She stopped pacing. Faced the audience directly.

"Daniel Farah built model trains. He also did freelance accounting on weekends and read science fiction and went to protests and lived with his mother. He contained multitudes. We all do. And a society that forces us to choose—that makes us pick one part of ourselves and abandon the rest—that's not a society. That's a prison with better marketing."

A ripple through the crowd. Shifting. Leaning forward.

"I see a world where the artist and the engineer sit at the same table. Where the risk-taker and the caretaker aren't enemies. Where we stop pretending that my freedom to swing my fist has to end at your nose—where we build a world with enough *room* that we don't have to constantly negotiate whose dreams matter more."

Her voice was building now. Finding something approaching fervor, but grounded. Real.

"I see a world where we take care of the vulnerable. Not because they're useful. Not because they might become productive. Because they're *people*. Because a society is measured by how it treats those who can't fight back. The disabled. The elderly. The children. The sick. The different. The difficult."

She paused. Her eyes found someone in the audience—a woman in a wheelchair near the aisle.

"I see you," Geneva said, directly to her. "And in the world I'm describing, you wouldn't have to fight so hard just to exist."

The woman's hand went to her mouth. Her companion's arm around her shoulders.

"But here's the part that keeps me up at night."

Geneva's voice dropped. Quieter now. More intimate, despite the microphones, despite the cameras, despite the two thousand people hanging on every word.

"It's not enough to have enough. It's not enough to be free. It's not enough to be taken care of. We need something else. Something harder to name."

She touched her chest. Right over her heart.

"We need it to *mean* something."

The hall was so quiet Ahdia could hear the electrical hum of the stage lights.

"I've met people who have everything. Money, security, freedom, health. And they're *empty*. I've met people who have nothing—nothing material, nothing secure—and they're the most alive humans I've ever encountered. Because they have *purpose*. They have *struggle*. They have something to push against."

She shook her head slowly.

"This is the part that scares me. Because I want to end suffering. I want to end poverty and disease and violence and all the thousand ways we break each other. But I also know—I *know*—that some part of us needs the struggle. Needs the challenge. Needs the possibility of failure to make success mean anything."

Ahdia's breath caught. This wasn't a campaign speech anymore. This was something else. Something that felt almost dangerous.

"How do you build a world where no one *has* to suffer, but everyone still has the chance to *strive*? Where we've solved the problems that destroy people, but we haven't solved away the problems that *make* people? Where we've ended the meaningless pain but kept the meaningful challenges?"

Geneva laughed softly. At herself, it seemed.

"I don't know. I really don't. Maybe it's impossible. Maybe we're built for scarcity and we'd fall apart in abundance. Maybe we need our brokenness more than we know."

She looked up. Something fierce in her eyes now.

"But I refuse to accept that. I refuse to believe that the only way humanity works is if most of us are suffering. I refuse to tell Gloria Farah that her son had to die for some cosmic balance to be maintained. That's not a society. That's a sacrifice machine, and we're all just fuel."

Her voice rose. Found its full power.

"I believe—I have to believe—that we can build something better. Something that keeps the struggle without requiring the suffering. Something that gives everyone enough without making anyone empty. Something that—"

She stopped. Smiled. A real smile, the first one of the night.

"Something that lets a quiet man build model trains in his spare bedroom, and take risks when he's ready, and be taken care of when he falls, and find meaning in all of it. Without needing to die at thirty-one to remind us that life matters."

The applause started. Not the polite debate applause. Something rawer. Wetter. People crying and clapping at the same time. People who had forgotten they were at a political event and remembered they were humans watching another human say true things out loud.

Geneva raised her hand. The applause faded. She had one more thing to say.

"I know this sounds like a dream. I know my opponent is going to call me a utopian. And maybe he's right. Maybe the world I'm describing can't exist. Maybe Thomas More was right and *utopia* really does mean *nowhere*—the place that can't be."

She stepped back toward the podium. Put her hands on its edges. Leaned forward like she was telling a secret to two thousand people.

"But I'd rather spend my life reaching for nowhere than accept that this—" She gestured. At the hall. At everything. At the whole broken world. "—that this is the best we can do. I'd rather fail trying to build something beautiful than succeed at maintaining something ugly."

She straightened. Found her formal voice again, but it was different now. Softer. Like the performance was finally aligned with the person.

"Daniel Farah went to that protest because he believed showing up mattered. He believed that bodies in the street could change things. That visibility was power."

Her voice cracked slightly. She let it.

"He was right. It does matter. It can change things. That's why people like Harding Kain work so hard to make it dangerous."

She looked out at the audience. Two thousand faces. Some crying. Some stone-faced. Some leaning forward like they'd never heard a politician speak before. Like they'd forgotten politicians *could* speak like this.

"I can't bring Daniel back. I can't promise you I'll build the world I just described. I can't even promise I'll win this election." A ghost of a smile. "My advisors are definitely firing me after this."

A ripple of laughter. Wet, uncomfortable, relieved.

"What I can promise is this: if you give me the chance, I will never stop reaching for that world. I will never accept that suffering is necessary. I will never—"

The pressure in Ahdia's chest spiked.

Her HUD flickered—EM readings spiking hard, same pattern as the riot, stronger now, much stronger. The hum behind her sternum was almost painful. She opened her mouth to say something, to warn—

"—I will never forget that Daniel Farah built model trains, and read science fiction, and loved his mother, and went to a protest because he believed—he *believed*—"

The window behind the stage made a sound like a single note struck on crystal. A small, clean hole appeared in the glass—perfectly circular, edges fused smooth.

Geneva stopped mid-word.

Her mouth was still open. Her hand was still extended toward the audience—reaching, like she could touch them, like she could close the distance between podium and people, between politician and human being, between the broken world and the beautiful one she'd been describing.

*—he believed—*

The lights went out in her eyes.

Not metaphorically. Ahdia watched it happen. One moment Geneva Windrow was *there*—present, alive, burning with conviction—and then she just... wasn't. The animating force behind her face switched off like someone had pulled a plug. Her expression didn't change. Her body didn't jerk. She simply stopped being a person and became a thing that hadn't fallen yet.

Two seconds. Maybe three. Geneva stood there, hand still reaching, mouth still open around a word she would never finish. A thin line of blood traced down her temple—almost delicate, almost nothing. The entry wound was barely visible. A dark point, no bigger than a pencil eraser.

Then gravity remembered her.

She dropped. Not dramatically, not cinematically. Just... dropped. Like a marionette with cut strings. Her body hit the podium, knocked it sideways, knocked her notes into the air—pages scattering, white rectangles floating down like leaves while her body hit the floor with a sound that was somehow worse than any gunshot.

The thud of weight. The thud of something that used to be a person and now was just mass and cooling cells.

The hall didn't scream. Not yet.

For three seconds—maybe four—maybe a lifetime—two thousand people sat in their velvet seats and didn't breathe. The silence was physical, a pressure, a vacuum where sound should be. Where the rest of her sentence should be. Where the beautiful world she'd been describing should be.

Ahdia could hear the buzz of the stage lights. The creak of someone's chair. The soft flutter of Geneva's notes settling on the floor. The distant hum of the HVAC system, doing its job, circulating air through a room where air no longer seemed to exist.

Blood pooled beneath Geneva's head. Slow. Patient. Taking its time. Spreading across the polished wood in a dark halo. Like it didn't know the urgency was over. Like it didn't know she was gone.

Her hand was still extended. Still reaching. For the audience. For the world she'd described. For nowhere.

*I'd rather spend my life reaching for nowhere than accept that this is the best we can do.*

That was the promise. That was what she'd been building toward. And now she was a body on a stage, and her nowhere was exactly as far away as it had always been, and her notes were soaking red at the edges, and her hand was still reaching.

The woman in the wheelchair in the front row was staring. Mouth open. Hand still at her face where she'd touched it when Geneva had seen her. Had said *I see you*.

No one saw her now. Everyone was looking at the body.

Daniel Farah. Gloria. The model trains. The ring Maya would never receive. The world where everyone had enough and everyone was free and everyone found meaning without suffering.

All of it. Gone. Mid-sentence. Mid-thought. Mid-reach.

Then someone in the front row started screaming, and the silence shattered, and two thousand people remembered how to move and breathe and panic, and the beautiful nowhere Geneva Windrow had been reaching for collapsed into chaos, into terror, into the ugly world she'd refused to accept.

---

"SNIPER!" Ben's voice cracked through comms. "Northwest vector, high angle!"

The hall erupted. People surging toward exits, trampling each other, the panic spreading like fire through dry grass. Ahdia was still staring at the stage. At the body. At the blood pooling on the polished wood where Geneva Windrow had been standing three seconds ago, describing a world worth living in.

"EVERYONE DOWN!" Ruth shouted.

Secret Service swarmed Kain—surrounding him, moving him toward the secure exit. Not Geneva. Geneva was beyond help. Geneva was a body now, a crime scene, a thing that used to be a person.

Ahdia had seen death before. She'd held her brother while he died. She'd watched Daniel Farah die at the riot while she froze time and couldn't save everyone.

But she'd never watched the light leave someone's eyes. She'd never seen that exact moment—the switch from person to thing, from alive to gone—happen in real-time, mid-sentence, mid-reach.

*—he believed—*

That was the last thing Geneva Windrow ever said. Not a complete thought. Not even a complete sentence. Just a fragment, hanging in the air forever.

"Ahdia!" Ruth's voice. "AHDIA!"

She blinked. The hall was chaos. People crushing toward exits, falling, screaming. The choreography had shattered into something primal and terrified.

"I'm here," she said. Her voice sounded strange. Distant. "I'm here."

"We need crowd control. Can you hold?"

Could she hold? She'd burned twenty-three points of baseline three days ago. Ryu said she needed treatment. Her chest still ached from the riot, and now there was this EM pressure, this hum that wouldn't stop, stronger than before.

On the floor near the east exit, a woman went down. People kept moving, stepping on her, stepping over her.

"I can hold," Ahdia said. "Not long. But I can hold."

She reached for the cold.

## Scene 2: Forty Seconds

The world went purple.

Darker than before. The color of a bruise, not the pale lavender she was used to. Her HUD flickered once, twice, then stabilized with a warning she didn't read.

Silence.

Two thousand people frozen mid-scream. Mid-run. Mid-fall.

Ahdia moved.

Not thinking. Couldn't think. The freeze was already slipping—she could feel it, like trying to hold water in cupped hands. Draining. Faster than it should.

Woman on the floor near the east exit. Two men about to step on her. Ahdia grabbed her under the arms, dragged her sideways, propped her against the wall. Safe. One.

Back into the crowd. Frozen faces everywhere. Mouths open. Eyes wide. A snapshot of terror she had to walk through.

Teenager pinned against a pillar. Crowd crushing in. Ahdia shoved at frozen bodies—they moved, slowly, like pushing through setting concrete. Made a gap. Pulled the kid free. Propped him in the gap. Two.

Her nose was bleeding.

She wiped it. Kept moving.

Elderly man on the ground near the center aisle. People frozen mid-step above him. One wrong angle and his ribs would crack when time resumed. She grabbed his arm, pulled him clear, felt something in her own chest *twist*—

The edges of her vision went black.

No. Not yet. One more. She could get one more.

She saw her. A woman in a wheelchair near the front row—the one Geneva had seen, had said *I see you* to—and she was tipped sideways, about to fall, about to be trampled by the crowd surging away from the stage.

Ahdia ran.

Ten feet away. Five.

The freeze broke.

Not released. *Broke*. Like a wire snapping. Like her body saying *no more* without asking permission.

Sound crashed back in. Screaming. Running. The chaos that had been frozen mid-surge now surging again, and Ahdia was on her knees somehow, palms flat on cold marble, blood dripping from her nose onto the floor.

She hadn't reached her.

Three. She'd saved three people.

The woman in the wheelchair—someone caught her. Ahdia saw it through the forest of running legs. A man in a gray suit, grabbing the chair, steadying it. Stranger. Random act of decency in the middle of horror.

Not Ahdia. She was on the floor. She couldn't even stand.

"Ahdia!" Ruth's voice in her ear. "Ahdia, report!"

"I'm—" The word came out wrong. Wet. She tried again. "Down. I'm down. East side. Near the pillar."

"Stay there. Ben's hunting the sniper. Victor's coming to you."

She didn't have a choice about staying. Her legs weren't working right. Her vision was still black at the edges, and the purple hadn't faded—everything still looked bruised, like the world itself was injured.

*CELLULAR INTEGRITY: 59%*

Dropped eight points. In forty seconds.

The math was getting worse.

Around her, the crowd was thinning. People making it to exits. Security finally doing their jobs, directing traffic, shouting orders. The initial crush was over. The panic was becoming organized evacuation.

On the stage, Geneva Windrow's body lay in a spreading pool of blood. Her hand still reaching. Her notes scattered and soaking red.

Ahdia couldn't look away.

*I'd rather spend my life reaching for nowhere than accept that this is the best we can do.*

She'd believed it. Ahdia had watched her say it and *believed* it, for just a second, that someone in power might actually mean the things they said.

And now Geneva was dead, and the nowhere she'd been reaching for was exactly as far away as ever.

"Ahdia." Victor's voice. Real voice, not comms. He was crouching beside her, hand on her shoulder. "Can you walk?"

"I don't know."

"Let's find out." He got an arm under her, lifted. Her legs wobbled but held. Barely. "There you go. Lean on me."

He half-carried her toward the side exit. Away from the stage. Away from the body. Away from the woman in the wheelchair who Ahdia hadn't been fast enough to save.

*Three*, she thought. *I saved three people.*

It didn't feel like enough. It never felt like enough.

---

They made it outside. Emergency vehicles everywhere—police, ambulances, news vans already setting up for the shot. The night air was cold and Ahdia breathed it in, trying to clear the bruise-purple from her vision.

Ruth met them by the side entrance. Her face was controlled, professional, but her eyes were doing that thing they did when she was holding too much in.

"Ben lost the sniper," she said. "Rooftop was empty by the time he got there. Professional job—they were gone before the first scream."

"How?" Victor asked.

"He's checking. But that shot..." Ruth shook her head. "800 meters, moving target, through glass. That's not amateur hour. That's military or better."

Ahdia leaned against the wall. Her legs were shaking. "She's dead."

"Yes."

"She was talking about—about a world where everyone has enough. About Daniel's trains. About—" Her voice cracked. She didn't know why. She hadn't known Geneva Windrow. Had only stood in the wings and watched her speak. But something about the way she'd died—mid-sentence, mid-reach, mid-hope—

"I know," Ruth said. Softer now. "I heard it too."

"I only saved three people."

"Three people who would have died without you."

"There were more. The woman in the wheelchair—"

"Someone else got her. I saw."

"It should have been me."

Ruth didn't argue. Didn't tell her she'd done enough, or that she couldn't save everyone, or any of the other things people said when they wanted you to feel better about failing.

Instead, she just said: "Your nose is still bleeding."

Ahdia touched her face. Her fingers came away red.

"Ryu needs to see you. Tonight. Not tomorrow."

"I know."

Leah and Tess appeared from around the corner. Tess was pale. Leah was shaking—not from cold.

"We were ten rows away," Leah said. Her voice was strange. Flat. "We saw the whole thing. Her head just—she was talking and then she wasn't and there was—"

"Don't," Ruth said. "Not here. Not yet."

"I want to hit something."

"I know. Later."

Tess hadn't said anything. She was staring at her phone, scrolling, her face doing something complicated.

"What?" Ruth asked.

"Kain's about to give a statement. Live. CNN."

They gathered around the phone. Ahdia didn't want to watch, but she couldn't look away.

Harding Kain stood at a podium somewhere—his campaign headquarters, probably. American flags behind him. The lighting was perfect. He looked *ready*, like he'd been waiting for this moment.

"Tonight," he said, "Caledonia lost a leader. Governor Geneva Windrow was a public servant who dedicated her life to this city. We disagreed on many things—but I respected her commitment to her beliefs."

His voice was smooth. Practiced. Every word calibrated.

"This act of domestic terrorism will not stand. The radical elements who perpetrated this violence—who turned a memorial for Daniel Farah into a scene of bloodshed—will be found and brought to justice."

Ahdia's chest tightened. *Radical elements.* He was already framing it. Already spinning it.

"Geneva wanted to protect Caledonia's vulnerable. She can't anymore." Kain paused. Let it land. "I will. I promise you—I will make this city safe. I will restore order. I will ensure that no family has to lose a mother, a daughter, a leader, to senseless violence."

"He's campaigning," Tess said quietly. "On her corpse. He's literally—"

"Yes," Ruth said.

"It's been twenty minutes. She's not even cold yet and he's—"

"Yes."

On the screen, Kain was still talking. Something about strength. Something about security. Something about the path forward.

Ahdia stopped listening.

She was thinking about Geneva's hand. Still reaching. Still extended toward an audience that had run away screaming. Reaching for a world that didn't exist yet. That might never exist.

*I'd rather spend my life reaching for nowhere.*

Kain was reaching too. But not for nowhere.

For everything.

And right now, with Geneva's blood still wet on the floor of the Caledonia Civic Center, he was winning.

## Scene 3: Fragments

Ben came back.

Ahdia saw his face before she heard his voice. Something bad. She knew that look—the one where someone had information they didn't want to have.

"Nest was on the Meridian Building." He was talking to Ruth. "Eighteenth floor. Maintenance access."

Ruth asked something. Ahdia didn't catch it. Her ears were ringing, or maybe that was just what happened when you burned eight points of baseline in forty seconds.

"—dust on the floor. Perfect circle where the shooter stood. No footprints out. No exit trail."

Dust circle. That meant something. Ahdia tried to hold onto it. The wall was cold against her back. When had she started leaning on the wall?

"My ears were still ringing when I got there," Ben said. "Pressure differential. Ozone. I've felt it before—joint ops with translocation assets."

Ruth's face did something. Went still. That was bad. Ruth going still was always bad.

More words. Ahdia caught pieces. *Professional. Military grade. Clean extraction.* The pieces didn't fit together in her head. They floated past like debris in water.

Her legs were shaking. Had been shaking. She should sit down. She should—

The wall slid up. Or she slid down. One of those.

"I got you." Victor. His arm under hers, catching her weight. "There's a bench. Come on."

She let him move her. The bench was cold. Everything was cold. The ambulance lights made red and blue patterns on the building across the street and she watched them pulse because it was easier than listening.

The team kept talking. Ben's voice, Ruth's voice, someone else. Tess saying something about her phone, about the news, about how fast it was spreading.

Ahdia looked up.

Rooftop of the parking structure. Across the street.

Someone standing there.

She blinked. Blinked again. Her vision was blurry, purple-edged, unreliable.

The figure didn't move. Hood up. Watching.

Watching her.

Purple light flickered. Around the figure's hands. Just for a second. The same purple. The exact same purple as the bruised world she'd walked through forty seconds ago.

Ahdia opened her mouth.

The figure turned. Stepped back from the edge. Gone.

"Ahdia." Victor's voice. Close. "Hey. You with us?"

She stared at the empty rooftop. At the space where someone had been.

"I..." She shook her head. "Nothing. I'm just—"

"Gray," Ruth said. She was crouching in front of Ahdia now. When had that happened? "You're gray and your pulse is thready and Ryu's meeting us at the penthouse. Car. Now."

Ahdia didn't argue. Didn't have the energy. Victor and Ben got her up, got her moving. Leah and Tess flanking, watching the street like something else might come out of the dark.

She looked back once. At the parking structure.

Empty.

The car was warm. Ruth drove. Ahdia closed her eyes and let the city blur past the windows and didn't think about the figure on the rooftop. Didn't think about purple lightning. Didn't think about Geneva's hand still reaching on a stage somewhere behind them.

She didn't think about anything.

That was easier.

---

## Scene 4: Treatment

Ryu was already at the penthouse when they arrived.

Pacing. That specific Ryu pacing—tight circles, hands clasped behind his back, glasses doing their usual slightly-crooked thing. He turned when the elevator opened and his face cycled through about four expressions in two seconds: relief, worry, more worry, then that clinical mask he put on when he was trying not to feel too much. Like watching someone flip through TV channels looking for the right one.

"Ruth called ahead," he said. "I've got the treatment ready. You should sit."

Ahdia didn't argue. Her legs made the decision for her anyway—three steps into the living room and they just quit, union rules, and she dropped onto the couch hard enough to knock a pillow to the floor.

Okay. Okay okay okay.

Ryu was already moving. Medical kit on the coffee table, the sleek CADENS autoinjector laid out next to gauze and monitoring patches. His fingers drummed on the kit's edge—tap tap tap-tap, tap tap tap-tap—that rhythm he fell into when his brain was chewing on too many variables at once. She'd seen him do it during the riot. During Firas. During every moment when the math got too ugly to say out loud.

"Fifty-nine percent," he said, not looking at her. "You were at sixty-seven this morning. That's eight points in—" He checked his tablet. "Forty seconds. The degradation curve is accelerating, which means the cellular repair protocols need to account for—" He stopped. Took a breath. "Sorry. You don't need the math right now."

"I really don't."

Ruth was still in the doorway. "I'll check in with the others. Give you two some space."

Gone before Ahdia could protest. Not that she would have. The apartment felt too big with people in it and too empty without them, and right now she couldn't figure out which one was worse. Schrödinger's loneliness. Both states at once until someone opened the box.

Ryu sat on the coffee table across from her. Close, but not touching. He had the autoinjector in his hand, turning it over, studying it like he'd never seen one before. Which he had. Many times. On her arm specifically.

"Last time," he said quietly. "The sound. It triggered something."

She didn't answer. Didn't have to.

"I'm going to count down from three. So you know when it's coming." He met her eyes—actually met them, not the sideways glances he usually defaulted to when faces got too complicated. "Is that... would that help?"

He'd noticed. Of course he'd noticed. He got the upload. Forty-seven minutes of watching her bleed from her eyes and wave her hand through his chest like he wasn't there. He noticed everything.

"Yeah," she said. "That would help."

He nodded. Positioned the injector against her arm. His hand was steady even though she could see the tension in his shoulders, the rigid line that said *I'm holding it together because someone has to*.

"Three."

She closed her eyes.

"Two."

Geneva's hand, still reaching. The small dark hole in her temple.

"One."

*Click. Hiss.*

The sound landed somewhere deep, echoed with red light and blue light and Firas's lips moving—but Ryu's voice had been there first. The countdown gave her something to hold. A rope in the dark.

When she opened her eyes, Ryu was watching her. Not clinically. Just watching. The way you watch someone you're worried about when you don't know what to say.

"Okay?" he asked.

"Okay."

He set the injector aside. Started applying the monitoring patches—wrist, temple, base of her throat. His movements were precise, practiced, but gentler than they needed to be. Like she might break. Like she hadn't already.

"You can talk about it," he said. "Or not. I'm not—I don't need you to process out loud. But if you want to, I'm..." He gestured vaguely at himself, at the room, at the whole situation. "Here. Obviously. Physically present in this location."

Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile.

"She was talking about a world." The words came out before she'd decided to say them. "Geneva. Right before. She was describing this place where everyone has enough. Where people don't have to suffer just to make life mean something."

Ryu's hands paused on the last patch.

"She called it nowhere. Like the word utopia—means 'no place.' A place that can't exist." The ceiling was the same ceiling she'd stared at a thousand times during 3 AM insomnia spirals, but it looked different now. Farther away. Unreachable. "She said she'd rather spend her life reaching for nowhere than accept that this is the best we can do."

"That's..." Ryu was quiet. His fingers had stopped drumming entirely, which was rare. Almost unheard of. "That's a significant philosophical position."

"It was a beautiful speech. And then someone shot her in the head."

The words just sat there. Brutal. True. No softening them. No TV-appropriate euphemism. Someone shot her in the head and she died mid-sentence with her hand still reaching for an audience that was already running away.

Ryu didn't try to comfort her. Didn't say *I'm sorry* or *that must be hard* or any of the things people said when they didn't know what else to say but felt like they should say something. He just sat there. Close but not touching. Letting the silence be what it was.

"Her hand was still reaching," Ahdia said. "After. When she fell. She was reaching for the audience. For the world she was describing. And then she was just—" Her voice cracked. She hadn't expected that. "Gone. And her hand was still reaching for nowhere."

Ryu was completely still now. No drumming, no fidgeting, no adjusting his glasses. Just still.

"I watched a lot of TV," he said. "When I was younger. Before CADENS. It was easier than—" He waved a hand. "People. Faces. The TV didn't expect me to make eye contact."

She didn't know where he was going with this. But his voice was soft, and he was trying, and that counted for something.

"There was this show. Old one, before my time, but I found it online. Star Trek. The original series." A small, self-conscious shrug. "The future they described—no poverty, no war, everyone working together to explore the unknown. I knew it wasn't real. I knew it was just... television. But I used to think about it. What it would feel like to live in a world like that."

He looked at her. That same direct eye contact, the kind that cost him something.

"Geneva was describing that world. The one I used to watch on a screen and pretend was possible." His voice barely audible now. "And someone killed her for it."

Her eyes burned. Not the Seed. Not cellular degradation. Just the ordinary human kind that meant she was about to cry, which she hadn't done in—how long? Since Firas. Since the reversal. Since the last time someone sat with her and didn't try to fix anything.

"Yeah," she whispered. "Yeah, they did."

They sat there. Two people in a penthouse that was too big for one person and too small for grief. The city glittered outside the windows, doing its city thing, going about its night like nothing had happened. Like Geneva Windrow hadn't died reaching for a better world. Like the math balanced somewhere Ahdia couldn't see.

Ryu didn't touch her. Didn't try to fix anything.

Just stayed.

*That was enough,* she thought. And then: *Is it? Is that enough?*

She didn't have an answer. She let the question sit there, unresolved, the way questions did when you were too tired to chase them down.

## Scene 5: The Night After

### Ahdia

Ryu left around midnight. She'd told him she was fine, that she'd sleep, that she didn't need him to stay. All lies, but comfortable ones. The kind of lies you tell people so they don't have to watch you fall apart.

The penthouse was too quiet after he was gone. The city hummed outside the windows—traffic, distant sirens, the white noise of a million people living their lives like Geneva Windrow hadn't died mid-sentence eight hours ago.

Ahdia stood at the window. The couch was right there. The bed was in the other room. Both of them were perfectly good places to lie down and close her eyes and pretend that sleep was something she could just *decide* to do.

Instead she watched the city lights and thought about nowhere.

*I'd rather spend my life reaching for nowhere than accept that this is the best we can do.*

Geneva's hand. Still reaching. The small dark hole in her temple. The way the light had gone out of her eyes—not slowly, not peacefully, just *off*, like someone had flipped a switch.

Ahdia rubbed her eyes. They burned. Everything burned. Her chest, her head, the space behind her sternum where the Seed lived.

*You should sleep. Ryu said sleep. Ruth said sleep. Everyone says sleep like it's something you can just decide to do, like you can flip a switch and—*

Her eyes drifted to the rooftop across the plaza.

Not that rooftop. A different one. The building with the green water tower.

Figure standing at the edge.

Hood up. Face shadowed.

Purple light flickering around their hands.

*That's not—*

The figure turned. Looked directly at her. Not at the penthouse. Not at the window. At *her*. Like it could see through the glass, through the walls, through whatever distance should have made this impossible.

Ahdia's heart slammed against her ribs.

*It sees me. It knows exactly where I am.*

She blinked.

Gone.

The rooftop was empty. Green water tower. Night sky. No figure. No purple lightning. Just the city, going about its business, like nothing had happened.

"What the hell are you?" she whispered.

The window didn't answer. The city didn't answer. The pressure in her chest pulsed once, twice, and then went quiet.

Third time. That was the third time she'd seen it. Riot. Parking structure. Now.

*Three times means something*, her brain supplied unhelpfully. *Three times means you're either losing your mind or being stalked by something that glows purple. Neither option is great.*

She didn't sleep that night. She sat on her couch, in her spot, and watched the rooftops until the sun came up.

Nothing else appeared.

That didn't make her feel better.

### Tess

Tess's apartment looked like a conspiracy theorist's fever dream, which was either appropriate or deeply ironic given the circumstances.

The way she said it. Like finding water in a desert.

She turned back to the monitors.

She kept digging.

### Kain

The private office was dark except for a single lamp. Harding Kain had dismissed his staff an hour ago—told them he needed time to process, to grieve, to prepare for the days ahead. They'd believed him. They always believed him.

He wasn't grieving.

He was pacing. Twelve steps to the window, twelve steps back to the desk. The city lights spread out below like scattered embers, and somewhere out there Geneva Windrow's body was being photographed, catalogued, reduced to evidence.

His phone buzzed. Private line. The one only three people had the number for.

He answered.

"Report."

Silence. The kind of silence that had weight to it.

"I am aware." No grief in the voice. No surprise. Nothing human at all. "The Temporalist's position during the speech."

Kain stopped pacing. That wasn't the response he'd expected. Geneva was dead—the woman who'd been polling twelve points ahead, the obstacle they'd spent six months maneuvering around—and all she wanted to know about was the girl with the purple lightning?

"Nothing. Stood in the wings. Watched her die."

"The assassination attempt is a constant. It happens every time—different shooter, different weapon, same result. The Temporalist preventing it is supposed to be a constant as well. The shooter's identity is irrelevant. I do not know it. I do not care. What matters is that my piece performs its function."

"Your piece."

"The girl. Ahdia. She exists to protect Geneva. That is why the Hyper Seed bonded with her. That is why she has those powers." A pause. Something almost like emotion bleeding through the flatness. "She is my hands in a dimension I cannot reach."

Kain moved to the window. His reflection stared back at him—composed, controlled, the face he showed the cameras. But underneath, something cold was spreading through his chest.

"And she just... didn't?"

"No."

"Why?"

The silence this time was different. Heavier. Like the voice on the other end was holding something back—or holding something *in*.

"That is what you will determine."

"Me?"

"You have assets. Use them. I need to know why the Temporalist deviated from the pattern. Where she was positioned. What she was doing. What she *chose* not to do."

Kain turned from the window. "If she didn't save Geneva, maybe she's not under anyone's control. Maybe she made her own choice."

"She is a tool I created." Flat. Final. "Tools do not make choices."

"This one did."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in it, Kain thought he heard something—a breath, maybe. Or the ghost of one.

"What happens now?" he asked. "Without Geneva—"

"You have an election to win. Your opponent is dead." The voice had recovered its cold precision. "I expect you will find that... convenient."

Kain pushed. He shouldn't—he knew he shouldn't—but the question was there and it wouldn't stay down. "She has the Hyper Seed. She can freeze time. If she's gone off-script, she's dangerous. To both of us."

"An alternative Temporalist?"

"An alternative solution." Something shifted in the voice. Not warmer—colder, if anything. The cold of deep space. "The shooter will return. They always do. Without Geneva to protect, without the pattern to maintain..."

"The game has changed." The finality in those words could have frozen oceans. "Report when you have information on the Temporalist's actions."

"And if I can't get that information?"

"Then you are less useful than I believed."

The connection ended. Not a click—more like the sound had simply stopped existing, leaving Kain alone in his dark office with the city lights and the weight of what he'd just learned.

Four iterations. The voice had said *four iterations*. Like this had all happened before. Like Geneva's death was supposed to be prevented, every time, by a girl who could stop time.

And this time, the girl had chosen not to.

Kain looked out at the city. Somewhere out there, Ahdia Bacchus was probably staring at a ceiling, or a window, or the inside of her own head. Probably had no idea that her inaction had broken something much larger than one politician's life.

Or maybe she did know. Maybe that was the point.

He reached for his phone. Started making calls.

The game had changed. Time to learn the new rules.

## Scene 6: Nowhere

She existed between dimensions now. Had for years—since Montana, since the avatar's destruction, since she'd been torn from the third dimension and locked out permanently. The space she inhabited wasn't physical. It was something else. A fold in reality where a being like her could persist without a body.

It was cold. Not temperature-cold—concept-cold. The absence of warmth as a fundamental property of existence.

The upload was still happening.

Geneva's consciousness bleeding in. Not data. Not memory in the clinical sense. *Experience*. The full weight of a life flowing back to its source.

*Stop*, she thought. *This is residue. This is—*

Geneva's last moment hit her like a wave.

Hand reaching. Mouth open. The word "believed" half-formed on lips that would never finish it. The audience a blur of faces—some crying, some stone-faced, all of them *listening*, actually listening, and she'd felt it, Geneva had felt it, the moment when a room full of strangers becomes something else, something unified, something that might actually—

Bellatrix forced the experience down. Compartmentalized. She'd done this before. Three times before. She knew how to wall off the uploads, how to file them away where they couldn't touch her.

But they always touched her.

*You were supposed to live.*

The thought came unbidden. Not her thought—or was it? After four uploads, after four Genevas absorbed into her consciousness, she couldn't always tell anymore whose grief was whose.

*Four times. Four times I built you. Four times I watched you become... this.*

Another wave. Geneva in Gloria Farah's living room. The model trains going around the track. The old woman's hand in hers—papery and warm and trembling. And Geneva had felt it, actually *felt* it, the weight of a mother's loss, and it hadn't been performance, it hadn't been calculated—

*That wasn't the script. You weren't supposed to feel it. You were supposed to use it.*

But Geneva always felt it. That was the flaw in the design—or the feature, depending on how you looked at it. Consciousness copies diverged. They became their own people. They developed the capacity to genuinely care about the things they were supposed to only strategically care about.

Naima's template, reshaped by experience, became something Naima never was: a true believer.

*I know.*

She did know. She'd watched it happen four times. Four Genevas, each one starting from the same template, each one becoming someone who actually believed in the world they were trying to build. Someone who reached for nowhere like it was a real place. Like it could be touched.

The final upload hit.

Geneva's last second. Not fear—confusion. *Why am I falling? Why is the light—*

And then nothing. The abrupt termination of a consciousness that had been mid-thought, mid-reach, mid-hope.

Bellatrix absorbed it. Added it to the others. Another layer of scar tissue on her cosmic form.

*The Temporalist was there.*

The thought crystallized, cold and sharp.

*She was right there. I felt her. The Seed was active. She could have—*

The realization settled in slowly. Like frost forming on glass.

*She chose not to.*

Bellatrix existed in the space between dimensions and felt something she hadn't felt in 3.7 billion years of cosmic existence: genuine surprise.

Her own creation. Her own hands in the third dimension. The tool she'd engineered specifically to solve this unsolvable problem—the one variable she'd thought she controlled—

Had made a choice.

*You were not supposed to have choices. You were supposed to be a function. A variable I controlled.*

But consciousness copies diverged. She knew that. She'd built the Genevas knowing that. Had she thought Ahdia would be different? That the Seed would keep her aligned? That twenty years of human neurodevelopment wouldn't reshape a cosmic consciousness into something that could say *no*?

Geneva's voice, still echoing in the upload: *I'd rather spend my life reaching for nowhere than accept that this is the best we can do.*

*You reached for nowhere and you almost made me believe it.*

The thought surprised her. She tried to wall it off, but it slipped through—

*Again. Four times you've made me believe it. Four times I've watched you reach and fall and—*

She stopped.

The cold of between-dimensions pressed in. The absence of warmth. The absence of anything except her own consciousness and the four dead Genevas she carried inside it.

*And I keep doing this.*

The thought was quiet. Dangerous.

*I keep building you. I keep watching you become someone who hopes. And then I watch you die with your hand still reaching.*

The grief wasn't hers. It was Geneva's grief, uploaded and absorbed. It was Naima's grief, from before—from Montana, from the children, from the life she'd lost when she tried to end it. It was four lifetimes of accumulated loss layered over a cosmic consciousness that had eliminated emotions 3.7 billion years ago.

And she couldn't separate them anymore.

*This was supposed to fix it. The perfect version. The one who succeeds. The one who proves that—*

That what? What had she been trying to prove?

*That I wasn't wrong. That the infection can become strength. That love can build instead of break.*

Geneva's upload completed. Her consciousness fully absorbed. Another incomplete life. Another interrupted arc. Another hand reaching for nowhere that would never get there.

Bellatrix existed in the cold between dimensions and did not move.

Then, slowly, the stoicism returned. The armor she'd built over 3.7 billion years of existence. The control that had allowed her to watch worlds die without flinching.

*The Temporalist will be dealt with.*

The thought was cold. Precise. The voice she used with Kain.

*The shooter will return. They always return. And I will find another way.*

She always found another way. That was what she did. That was what she was. A being of infinite patience and infinite determination, engineering solutions across cosmic timescales.

*I always find another way.*

But underneath the cold, something Geneva had left behind:

A hand. Still reaching.

For nowhere. For something that couldn't exist. For a world where everyone had enough and everyone was free and a quiet man could build model trains in his spare bedroom without dying at thirty-one.

Bellatrix felt it. The reach. The hope.

She couldn't stop feeling it.

*He believed—*

She cut the thought off. Walled it away. Added it to the others.

The space between dimensions was cold. The absence of warmth as a fundamental property.

She existed there alone, carrying four deaths, four hopes, four reaches toward nowhere that never got there.

And somewhere, in a dimension she couldn't touch, a girl who was supposed to be her hands had decided to stop reaching.

The pattern was broken.

Bellatrix would have to build a new one.

She always did.
# Chapter 3: The Gaps

## Scene 1: Team Debrief

The penthouse felt different in daylight. Smaller, somehow.

Ahdia had watched from this same couch as Geneva Windrow's head exploded on live television, and now here she was, eighteen hours later, watching Ruth pace in front of the windows like a general without a map. Ben needed translocation logs.

Ryu had both.

## Scene 2: Treatment

The doorbell was the first sound Ahdia had registered in hours.

She'd been watching CNN with the sound off—talking heads discussing Geneva's "legacy," which apparently meant arguing about whether she'd been too radical or not radical enough. Nobody on screen seemed to remember she'd been a person twenty-four hours ago. A person who'd stayed three hours in a grieving mother's apartment because she couldn't make herself leave. Ryu stood there with a medical case that probably cost more than her first car. Like he'd come out of the womb worried about someone's cellular integrity.

Neither of them believed it. She stepped aside to let him in.

"Okay, so, this is like... *Crank*? The Jason Statham movie?"

Ryu's hands paused. "I'm not familiar."

"Guy has to keep his adrenaline up or his heart stops. Except mine is 'use powers less or cells stop.'"

"That's somewhat reductive but not entirely inaccurate."

"Wait, you HAVE seen it?"

A slight smile crossed his face. "I reviewed your media consumption history. Contextual understanding aids communication."

Ahdia stared at him. "You studied how I talk?"

"I studied how you think. You process reality through narrative frameworks. Television, films, genre conventions. It's not ineffective, merely... indirect."

She wasn't sure if she should be touched or creeped out. Landed somewhere in the middle.

Ryu held up the syringe. "This will feel cold. Then warm. Then nothing."

The needle entered her arm. Cold spreading through her bloodstream—foreign, synthetic, not quite pain but not quite anything else either. Then warm—her body recognizing, accepting, cellular repair activating. Then nothing.

But something fundamental had shifted. She could feel it in her chest, the Hyper Seed responding to whatever Ryu had injected. Like giving water to a plant that had been dying slowly.

"So that's keeping me alive?"

"That's extending your cellular viability by approximately 18-24 months."

"You're very careful not to call it 'keeping me alive.'"

"Language precision is important in medical contexts."

"But I AM dying. Just... slower now."

Ryu paused. Weighed his words with the care of someone defusing a bomb.

"Your human cellular structure is degrading from temporal stress. The treatment counteracts this degradation."

"That's a very fancy way of saying 'yes, dying.'"

"Degradation and death are not always synonymous."

Ahdia caught it. Weird phrasing. "What does THAT mean?"

"It means the treatment is working as designed." He resumed packing his equipment, not meeting her eyes.

Before she could press, he continued:

"I need to make you aware of something. CADENS monitors temporal distortions globally. When you use your powers, we register the signature."

"You're tracking me?"

"We're tracking temporal anomalies. Yours happen to be... distinctive."

Every time she used her powers, CADENS knew. The realization settled into her chest next to the cold of the Seed.

"This is not surveillance," Ryu said. "This is protection. If WE can track temporal signatures—"

"Others can too."

"A flare we can triangulate to within ten meters. If someone else has that technology, they know where you are and when you're vulnerable."

The weight of it landed. She wasn't just dying faster when she used powers. She was advertising her location to anyone with the right equipment.

"This is why we come to you," Ryu said. "House call, not facility summons. Protection."

Ahdia understood: the house call was keeping her location private from CADENS staff. A small kindness. Or a leash disguised as kindness. She couldn't tell anymore.

"You're valuable, Ms. Bacchus. Act accordingly."

He resumed packing. Monitoring complete. But Ahdia's brain wouldn't stop turning.

"Ms. Bacchus, may I ask a personal question?"

"That's usually when doctors tell you you're dying faster."

"When did you last leave this apartment for non-essential purposes?"

Silence. She didn't have an answer. Or she had an answer and didn't want to say it.

"Isolation exacerbates depression," Ryu said quietly. "Depression exacerbates risk-assessment impairment."

"I'm not impaired. I did the investigation fine—"

"You burned 7% cellular integrity investigating a crime that had no immediate personal stakes."

Another hit. She'd used powers to feel useful, not because they were necessary.

"The treatment keeps your body functional. But your decision-making determines how long 'functional' lasts."

Depression was making her waste lifespan. The cage was becoming visible now—bars she hadn't noticed forming around her.

Freedom costs life. Using powers burns baseline.
Life costs freedom. Treatment keeps her dependent on CADENS.

She can't use powers without being tracked.
She can't survive without treatment.
She can't matter without burning the life she needs treatment to extend.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"CADENS tracks translocation signatures, right? Like, who's authorized, when it's used?"

Ryu's hands stilled on his equipment. Just for a second. Then he resumed packing.

"That's classified operational data."

"I'm not asking for state secrets. Ben found a translocation signature at the sniper nest. He can't trace it without access logs."

"And you want me to...?"

"I don't know." She heard her own voice—flat, tired, the sound of someone who'd stopped pretending. "I just want to help. The team's investigating and I'm sitting here getting juice boxes and naps while they do the actual work."

"Treatment isn't optional. Without it—"

"I know. Eleven days. You told me."

The number hung in the air. Eleven days without treatment. That's how long she had before her cells gave up entirely.

"Geneva was trying to build something." Ahdia's voice had gone quiet. "And someone erased her mid-sentence. While I stood there."

"You couldn't have known—"

"I have powers that stop time and I couldn't save one person who actually mattered."

Ryu stopped packing. He stood there, medical case half-closed, looking at her with an expression she couldn't name. Concern. Guilt. Something else.

"The team's hitting walls," Ahdia said. "Ben needs translocation access. Tess needs to trace the bot network funding. And I'm just... here. On this couch. Watching news I can't hear about a woman I couldn't save."

"You're recovering. That's not nothing."

"It feels like nothing."

Long pause. Ryu's fingers drummed against the case—three taps, then stop. She'd noticed he did that when he was processing something difficult.

"I could look into a few things." His voice was careful. "Off the record."

Ahdia looked at him. "Really?"

"I'm not promising anything. But if there's information that could help the investigation... you're not wrong that CADENS would have it."

"That would be... yeah. That would help."

"I'll see what I can find."

He finished packing. Paused at the door.

"Get some rest, Ahdia."

The door clicked shut behind him.

Ahdia sat alone in her penthouse, treatment coursing through her veins, keeping her human for another few weeks. The cage was visible now—dependent on CADENS to survive, trackable whenever she used powers, isolated by depression, wasting the life she needed treatment to extend.

On the muted TV, a newscaster was saying something about a dictator in Eastern Europe. Some strongman vanishing without explanation. Unrelated. Background noise.

She didn't notice it yet.

But she would.

## Scene 3: The Kettle

The kettle wasn't boiling because Ruth hadn't turned it on.

She knew this. Had known it for—how long now? Three minutes. Five. The blue pilot light glowed but the flame wasn't lit, and her hands rested flat on the counter like they belonged to someone else.

She'd finally done it tonight. Couldn't sleep anyway, everyone gone home, the penthouse too quiet. Thought: just start. One drawer. You can do one drawer.

Eight months of walking past his jacket on the chair. Eight months of not opening his side of the closet. His books still stacked on the nightstand in their bedroom—her bedroom now, just hers, except it wasn't because it still smelled like him when she let herself notice.

She'd opened his dresser. First drawer. Socks. Just socks. She could fold them, box them, donate them. Normal task. Manageable.

Then her hand hit the box.

Small. Velvet. Hidden under wool socks he'd never worn because Caledonia wasn't cold enough.

She'd known what it was before she opened it.

She'd opened it anyway.

Simple ring. Silver band. Small diamond. He'd known she didn't want anything flashy. He'd known her taste better than she did.

She'd closed it. Put it back. Closed the drawer. Walked out here. Put the kettle on.

Forgot to turn it on.

Had been standing here since.

"Hey."

Ahdia's voice, soft from the doorway. Ruth didn't startle—old ER habit, always know who's behind you—but she didn't turn around either.

"Can't sleep?"

"Treatment makes me jittery." A pause. Shuffling footsteps. "You?"

Ruth didn't answer. Just looked at the kettle that wasn't boiling.

Ahdia moved into the kitchen. Got herself water. Leaned against the counter a few feet away, giving Ruth space without leaving. For a while neither of them spoke. The pilot light flickered blue. The microwave clock said 2:17 AM.

Ruth reached out. Turned the dial. The flame caught, licking the bottom of the kettle.

"I tried to clear his things tonight."

She heard Ahdia go still behind her.

"First time." Ruth's voice came out flat, too controlled. "Eight months and I finally thought I could."

Pause. Swallow.

"I found a ring. In his sock drawer."

Silence. Ruth could feel Ahdia processing it.

"He was going to propose." The words tasted like ash. "I wasn't supposed to find it. He was waiting. For the right moment. After things calmed down."

She watched the flame heat the water. Watched bubbles start to form at the bottom of the kettle.

"And then there wasn't one."

Her hands were shaking now, pressed flat against the counter like she could hold herself together through the granite.

"I put it back. Closed the drawer. Came out here." Her voice got smaller. "I can't do it. I thought I could and I can't."

"Ruth—"

"I was going to tell him I loved him." The words came faster now, eight months of holding them back. "I kept waiting for the right moment and I kept thinking there would be time and then there wasn't any time and now there's a ring in his sock drawer that I was never supposed to find and I never said it, I never—"

Her voice cracked. Broke.

"He knew, right?" Almost begging. "He must have known."

"He knew." Ahdia's voice was quiet but certain.

"How? If I never said it?"

"Because you were you. With him."

Ruth turned around. Looked at Ahdia—really looked—and something in her chest finally gave way.

She was crying. First time since Firas died. First time since the singularity took him. First time since she'd started holding everything together because someone had to.

"His things are still everywhere." Sobbing now, couldn't stop. "His jacket on the chair. His books. I walk past them every day and I can't—I thought if I just started I could—"

The kettle started to whistle. Neither of them moved.

Ahdia stepped closer. Not touching. Just there. Present. Witnessing.

Ruth didn't know how long she cried. Long enough for the kettle to scream and then go quiet when the water boiled down. Long enough for the shaking to subside into something duller, heavier.

She turned off the burner. Wiped her eyes with the back of her hand.

"I'm sorry." Her voice was wrecked. "You didn't need to—"

"Stop."

Ruth looked at her.

"You're allowed to fall apart." Ahdia's voice was awkward, uncertain, like she was reading from a script she'd only half-memorized. "And lead. Both things."

Ruth almost laughed. Wet, broken. "Both things."

Silence. But different now. Less like drowning.

"You don't have to clear it all tonight," Ahdia said.

"I know. I just thought if I started—"

"You started. You opened a drawer. That counts."

Ruth nodded. Barely.

"And you should make tea." Ahdia gestured at the empty kettle. "That's what it was for. Before you boiled it dry."

Ruth filled the kettle again. Turned it on. Hands still shaking, but moving. Ahdia stayed—didn't fill the silence with words, didn't try to fix anything. Just present.

The drawer in the bedroom was still closed. The ring was still there, hidden under wool socks he'd never wear.

But Ruth had told someone. After eight months, she'd said it out loud.

That was something. That was a drawer opened.

Tomorrow she would be the leader again. She would hold everything together because someone had to.

Tonight she was a woman who found a ring and couldn't finish clearing a sock drawer. And someone had witnessed it. Someone she trusted.

She didn't know yet what that trust would cost her. What Ahdia was already hiding, would keep hiding, would hide for months while Ruth defended her absences and made excuses to the team. She didn't know that the woman standing in her kitchen right now was lying about everything except this moment.

But that was later. That was a different kitchen, a different conversation, a different kind of breaking.

Tonight, there was just tea, and silence, and the small mercy of not being alone with a velvet box in a sock drawer.

d# Chapter 4: Dependencies

## Scene 1: The Call

The hologram room was designed for secrets.

Soundproofed walls, signal encryption that would take a quantum computer decades to crack, no windows, no witnesses. Harriet Bourn had held conversations in this room that would have ended careers, toppled governments, prevented wars no one would ever know almost happened.

Today she stood alone, waiting for a call she'd been dreading for weeks.

The projector hummed. Light coalesced into the shape of a man in a Pentagon office—Secretary of Defense Patterson, his face arranged in the careful neutrality of someone about to threaten you politely.

"Director Bourn."

"Mr. Secretary." She matched his tone. "To what do I owe the pleasure?"

"Cut the civility. We both know why I'm calling."

Bourn waited. She'd learned decades ago that the person who speaks first in these conversations loses.

"Geneva Windrow," Patterson said. "You had CADENS assets investigating her assassination."

"Consultation at the request of local law enforcement—"

"Consultation. With your enhanced assets. Off the books."

Bourn's face stayed neutral. So Patterson had been monitoring CADENS activity. That wasn't surprising. What was surprising was that he was admitting it.

"CADENS operates with near-total autonomy," Patterson continued. "Defense doesn't get briefed. Intelligence doesn't get briefed. You answer to one person—the President—and even that's more courtesy than requirement."

"Our charter is Presidential. That's been the arrangement for forty years."

"Your charter is a blank check written by an administration that's about to change."

There it was. The threat, finally visible.

"CADENS answers to whoever holds the office," Bourn said carefully. "That hasn't changed."

"Then you should be very interested in who that might be next year."

Bourn felt the calculation happening—Patterson backing Kain, or at least hedging his bets. The polls had Kain up eight points since Geneva's death. The smart money was already shifting.

"When oversight finally comes," Patterson said, "and it will come—CADENS will answer to more than just one person."

"Oversight. Or control?"

"Is there a difference?"

"CADENS was designed to operate independently for a reason—"

"To avoid accountability. Yes, I've read the documentation."

"To avoid *political* accountability. Cataclysm-derived threats don't wait for committee approval."

"And who decides what qualifies as 'cataclysm-derived'? You? Personally?"

Bourn couldn't argue that point. She did have unilateral authority on threat classification. She'd designed it that way, back when she trusted herself completely.

"One person with access to cosmic-level weapons," Patterson said, "operating in secret, answering to no oversight. Tell me, Director—how is that different from dictatorship?"

"Because I can be fired by the President. Tomorrow. With one signature."

"And if the President doesn't fire you? Then what? Who stops you?"

Silence. The question hung in the recycled air.

"Do you know how many defense initiatives get killed in committee?" Patterson leaned forward. "Budget fights? Authorization deadlocks? Meanwhile, CADENS gets blank checks, black sites, and carte blanche."

"Because we deal with threats you can't classify, understand, or contain through conventional means."

"Or because you've convinced every administration you're indispensable."

Bourn felt something shift in her chest. Not guilt, exactly. Recognition. Patterson wasn't wrong about everything.

"I'm recommending CADENS reform to Kain's transition team," Patterson said. "Oversight. Accountability. Transparency where possible."

"You'll compromise operational security—"

"I'll compromise your operational *autonomy*. There's a difference."

"Not when threats move faster than oversight committees."

"Then maybe CADENS needs to move slower. More carefully. More accountably."

"Or maybe Defense needs to accept that some threats require trust."

Patterson's expression went cold. "Trust is earned, Director. And you haven't earned mine."

The hologram flickered and died. Patterson disappeared, leaving Bourn alone in a room designed for secrets.

She stood in the dark for a long moment.

*Is he wrong?*

CADENS did operate with no oversight except Presidential. She did have unilateral authority. And if Kain became President—a man who'd integrated a cosmic artifact into his chest and become a kaiju, who'd caused forty-seven deaths and blamed the victims—he would control everything she'd built.

The system she designed to prevent catastrophe could be weaponized by whoever controlled the Presidency.

She'd made CADENS powerful to protect America. What if that power was turned against it?

Bourn pulled up a display—CADENS operations, enhanced individuals program, translocation tech, cataclysm research. All of it accessible to the President. All of it could be used for oppression, surveillance, control.

She'd designed it for defense. Kain could use it for offense.

And she couldn't refuse Presidential orders without violating the CADENS charter. Without becoming exactly what Patterson accused her of being—an unaccountable dictator.

The cage became visible. Different from the one holding Ahdia Bacchus. Same bars.

## Scene 2: The Alley

Victor had been doing this work for fifteen years.

Community organizing, mutual aid, showing up when showing up mattered. He'd faced cops, fascists, corporate security. He'd seen friends arrested, neighbors evicted, small victories erased by larger defeats—and kept going. Setbacks didn't stop the work. The work was what you did instead of drowning.

Tonight felt different. Wrong in a way he couldn't name.

The streets were quieter than usual for a Thursday. Shops closing early, metal gates rattling down. People walking faster, heads down. A woman crossed the street to avoid passing him on the sidewalk—actually crossed the street, like he was a threat.

He was wearing civilian clothes. Jeans, hoodie, nothing that said "vigilante." Just a Black man walking through his own neighborhood, the same neighborhood where he'd organized food drives and voter registration and community defense trainings.

That used to be enough.

*Something's off. The air feels thick. Heavy.*

His earpiece crackled.

"Victor, you're near Fifth and Morrison." Ruth's voice, steady as always. "We're getting reports of a disturbance—sounds like a mugging in progress."

"On it. Two blocks out."

He picked up his pace. This was the work. Someone needed help. You showed up.

The alley behind the bodega was dark, lit only by a flickering security light that probably hadn't been serviced since the Bush administration. The first Bush. Victor saw the shapes before he heard the voices—two figures, one pressed against the wall, one looming.

"Hey." He stepped into the alley, keeping his voice calm. "Everything okay here?"

The looming figure turned. Young guy, maybe twenty, face twisted with something that looked like fear pretending to be anger. Victor knew the look—had seen it on a hundred faces at protests, at community meetings, at funerals. Fear that had nowhere to go except out.

"Mind your business."

"I'm making it my business." Victor kept his hands visible, non-threatening. "You okay back there?"

The person against the wall—a woman, older, clutching her purse like it contained her whole life—nodded frantically.

"Why don't you step back and let her go."

The young man's hand twitched toward his pocket. Could be a weapon, could be nothing. Victor tracked the movement, calculating. He'd de-escalated a hundred situations like this. Read the body language, find the fear underneath the aggression, give them a way out that didn't require violence.

"Who the hell are you?" The young man's voice was rising. "Some kind of hero?"

"Just someone who lives here. Same as you."

Something shifted in the young man's eyes. The fear-anger flickered, and for a second Victor thought he'd gotten through—

Then the woman screamed.

Not at the young man. At Victor.

"That's one of them! The vigilantes! He's one of them!"

Victor's blood went cold.

"Ma'am, I'm trying to help you—"

"Help?" She was backing away now, pressing herself against the wall like he was the threat. "You people are the problem! You brought this on us! The riots, the violence—"

"I wasn't at the—"

But she wasn't listening. She was screaming, and now there were other sounds—footsteps, voices, people emerging from doorways. The young man had vanished, forgotten. The woman kept screaming.

"Is that Crimson Sable?"

"The vigilante? Get him!"

Victor's training kicked in. Count the threats. Find the exits. Don't engage civilians.

Except the civilians were engaging him.

A man grabbed his arm—shop owner, Victor realized, someone he'd helped with a permits issue last year. Victor twisted free gently, no force, just redirection. But the man stumbled, and someone else shouted "He's attacking us!"

More hands. More bodies. The crowd was maybe fifteen people now, faces he might have recognized last week. Neighbors. Shop owners. Parents from the community center where he'd taught de-escalation workshops.

All of them looking at him like he was the enemy.

*This isn't right. These people know me. I've helped them.*

Someone threw a punch. Victor ducked, but another fist caught his shoulder. He could fight back—his training was better than theirs—but these weren't enemies. These were scared people, and hitting them would make everything worse.

"Ruth, I need extraction." He kept his voice level, which took more effort than blocking punches. "Fifth and Morrison, alley behind the bodega. Civilians have turned—it's like they're not themselves."

"Turned? What do you mean *turned*?"

"I mean they're trying to kill me and I don't think they know why."

A bottle shattered against the wall near his head. The crowd was growing—twenty people now, maybe more. Victor backed toward the alley's dead end, which was exactly the wrong direction, but the street was blocked by bodies.

*Fifteen years. Fifteen years of building trust. And they're looking at me like I'm a monster.*

He saw the knife a second before it would have opened his throat.

Grabbed the wrist, redirected, watched the blade clatter away. The attacker's face was blank with terror—not anger, Victor realized. Fear. They were all afraid. Desperately, irrationally afraid.

Of him.

"Something's amplifying their fear," he said into comms. "This isn't natural."

"Leah's en route. Two minutes."

"I might not have two minutes."

The fire escape to his left was rusted but climbable. He'd have to go through the crowd to reach it—and they'd have to choose not to follow.

Victor ran.

Three steps, four, feeling hands grab at his jacket. He jumped, caught the ladder, hauled himself up as fingers scraped at his ankles. The rust bit into his palms. He climbed.

Below, the crowd milled, confused. Whatever had gripped them was fading—he could see it in their faces, the fear flickering to bewilderment. They looked at each other, at their own hands, at the blood on Victor's jacket.

"What... what happened?"

Victor reached the rooftop. Sat down hard. His hands were shaking.

Fifteen years of building trust. One night of whatever that was.

And he hadn't even done anything wrong.

"I'm clear," he said into comms. "Rooftop on Morrison. But Ruth—we need to talk about what just happened. Something turned those people. Something made them afraid of me."

"Come in. We'll debrief."

Victor looked down at the street. The crowd was dispersing, people wandering away like sleepwalkers waking up. None of them would remember why they'd attacked him. None of them would understand.

But he would remember their faces. Every single one.

## Scene 3: Neutral Ground

Ruth had been to CADENS headquarters exactly once—the day they'd recruited Firas, back when everything was simpler. Back when he was alive.

This wasn't headquarters. This was a satellite facility, a converted warehouse on the edge of the industrial district. "Neutral ground," Director Bourn had called it in her message.

Ruth didn't believe in neutral ground. Every space belonged to someone.

The door was guarded by a single agent who checked her credentials without speaking. Inside, the warehouse had been converted into something that looked almost cozy—leather couches, a coffee station with an espresso machine, soft lighting that probably cost more than Ruth's monthly rent. It looked like someone's idea of what a spy would want for a private meeting. Spy aesthetic. Safe house chic.

Director Bourn was already there, standing by the coffee station like she'd been waiting. Older than Ruth expected from her file—late fifties, silver hair cut short, the kind of stillness that came from decades of power. The stillness of someone who didn't need to move to get what she wanted.

"Dr. Carter." Warm voice. Practiced warmth. "Thank you for coming."

"Your message said it was urgent."

"Please, sit. Coffee? It's better than it looks."

Ruth sat. Didn't take the coffee. Watched Bourn pour herself a cup and settle into the chair opposite with the ease of someone who'd had a thousand conversations in rooms like this.

"I'll get straight to the point," Bourn said. "I've been following your team's work. Impressive, given your resources."

"We manage."

"You do more than manage. You've built something real. A team that trusts each other, that shows up, that does the work." Bourn sipped her coffee. "That's rare."

Ruth waited. Compliments from people like Bourn always had a second half.

"I also know you're carrying a load that would break most people. Team leader. Medical officer. Primary treatment provider for your temporalist. Grief counselor, apparently, based on what I've observed."

Ruth went still. "You've been observing us?"

"CADENS monitors enhanced individuals. It's not surveillance—it's protection. For you as much as anyone."

"That's a distinction I'm not sure I buy."

Bourn smiled—small, acknowledging. "Good. You shouldn't buy it automatically. Question everything, Dr. Carter. That's what makes you valuable."

Ruth felt the manipulation happening—the flattery, the acknowledgment of her suspicion, the subtle framing of CADENS as ally rather than threat. She'd seen doctors do this with families. Build trust before delivering bad news.

"What do you want, Director?"

"I want to help. And I think you could use help."

"Help with what?"

"Everything." Bourn set down her cup. "Resources. Intel. Institutional backing. Your team is doing important work with no support, no budget, no protection. I can offer all three."

"In exchange for?"

"Coordination. Not control—I know the difference, and I suspect you do too. But coordination. Sharing information. Working together instead of at cross purposes."

Ruth's instincts were screaming warnings. This was recruitment. This was co-optation. This was exactly how institutions absorbed grassroots movements—offer help, create dependency, slowly turn "coordination" into control.

But something else was happening too. A tired part of her, the part that had been holding everything together for months, heard "help" and wanted to believe it.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why not approach the whole team?"

"Because you're the leader. And because..." Bourn paused. Set down her coffee. "Because I see something in you that I recognize. Someone who takes responsibility. Who can't stop taking responsibility, even when the weight is crushing."

That landed. Ruth felt it in her chest, right where she'd been carrying Firas's death, Geneva's death, Ahdia's slow decay.

"I've been that person for forty years," Bourn continued. "Leading CADENS, protecting people from threats they can't imagine. And I've learned something important: you can't do it alone. Not forever. The weight breaks you eventually, unless you let other people help carry it."

"Is this a recruitment pitch?"

"This is an offer. A partnership." Bourn leaned forward, and her face was different now—less practiced, more real. Or a better performance of real. Ruth couldn't tell anymore. "You keep your independence, your team, your methods. But you have CADENS behind you when you need it. Resources, intel, backup. No strings—or at least, strings you can see and cut if you don't like them."

Ruth wanted to say no. Wanted to walk out, maintain the clean separation between her team and the shadow agencies that had taken Firas into a singularity and never brought him back.

But she was so tired. And Bourn was right about the weight.

"I'll think about it."

Bourn nodded—not pushing, not triumphant. Just accepting. "That's all I ask. Here's my direct line." She handed Ruth a card. Plain, just a phone number. No name, no title. "No intermediaries. Call me when you're ready to talk more."

Ruth took it. Put it in her pocket.

She told herself she was keeping her options open. She told herself this didn't change anything.

Walking out of the warehouse, she felt something she hadn't felt in months: the faint, dangerous sensation of not being alone.

*Be careful*, a voice in her head said. It sounded like Firas. *Be careful what you reach for.*

But the card was already in her pocket. The door was already open.

And Ruth was so tired of carrying everything by herself.

## Scene 2: The Shift

The first few blocks felt almost normal.

Pedestrians recognized them, waved, took photos. A woman in a Geneva Windrow campaign shirt gave them a thumbs up. A kid asked Ben if he was really a sniper.

"Not tonight. Tonight I'm just walking."

# Chapter 6: Rest and Recovery

## Scene 2: Alone with Grief

Ben's apartment was everything a military man's home should be.

Clean lines, everything in its place, the kind of precision that came from decades of discipline. Too clean. Too precise. No mess meant no living.

The kitchen had a single plate, a single mug. There used to be two of everything. The living room couch had dust on the cushions—he never sat there anymore. Photos had been removed from the walls, but you could see the outlines in the dust. Rectangles of absence.

He stood in the doorway after the CADENS debrief, still in tactical gear, and didn't want to enter.

"Okay. I'm home."

No one answered. No one had answered in eight months.

He finally moved, dropped his gear by the door—not his usual precision—and went straight to the bedroom. Not to sleep. To work.

His desk was buried in files. Kain investigation. Tank Kain massacre evidence. Timeline charts. Connection maps. This was what he did instead of feeling anything.

He opened his laptop. Pulled up surveillance footage. Cross-referenced Kain campaign events with riot locations. Built the pattern, connected the dots, lost himself in the work.

The clock showed 11 PM. He'd been at this for three hours.

The coffee mug beside his laptop was cold. He'd made it this morning.

Work was safer than remembering. He'd learned that eight months ago, when work became the only thing between him and the kind of grief that swallowed people whole.

It had worked for seven months. It was starting to fail now.

He sorted through riot footage, looking for patterns, and a face caught his eye. Woman, mid-thirties, caught in mob panic. Nothing significant to the investigation. Just background.

But something about her expression—fear, confusion, trying to help someone else escape.

Sarah. Her name was Sarah.

His wife had had that same look. Eight months ago. The peaceful protest that turned violent. Agent provocateurs and something else—Eidolon, they were calling it now, though they hadn't known that then. She'd been there because she was always at protests. Activist. Community organizer. Couldn't stay quiet when people needed help.

She tried to de-escalate. Got caught in the stampede.

Died helping someone else escape.

And Ben had been across town on a Go Squad operation, saving strangers, while his wife died alone.

The woman on the screen wasn't Sarah. But she could have been. Should have been. Wasn't.

The laptop screen blurred. Tears he hadn't felt coming.

"Fuck."

He tried to keep working. Couldn't focus. Eight months of avoidance cracking all at once.

He got up. Paced. Military precision falling apart with every step.

In the kitchen, he opened a cabinet. Sarah's favorite tea was still there. He'd kept it because he couldn't use it and couldn't throw it away. Her mug, collecting dust beside it.

He touched the mug and broke down properly. Not crying—gasping. Drowning. Eight months of suppressed grief hitting all at once.

He slid down the cabinet, sitting on the kitchen floor in tactical gear, and let it happen.

The work wasn't working anymore. The avoidance had failed.

He knew, in that moment, what he'd been trying not to know. He wasn't investigating Kain to get justice. He was investigating to avoid feeling this. If he worked hard enough, built the case well enough, caught Kain... maybe Sarah's death would have meaning. Maybe it wouldn't have been random, senseless, preventable.

But it was random. Agent provocateur could have hit any protest. She was just there. Wrong place, wrong time.

No cosmic significance. No grand narrative. Just gone.

Ben's conservative ideology had always said the system worked if you worked hard enough. Follow the rules. Do the right thing. Trust the institutions.

The system had killed his wife. The institutions had failed. And he couldn't process that yet—that reckoning was still coming, months away—so he kept investigating, kept working, kept pretending it fixed something.

It didn't. It never had.

But it was all he knew how to do.

Still on the kitchen floor, grief subsiding to numbness, he pulled out his wallet. Military habit. Hidden compartment.

Photo of Sarah. Not the wedding photo—too formal. A candid shot. Her laughing.

That's what he missed most. Her laugh.

She'd been funny. Sharp. Politically active. They disagreed on almost everything—he was conservative, she was activist—but they talked. Really talked. Challenged each other.

She'd been changing him slowly, without either of them realizing it.

And then she was gone.

And he was stuck. Half-changed, half-conservative, fully broken.

Couldn't go back to who he was. She'd changed him too much.

Couldn't move forward. She wasn't there to show him how.

His phone buzzed. Ruth checking in—she did that, noticed who was struggling.

Text: *"You good? Haven't heard from you since debrief."*

Ben typed "I'm fine." Deleted it.

Typed "Just working." Deleted it.

Typed "Yeah, all good." Deleted it.

Finally sent: *"Processing. Talk tomorrow?"*

Ruth's response came quickly: *"Yeah. I'm here if you need."*

He sat on the kitchen floor with Sarah's photo. Two options: Keep working until he passed out—the usual method. Or actually process the grief.

He wasn't ready for option two.

He got up slowly. Put the photo back in his wallet. Returned to the desk. Opened the laptop.

Back to the Kain investigation. The familiar pain.

But something was different now. He knew it was avoidance. Knew it wouldn't work. Did it anyway because he didn't know what else to do.

Work as coping mechanism: failing.

But it was the only coping mechanism he had.

So he kept building the case, connecting dots, pretending it mattered while the screen reflected his face—exhausted, broken, pretending to function.

The system had to work. The evidence had to matter. Because if it didn't, if perfect evidence couldn't catch Kain, then Sarah's death was truly meaningless.

And Ben wasn't ready to accept that.

Not yet.

# Chapter 7: Concerns and Shared Trauma

Ahdia was on the couch, wearing the same clothes Ruth had seen her in three days ago. Hollow-eyed. Exhausted in a way that went beyond normal degradation.

"Hey." Ahdia attempted a casual wave. "Didn't expect company."

"Which is why I didn't call." Ruth set down her medical bag. "How are you doing?"

"Fine. Tired. The usual."

Ruth didn't sit. She stood, assessing. The medical part of her brain was already cataloging: pallor slightly off, micro-tremors in Ahdia's hands, the way she was positioning herself to minimize movement.

"Mind if I check your vitals?"

"What, no small talk first?" Ahdia tried for humor. "Usually doctors at least pretend to care about my weekend plans."

"I do care about your weekend plans. I also care about your cellular integrity. One of those I can measure."

Ahdia sighed but gestured toward the couch. "Fine. Do your thing."

Ruth sat beside her, pulling out her portable scanner—the same tech Ryu used, compact and civilian-approved. She ran it over Ahdia's arm, watching the numbers populate.

The readings made her frown.

"When was your last treatment?"

"Two days ago. Ryu came by."

That tracked with the equipment on the table. But the degradation numbers didn't. Two days post-treatment should show cellular repair still active, not this level of temporal stress.

"Have you been using your powers?"

"Just during the investigation. Chapter 3 stuff." Ahdia's eyes flickered away. "Nothing since."

Ruth filed that away. The numbers said otherwise. But she wasn't ready to push—not yet.

"How are you really doing?" She put away the scanner. "Not the medical answer. The real one."

Ahdia stared at the paused TV screen. Some streaming show Ruth didn't recognize.

"Depressed. Isolated. The usual." She shrugged. "Powers make everything complicated. Can't sleep right. Can't eat right. Can't be around people without exhausting myself. Standard temporal manipulation bullshit."

"And the mob attack?"

Silence.

"I saw the footage," Ahdia said quietly. "Leah got hurt."

"You hacked our patrol cams?"

"I have access through FAERIS." Ahdia finally looked at her. "I should have been there."

"Why weren't you?"

"Treatment. Knocked me out. I slept through—" She stopped. Started again. "I slept through the whole thing. Woke up to forty messages and a blood pressure I didn't want to measure."

Ruth heard the lie. Not a complete lie—depression could absolutely cause that kind of crash. But something was off about how Ahdia said it. Too practiced. Too smooth.

She let it go. For now.

"The team misses you."

"I know."

"We can't keep covering your absences forever."

"I know that too."

"Then what's the plan, Ahdia?"

Ahdia laughed, hollow. "Plan? Ruth, I'm barely functioning. I get up, I watch TV, I try not to use my powers for anything because every second I freeze costs me something. I don't have a plan. I have a slow degradation curve and a lot of streaming services."

Ruth felt the conversation shifting. Ahdia was being honest about the depression—that part wasn't an act. The exhaustion was real. The isolation was real.

But something else was happening too. Something Ruth couldn't quite name.

"I miss him," Ruth said.

Ahdia blinked. "What?"

"Firas. The mob attack..." Ruth heard her own voice crack slightly. "It reminded me of the night we lost him. Being helpless. Knowing I couldn't protect everyone."

"Ruth—"

"I'm the team leader now. I make the calls. I decide who goes where, who does what. And when they get hurt—when Leah's bleeding because I didn't pull out fast enough—that's on me."

Ahdia was quiet. Listening. Not trying to fix it.

"He was going to propose," Ruth said. "Did I tell you that? I found a ring. In his sock drawer. After."

"You didn't tell me."

"Couldn't. Can't, usually." Ruth stared at the floor. "But here, with you... I don't know. It feels safer."

"Because I'm not going to judge you for falling apart?"

"Because you already know what falling apart looks like."

Something passed between them. Two women who'd lost too much. Two friends carrying weight they couldn't share with anyone else.

"I think about my parents sometimes," Ahdia said quietly. "Montana. Before everything went wrong."

Ruth knew the story. Knew more than she'd ever told Ahdia she knew—CADENS files were thorough.

"Losing them made me stop trusting people," Ahdia continued. "Can't lose someone if you never let them close, right? Hermit mode. TV and isolation and pretending the world doesn't exist."

"But you let the team in."

"Did I?" Ahdia looked genuinely uncertain. "Sometimes I think I'm just... performing connection. Doing what a person who has friends would do. Faking it."

"That's depression talking."

"Is it? How do you tell the difference?"

Ruth didn't have an answer. She reached over and took Ahdia's hand instead.

"We're not performing," Ruth said. "This, right now? This is real. I came because I was worried. You're listening because you care. That's not fake."

Ahdia squeezed her hand. Said nothing.

"Whatever's going on with you," Ruth continued, "whatever's making those numbers worse than they should be—you can tell me. When you're ready."

Ahdia's jaw tightened. For a moment, Ruth thought she might actually open up.

Then: "It's just the depression. I promise."

Lie. Ruth felt it.

But she let it go.

"Okay." She released Ahdia's hand. "Since I'm here, want me to top off your treatment? Might as well take advantage of the house call."

Ahdia nodded, relief visible. "Yeah. That'd be good."

Ruth set up the portable synthesis equipment on the coffee table, same spot Ryu's had occupied. Prepared the injection with practiced efficiency.

Ahdia rolled up her sleeve. The injection site was familiar, slightly bruised from recent treatments.

The cold spread first, then warmth. Cellular repair accelerating. Ruth watched the readout improve, but not as much as it should have.

"I'm here," Ruth said as she worked. "Any time. Day or night. You call, I come."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you didn't call after the mob attack. You didn't answer when I called."

"I was—"

"Sleeping. I know." Ruth met her eyes. "Just... reach out, okay? Before it gets worse. Not after."

Ahdia nodded. Something that might have been guilt flickered across her face.

"I promise."

Another lie. Ruth heard it clearly this time.

She finished the treatment, packed up her equipment, stood to leave. At the door, she turned back.

"I love you, you know. Not romantically. But you're important to me. You matter."

Ahdia's expression cracked, just for a moment. Real emotion underneath all the deflection.

"You matter to me too."

Ruth left.

In the hallway, she let her professional mask drop. The numbers didn't add up. The evasions were too practiced. Something was happening with Ahdia that went beyond depression.

She'd check with Ryu tomorrow. Compare notes.

For now, she'd trust. But she'd verify.

## Scene 2: Ruth Runs

Ruth hit the fire escape at a dead sprint.

Three floors up, she vaulted the railing and caught the drainage pipe, swinging her momentum into a wall run that carried her to the adjacent rooftop. Her body knew this route—had mapped it years ago when she first moved to the city, back when running was about joy instead of escape.

Tonight it was both.

*The numbers didn't add up.*

She cleared the gap between buildings without breaking stride, rolling through the landing into her next step. Two days post-treatment, degradation that high—Ahdia was either burning through power she wasn't admitting to, or something else entirely was happening.

*"It's just the depression. I promise."*

Ruth heard the lie. Clear as the city lights spreading below her.

She reached the water tower and climbed it without thinking, hands finding familiar grips. At the top, she paused—lungs burning, heart pounding, the good kind of pain that reminded her she was alive.

What was she supposed to do? Interrogate Ahdia? Demand answers? She wasn't a patient. She was a friend. And friends got to have secrets, even stupid ones.

Even dangerous ones.

Ruth dropped from the tower, caught the ladder halfway down, used it to redirect into a precision jump to the next building's ledge. Her fingers found the concrete lip and she mantled up smoothly, muscle memory taking over while her mind kept spinning.

The empty apartment waited for her. The one Firas used to fill with his presence, his laugh, his terrible cooking experiments that somehow always tasted better than they looked. Now it was just Ruth and the silence and the ring she'd found in his sock drawer.

*He was going to propose.*

She ran harder. Longer strides, bigger gaps, the kind of risks Firas would have scolded her for. *"Calculated risks, Ruth. Not emotional ones."*

But Firas wasn't here to scold her anymore.

A chimney stack ahead—she planted her foot and launched over it, twisting mid-air to spot her landing. The rooftop garden on the other side was soft under her shoes. Someone's tomato plants, thriving despite the altitude. Life finding a way.

She was team leader now. Medical officer. Treatment coordinator. Grief counselor, apparently. Every role piling up, none of them fitting right. She carried them all across these rooftops, weight she couldn't put down.

*Why couldn't I save him?*

The thought hit mid-stride and she stumbled. Caught herself on a ventilation unit, breathing hard. The city sprawled indifferent below—cars and lights and people who didn't know that somewhere above them, a woman was running from questions she couldn't answer.

Ahdia was lying about something. Ruth could feel it the same way she felt a bad landing coming—in her bones, before her brain caught up. But pressing wouldn't help. Would just make her retreat further into that penthouse, that TV, that isolation.

Ruth straightened. Rolled her shoulders. Found her center again.

She had three more miles to home. Three more miles of rooftops and fire escapes and the particular freedom that came from moving through space on her own terms.

So Ruth would wait. Watch. Be there when Ahdia was ready to talk.

That's what you did for people you loved.

You stayed.

Even when they lied.

She ran.

# Chapter 8: The First Line Crossed

## Scene 1: Routine Treatment

The doorbell rang at 9 AM, exactly when it always did.

Ahdia pulled herself off the couch—same couch, same position, same pattern of indentation in the cushions from days of not moving. The apartment had accumulated a new layer of chaos since Ruth's visit: more takeout containers, more streaming queues paused mid-episode, more evidence of a person not quite coping.

She opened the door. Dr. Ryu Matsuda stood in the hallway with his medical case, CADENS credentials visible on his lanyard. Same concerned expression he always wore. Same careful posture that suggested he was already cataloging her condition before she said a word.

"Ms. Bacchus."

"You can still call me Ahdia. We've been doing this for months."

"Ahdia." He smiled—small, careful, professionally appropriate. "How are you feeling?"

"Like I've been watching TV for a week straight and forgot what sunlight looks like." She stepped aside to let him in. "So, normal."

Ryu set up his equipment on the coffee table—same spot Ruth had used, same spot his equipment always occupied. The routine was familiar now: scanner, synthesis device, vials that glowed in colors she'd stopped asking about.

"Treatment first," he said, "then we can discuss your numbers."

Ahdia settled onto the couch, rolling up her sleeve without being asked. The injection site was a constellation of small bruises—too many treatments, too close together, her body keeping score even when her mind tried not to.

The scanner hummed as he ran it over her arm. She watched his face, looking for the micro-expressions that would tell her how bad the news was.

"Seventy-three percent," he said. "Stable from last week."

"That's good, right?"

"It's not declining. Given your baseline trajectory, that's significant." He prepared the injection with practiced efficiency. "Dr. Carter's synthesis compound is working."

Ruth. Keeping her alive. Again.

The needle entered her arm. Cold first, spreading through her bloodstream like ice water. Then warmth—the cellular repair activating, her body recognizing the treatment and responding. Then nothing. Just the familiar numbness of being kept human by science she barely understood.

"Twenty minutes for full integration," Ryu said, monitoring the readout. "You know the routine."

She did. Sit still, let the treatment work, don't use powers for at least an hour afterward. The rules of her existence, handed to her by doctors who cared whether she lived or died.

Ryu settled into the chair across from her, waiting out the integration period. This was when he usually made small talk—weather, city news, the particular brand of nothing that filled the space between medical procedures.

Today he hesitated.

"Something interesting came through this week," he said. "CADENS intelligence intercepts."

Ahdia looked at him. He didn't usually share work details.

"Not classified exactly, but..." He adjusted his position, casual in a way that seemed rehearsed. "Frustrating. Bureaucratically frustrating."

"What kind of intercept?"

"Arms shipment. Eastern Europe." He said it like he was complaining about traffic. "Routing through Moldova, destination unclear, but the intelligence suggests ethnic militia groups. Pattern matches previous genocides in the region."

Ahdia felt something shift in her chest. The Hyper Seed, cold and heavy, responding to information it recognized as important.

"Genocides?"

"The region has history. Ethnic tensions, territorial disputes, the kind of powder keg that needs a single match." Ryu's tone remained academic. "CADENS flagged it because of the scale—enough weapons to arm several thousand fighters. Enough to destabilize the entire region."

"And nobody's stopping it?"

"Outside CADENS jurisdiction. Local authorities either compromised or complicit. International bodies moving at international body speed, which means—"

"They'll get there after everyone's dead."

"That's the frustrating part." He sighed. "We have intelligence. We have capabilities. We have authorization gaps that mean we watch instead of act."

Ahdia's brain was doing math she didn't want it to do. Several thousand fighters. Ethnic conflict. History of genocide. The numbers translated automatically into potential casualties—thousands, maybe tens of thousands, depending on how bad it got.

"When?" she asked. "The shipment."

"Forty-eight hours. Transfer point in a rural area, minimal security, maximum deniability." Ryu checked the treatment readout. "Your integration is proceeding normally."

He'd changed the subject. She noticed.

"Do you have coordinates?"

Ryu looked at her. Something passed across his face—conflict, calculation, a decision being made in real time.

"Why do you ask?"

"Just curious." The lie came easily. Too easily. "Academic interest."

"Academic interest in translocation coordinates for an arms shipment."

"I watch a lot of news." Another lie. "Want to follow the story."

Ryu was quiet for a long moment. The treatment readout beeped softly, numbers climbing as her cells repaired.

"Before I answer that," he said, "I should mention something else. CADENS facility news."

The subject change was obvious. Ahdia let him deflect, curious where he was going.

"Southwest lab got requisitioned this week. Government project, everyone reassigned or loaned out."

"What kind of project?"

"Fusion energy research. Stellarator prototype." He said it like it was office gossip. "The President-elect's big initiative."

"Kain?"

"American Energy Independence Act. Announced it yesterday—artificial sun, American jobs, energy dominance."

Ahdia processed this. Kain backing science felt wrong, like watching a cat suddenly advocate for dogs.

"Fusion is real science, right? Not a scam?"

"Very real. Same process that powers stars—hydrogen atoms fusing into helium, releasing enormous energy. A hundred million degrees, magnetic containment, controlled sun in a bottle."

"That sounds..." She searched for the word. "Dangerous? Exciting? Both?"

"Both." Ryu's expression was complicated. "Fusion would be transformative. Clean energy, essentially unlimited. The kind of technology that advances civilization by orders of magnitude."

"So why do you look worried?"

"Because the timeline is wrong. Scientists estimate three years minimum for safe prototype development. Kain promised ten months."

"And CADENS has to help?"

"Government pressure after the authorization delays in Book—" He stopped, corrected himself. "After last year's incidents. We're required to collaborate."

Ahdia thought about this. Kain rushing dangerous technology for political points. Scientists saying it couldn't be done safely. CADENS forced to participate anyway.

"You know about the Kardashev Scale?" Ryu asked.

"The what?"

"Framework for measuring civilizational advancement. Type I civilization harnesses all energy available on their planet. Type II harnesses their star's full output—Dyson spheres, stellar engineering. Type III harnesses an entire galaxy."

"And we're..."

"Type 0.72. Not even Type I yet." He smiled slightly. "Cosmic toddlers, comparatively. Fusion would push us toward 0.8, maybe 0.9. Still far from planetary-scale, but significant progress."

"So fusion is good."

"The technology is good. The implementation..." He shook his head. "Kain's rushing it for campaign promises. Safety protocols waived, environmental protections ignored. Good technology, dangerous approach."

"Both at once."

"Exactly." Ryu checked the readout again. "Treatment complete. Your numbers look stable."

He began packing his equipment, and Ahdia felt the moment slipping away. The arms shipment. The coordinates. The thousands of people who might die while bureaucracies moved at bureaucratic speed.

"Ryu."

He paused.

"The coordinates. For the Moldova shipment."

"Ahdia—"

"I'm not asking you to do anything. Just... information. That's all."

She watched him wrestle with it. The professional ethics he'd spent years building. The boundaries that kept him employed, credentialed, trusted. All of it pressing against something else—something she'd noticed in the way he looked at her, the way he always came when she needed him, the way he couldn't quite say no.

"This crosses lines," he said quietly.

"I know."

"If anyone found out—"

"They won't."

Long silence. The treatment equipment sat half-packed on the coffee table. Outside, the city hummed with ordinary life—people going to work, getting coffee, living in a world that didn't know about arms shipments and genocide planning and the particular weight of power unused.

"I'll send them tonight," Ryu said. "After I verify the latest intelligence."

Something released in Ahdia's chest. Relief. Guilt. The particular vertigo of a line being crossed.

"Thank you."

"Be careful." He finished packing, stood to leave. "If you decide to investigate."

They both knew she wouldn't be investigating. They both pretended otherwise.

"I'll see you at the next scheduled treatment," he said at the door. "Two weeks."

"Two weeks."

He left. The door clicked shut. Ahdia sat alone with the information settling into her mind like a stone into water—ripples spreading, changing everything it touched.

Forty-eight hours. Moldova. Thousands of lives.

She could stop it.

She was probably the only one who could.

The justification was already forming, smooth and logical: CADENS couldn't act, international bodies were too slow, local authorities were compromised. But she had powers that stopped time, that let her be anywhere instantly, that made walls and borders and authorization gaps irrelevant.

With great power comes great responsibility. The words felt like a joke and a truth simultaneously. TV philosophy applied to real genocide prevention.

Her phone buzzed. Message from Ryu: coordinates attached, intelligence summary, FAERIS access codes for international translocation.

Everything she needed. Handed to her by someone who should have said no.

She opened the file.

## Scene 2: The Aftermath

Forty-eight hours later, Ahdia materialized in her apartment and collapsed.

The translocation had taken everything she had left. One second in Moldova, frozen time crystallizing around her as she worked—then the nauseating lurch of long-distance jump, eight thousand miles folded into a single step, her cells screaming in protest.

She hit the floor. Couldn't make it to the couch. Just lay there on the hardwood, trembling, watching her HUD flicker with numbers she didn't want to read.

Fifty-two percent. She'd been at seventy-three two days ago.

Twenty-one percent burned in a single operation.

But the operation had worked. That was the thing she kept telling herself as her body refused to move. The arms shipment was scattered across three countries now, documents exposed to local journalists, leaders confronted with evidence of their complicity. The militia groups were fighting each other instead of civilians. The genocide had been prevented.

Eight thousand lives. Maybe more. The math was imprecise but the direction was clear.

Her phone showed seventeen missed calls. Ruth (4), team group chat (23 messages), Ryu (3). All wondering where she was. All getting the same silence she'd been giving them for days.

The doorbell rang.

She couldn't get up. Couldn't move. Just lay there as the buzzing repeated, as keys turned in locks she'd given out months ago for exactly this situation, as footsteps approached.

"Ahdia."

Ryu's voice. Of course it was Ryu. He'd known she would go. He'd known what it would cost. He'd come prepared.

"Can't move," she managed.

He was beside her in seconds, scanner out, medical bag opening. The professional mask was intact but his hands were shaking—just slightly, just enough to notice.

"Cellular integrity at fifty-two percent." His voice was carefully flat. "You burned twenty-one points in—"

"Forty-eight hours. I know."

"This is critical range. Another five percent and we're looking at permanent degradation."

"I know."

He set up the emergency synthesis equipment right there on the floor, not bothering to move her to the couch. Stronger compound than usual—she could tell by the color, by the way the vial glowed with an urgency the standard treatment didn't have.

The injection felt different this time. Colder. Sharper. Her cells responding with something like desperation, like drowning and suddenly finding air.

"Don't talk," Ryu said. "Let the treatment work."

She didn't talk. Just lay there as the numbers slowly climbed—fifty-three, fifty-four, fifty-five. Still critical. Still dangerous. But not dying.

The news played on the TV she'd left running. Arms deal mysteriously falls through. Diplomatic crisis averted in Eastern Europe. Ethnic tensions cooling after surprise exposure of militia funding.

No mention of enhanced individuals. No attribution. Perfect intervention.

"It worked," she said when she could speak again.

"I can see that."

"Eight thousand people. Maybe more."

"I can see that too."

Ryu's voice was tight. He wasn't looking at her—just at the readout, at the numbers climbing too slowly, at the evidence of what she'd done to herself.

"I'm going to falsify this treatment in the records," he said quietly.

Ahdia turned her head. "What?"

"Ruth monitors your baseline. If she sees a twenty-one point drop and emergency synthesis, she'll ask questions. Questions we can't answer."

"Ryu—"

"I'll log it as routine treatment. Normal degradation rate. Compound adjustment for optimization."

"That's—"

"Lying. Yes." He finally looked at her. "We're already lying. This is just maintaining the lie."

She should argue. Should tell him not to risk his career, his ethics, his relationship with Ruth. Should push back against the conspiracy forming between them.

She didn't.

"Okay," she said.

The word hung in the air. First explicit agreement. First acknowledgment that they were in this together now—whatever "this" was.

Ryu finished the treatment in silence. Packed his equipment. Stood to leave.

At the door, he paused.

"Another intercept came through today."

Ahdia's exhausted attention sharpened.

"Humanitarian crisis forming. Different region. I don't have details yet."

"When you do—"

"I'll let you know."

He left.

The pattern was established. Neither of them had said it out loud, but they both knew: this would happen again. Intel would flow. Coordinates would follow. She would intervene. He would treat. Records would be falsified.

Months of this. Maybe longer.

Ahdia lay on her apartment floor, cells slowly repairing, and told herself the math made sense. Twenty-one percent degradation for eight thousand lives. Less than a thousandth of a percent per person saved.

The numbers justified everything.

They had to.

## Scene 3: Justification

By evening, she could sit up.

The treatment had stabilized her at sixty-one percent—not recovered, but not critical. Ryu's emergency compound had bought her time. Time she would spend burning through the next crisis, the next intervention, the next necessary sacrifice.

The TV still showed news coverage. Analysts discussing the mysterious collapse of the arms deal. Experts speculating about intelligence leaks, insider betrayals, the kind of explanations that made sense to people who didn't know about temporal manipulation and cosmic artifacts.

She watched their confusion with something like satisfaction. They'd never know. That was the point.

Her phone buzzed. Ruth: *Checking in—everything okay? Haven't heard from you in days.*

Ahdia typed: *Rough week. Depression bad. Sorry for the silence.*

Not entirely a lie. The depression was real. Had been real since long before the powers, long before the Hyper Seed, long before her life became a series of impossible choices. She just wasn't mentioning the other part—the part where she'd burned twenty-one percent of her cellular integrity preventing a genocide.

Ruth responded: *I'm here if you need anything. Always.*

Guilt twisted in her chest. Ruth. Keeping her alive. Trusting her completely. Being lied to with every message.

*Thanks*, Ahdia typed back. *See you at next team meeting?*

*Wouldn't miss it. Take care of yourself.*

She set down the phone. The lies were accumulating now—each one building on the last, each one necessary to protect the next operation, each one eroding something she couldn't name.

But eight thousand people were alive who would have been dead.

That had to matter. That had to be worth the cost.

Her HUD displayed the intelligence summary Ryu had sent. The Moldova operation was complete, but the file had footnotes—other intercepts, other crises, other places where people would die unless someone intervened.

She scrolled through them. Sudan. Myanmar. Venezuela. Smaller operations, some of them. Targeted interventions. The kind of work that CADENS couldn't authorize and international bodies couldn't reach and local powers wouldn't touch.

The kind of work only she could do.

The justification solidified as she read. This was what powers were for. This was what the Hyper Seed had made possible. Not fighting supervillains or stopping bank robberies—those were comic book problems, small-scale, manageable. This was real. This was thousands of lives at a time. This was the difference between watching history happen and changing it.

She thought about the fusion subplot—Kain rushing dangerous technology for political points, scientists warning about consequences, corners cut for timelines that couldn't be met. Different scale, same pattern. Both of them operating beyond sustainable limits, justifying risk with scale of outcome.

She didn't see the parallel.

Couldn't see it, maybe. The hero complex was too strong, the math too convincing, the alternative too unbearable.

Let people die when she could stop it? That wasn't a choice. That was cowardice dressed up as wisdom.

Her phone buzzed again. Ryu: *New intercept verified. Details tomorrow.*

The cycle was already starting again.

Ahdia looked at the message, at the news coverage, at her own reflection in the darkened window. Exhausted. Hollow-eyed. Burning through her humanity to save people who would never know her name.

This was what heroes did.

This was what she'd become.

She told herself it was worth it.

She had to.

# Chapter 9: Building the Case

## Scene 1: The Footage

Two in the morning. Ben's apartment looked like a crime scene.

Files covered every horizontal surface—coffee table, kitchen counter, the floor around his desk. Three monitors glowed in the darkness: primary display frozen on a grainy protest video, secondary showing police dispatch logs, tertiary running financial tracking software he'd borrowed from his FBI days.

He hadn't turned on the lights. Didn't need them. The screens provided enough illumination to work, and darkness felt appropriate for the kind of evidence he was reviewing.

Tank Kain massacre. Six months ago. Fresh enough that the country still remembered, distant enough that most people had moved on to the next outrage.

Kain was running for President now. "Tough on crime," the campaign slogan said. "Hard choices for hard times." The massacre footage played in every campaign ad—edited carefully, of course, to show only Kain's response. The armored vehicle. The decisive action. The candidate who didn't flinch when violence erupted.

The footage Ben was watching showed something different.

He dragged the playhead back to 13:47. Again. For the forty-third time, if he was counting correctly. The protest had been peaceful until this moment—signs, chants, organized march along the permitted route. Standard demonstration, the kind that happened every weekend somewhere in America.

At 13:47, violence erupted.

Ben isolated the frame and zoomed in. There—moving through the crowd—a group of six individuals. They wore protest gear. Black hoodies, face coverings, the kind of anonymizing uniform that real protesters sometimes adopted.

But their body language was wrong.

He'd spent twenty years reading people. FBI training, military intelligence before that. Body language didn't lie the way words did. These six weren't protesters. They moved with purpose. Tactical positioning. Coordinated sight lines.

Operators.

At 13:48, one of them threw the first object. Rock. Hit an officer's shield. At 13:49, another started a fire in a trash can. At 13:51, the third group member attacked a police line from behind.

Each action calculated. Each escalation precisely timed.

Ben had found them in multiple camera angles now—news crews, security footage, civilian phones that had been uploaded to social media before anyone realized their evidentiary value. The same six individuals, starting violence, then fading back into the crowd to let chaos spread.

Agent provocateurs. Textbook tactics.

Plant operatives. Start violence. Blame the target group. Justify response.

He pulled up another window: traffic camera feeds from the surrounding area. The armored vehicle that became Kain's campaign footage had been positioned two blocks from the protest site.

It had arrived forty minutes before the march began.

Forty minutes before violence erupted.

Kain's team had positioned for response before there was anything to respond to.

Ben's hands were steady. Years of training kept them that way. But something cold was growing in his chest—the particular weight of seeing a pattern he couldn't unsee.

Fourteen people had died. Forty-seven injured. Mostly protesters. Some police. Some bystanders who'd just been walking by when the world exploded.

Names scrolling on his screen. Faces. Maria Gutierrez, 34, community organizer. James Chen, 19, college sophomore. Denise Washington, 67, retired teacher.

Not rioters. Not violent. Just people exercising their constitutional rights who happened to be in the blast radius when someone decided their deaths would make good campaign footage.

Sarah's name wasn't on this list.

Different protest. Different city. Different day.

Same tactics.

Ben closed his eyes. Opened them. Focused on the evidence.

Work was safer than remembering.

## Scene 2: The FBI Contact

The Caledonia FBI field office looked exactly like every other federal facility Ben had ever worked in. Fluorescent lights, government-issue furniture, the particular institutional smell of floor wax and coffee that had been sitting too long.

He'd worn a suit. First time in months. The briefcase in his hand contained everything—printouts, USB drives, timeline documents, his comprehensive analysis.

Agent Marcus Webb met him in the lobby. Former colleague, still active duty. They'd worked cases together back when Ben was stationed in Caledonia, before Sarah, before everything changed.

"Ben." Webb shook his hand, grip firm but careful. "You look like shit."

"Thanks. I've been sleeping great."

"I can tell." Webb gestured toward the elevator. "Conference room six. Pulled the morning clear when you called."

They rode up in silence. The building felt familiar and foreign simultaneously—like visiting a house you used to live in after someone else had repainted it.

Conference room six had windows overlooking the city. Ben set up his materials while Webb poured coffee neither of them would drink.

"Tank Kain massacre," Ben said. "I've been reviewing the footage."

"Lot of people have reviewed that footage."

"Not like this." Ben pulled up the first video on the room's display screen. "Watch the crowd at 13:47. These six individuals."

Webb watched. His expression didn't change, but Ben knew him well enough to read the subtle shift—the way his attention sharpened, the micro-adjustment in his posture that meant he was actually seeing what Ben was showing him.

"Body language is wrong," Webb said.

"Operators. Moving tactical." Ben advanced the video frame by frame. "First violence starts here. Then here. Then here. Each escalation precisely timed. Coordinated sight lines, professional execution."

"Agent provocateurs."

"Textbook." Ben pulled up the traffic camera data. "Kain's armored vehicle was positioned two blocks away. Arrived forty minutes before the protest started."

Webb was quiet for a long moment. His coffee sat untouched.

"This is solid work, Ben."

"There's more." Financial records now—shell company analysis, payment timelines. "Security firm called TRIOMF Solutions. Large payments from Kain's campaign every month. Vague service descriptions—'event security,' 'risk assessment.' Payments spike before each incident."

"Each incident?"

"Seven riots in the past year. All in cities where Kain made campaign stops. All following the same pattern—peaceful protest, sudden violence, Kain gets his 'decisive response' footage. Polling numbers go up every time."

Webb ran a hand over his face. The gesture was tired. Older than Ben remembered him being.

"You understand what you're bringing me."

"Evidence of systematic campaign violence orchestrated by a presidential candidate."

"Evidence that, if pursued, puts us in direct conflict with one of the most powerful political operations in the country."

"The evidence is solid, Marcus. The analysis holds up."

"I'm not questioning your analysis." Webb leaned forward. "I'm questioning whether you understand what happens next. This isn't a normal case. Presidential campaigns have protection most investigations don't have to deal with. Political pressure. Media attention. Legal teams with unlimited resources."

"So we let him get away with it?"

"I'm not saying that." Webb's voice was careful. "I'm saying authorization for this kind of investigation comes from the Director's office. Possibly DOJ. That's weeks of bureaucratic process, minimum. And every step of that process, there will be people trying to shut it down."

Ben felt the frustration rising. Pushed it back down. Webb wasn't the enemy. Webb was doing exactly what a professional investigator should do—laying out the obstacles, making sure Ben understood the terrain.

"I'll wait for authorization," Ben said. "Follow proper procedure. That's how the system works."

Something flickered across Webb's face. Not quite skepticism—more like the particular expression of someone who'd seen too many proper procedures lead nowhere.

"I'll bring this to my supervisors. Start the preliminary review process." Webb gathered the materials Ben had spread across the table. "You've done good work here, Ben. Thorough. Professional."

"But?"

"But I've seen investigations like this before. Good cases. Solid evidence. The kind of work that should lead to indictments." Webb paused at the door. "Sometimes it does. Sometimes political pressure buries it so deep no one ever sees it again. I'm not telling you not to pursue this. I'm telling you to go in with your eyes open."

Ben nodded. The weight in his chest hadn't lifted.

"Sarah would have understood," he said quietly. "Why I have to do this."

Webb's expression softened. The professional mask slipping for just a moment.

"Yeah. She would have."

He left. Ben sat alone in the conference room, evidence scattered around him like fragments of a bomb that hadn't gone off yet.

The system would work. Evidence mattered. That was what he had to believe.

Anything else was unbearable.

## Scene 3: Expanded Investigation

One week later. Ben's apartment had transformed from crime scene to something worse—the particular organized chaos of obsession.

Files sorted by date, color-coded by category. Wall covered in photos and timeline markers, connected by string like a movie detective's conspiracy board because the cliché existed for a reason: it worked.

Seven riots. Seven cities. Seven instances of the same pattern.

Portland. Minneapolis. Atlanta. Phoenix. Detroit. Denver. Chicago.

Each one preceded by TRIOMF payments. Each one featuring the same tactical signatures—agent provocateurs, coordinated violence, Kain campaign response teams already positioned before chaos erupted.

He'd mapped the financial trail through CADENS databases. His clearance still worked, even in semi-retirement. Enhanced access that let him follow money through shell companies, offshore registrations, the particular labyrinth of corporate structures designed to hide ownership.

TRIOMF Solutions. Owned by a holding company. Owned by another holding company. Owned by—

The trail got murky there. Deliberately obscured. But the payments were clear, and the timing was undeniable.

Violence follows Kain. Every time.

The fusion project had been a surprise.

Ben noticed it while reviewing TRIOMF expenditures, looking for more riot-funding patterns. A new payment series, starting three weeks ago. Different scale—construction costs, equipment purchases, facility acquisition.

Southwest lab. Stellarator prototype. American Energy Independence Act.

Kain's campaign promise: artificial sun, American jobs, clean energy dominance. Announced the same week the payments started.

Ben pulled construction permits. Environmental reviews. Safety inspection schedules.

Permits rushed through in weeks instead of months. Environmental impact statements incomplete, sections waived entirely. Safety inspections scheduled but not completed before construction began.

EPA communication logs showed officials raising concerns. Being overruled. Political appointees pushing approval over career scientists' objections.

The same pattern. Cut corners, prioritize speed, ignore safety.

Except fusion wasn't crowd control. Fusion was nuclear-level energy release. A hundred million degrees of plasma contained by magnetic fields that had never been tested at this scale.

Scientists said three to five years minimum for safe prototype development. Kain promised ten months.

Catastrophic failure scenarios from academic papers: magnetic field collapse, plasma containment breach, thermal devastation in the blast radius. Environmental contamination lasting decades.

Ben documented everything. Safety violations. Environmental violations. Timeline pressure. Scientists' concerns noted in emails, then silenced.

The same shell company funding riots was funding a reactor that could kill thousands if it failed.

Different scale. Same recklessness.

He added the fusion evidence to his comprehensive case. Another pattern. Another demonstration that Kain touched nothing without corrupting it.

Good goals—clean energy, law and order—twisted by execution that valued image over safety, polling over lives.

Sarah would have seen this clearly. Would have named the pattern without hesitation.

But Sarah wasn't here.

Ben worked until his eyes burned. Until the numbers blurred and the coffee turned cold and the apartment felt like it was shrinking around him.

He didn't stop.

---

## Scene 4: The Parallel

Four in the morning. Ben caught his reflection in the darkened window and barely recognized himself.

Dark circles under his eyes. Weight loss visible in his face, his clothes hanging looser than they should. He hadn't shaved in—how many days? The stubble had graduated to something approaching an actual beard.

The apartment looked like his reflection felt. Dishes piled in the sink. Takeout containers on surfaces he'd cleared of files only to bury under new evidence. The military precision he'd maintained his whole life had dissolved into something messier, more desperate.

Obsession replacing discipline.

Somewhere in Eastern Europe—he didn't know exactly where, would never know—Ahdia was doing the same thing. Different investigation, different scale, but the same pattern. Working alone. Carrying secret burden. Convinced only she could do this.

He didn't know about her operations. Didn't know she was burning through her cellular integrity preventing genocides, lying to the team, using Ryu as her enabler. But if he'd seen it, he would have recognized the shape.

Hero complex. Isolation. Work as avoidance.

Both of them drowning in purpose to avoid feeling anything else.

His phone showed seventeen missed calls. Ruth (4). Victor (3). Tess (2). Team group chat (47 unread messages). He'd stopped responding days ago. Couldn't explain what he was doing without revealing the investigation, and couldn't stop long enough to make up convincing lies.

*Team meeting tomorrow*, Ruth's latest message said. *Will you be there?*

He should go. Should reconnect with the people who cared about him. Should let them help carry whatever weight he was accumulating.

But the case was almost done. Just a little more. Finish the documentation, prepare the submission, then he'd explain everything. They'd understand. They'd see why it had to be this way.

Ben turned back to his screens. More evidence to catalog. More patterns to document. More work to fill the space where grief wanted to live.

Sarah had been an activist. Had believed in the power of collective action, community organizing, people working together to change systems. She would have told him not to do this alone. Would have insisted that isolation was the enemy of justice.

But Sarah was dead.

And the only way Ben knew to make that mean something was to stop the man whose tactics had killed her.

He worked until dawn crept through the windows. Until his hands finally started shaking too hard to type.

Then he slept for three hours and started again.

---

## Scene 5: Submission and Hope

Morning light. Ben stood in front of his bathroom mirror, actually seeing himself for the first time in weeks.

He'd shaved. Showered. Put on a clean suit—the same one he'd worn to Webb's office, now pressed and professional. The dark circles remained, but the wildness was contained. Federal investigator mode, activated by conscious effort.

The briefcase on his bed contained everything. Master document. Evidence summaries. Timeline analysis. Financial tracking. Video timestamps with frame-by-frame breakdowns. Fusion project safety violations. Everything sourced, verified, timestamped, organized by category.

Months of work compressed into physical form. Eighteen inches of documentation that could end a presidential campaign.

If the system worked the way it was supposed to.

Ben believed it would. Had to believe it would. That was the foundation his entire worldview rested on—the conservative faith that institutions served justice, that proper procedure led to proper outcomes, that presenting truth clearly enough would make the truth matter.

Webb's warning echoed in his memory. *Political pressure. Idealism gets crushed. I've seen good cases buried.*

But those were other cases. Other evidence. Not this comprehensive. Not this undeniable.

Ben picked up his phone. Typed a response to Ruth's message: *Yes. Will explain everything soon.*

Soon he'd return to the team. Soon he'd share what he'd found. Soon they'd understand why he'd disappeared, why he'd worked in isolation, why he couldn't stop until the case was complete.

Soon Sarah's death would mean something.

He gathered the briefcase. Checked his reflection one more time. Professional. Competent. Ready.

The walk to the FBI field office took twenty minutes. Ben used the time to organize his thoughts, rehearse his presentation, prepare for the questions Webb's supervisors would ask. Every objection anticipated. Every gap in the evidence acknowledged and explained.

This was what he was good at. Building cases. Following evidence. Trusting process.

The system would work. It had to work.

Because if evidence this comprehensive, this undeniable, this carefully documented couldn't stop a man who orchestrated violence for political gain—

Then what was any of it for?

Ben pushed through the field office doors. Ready to submit. Ready to wait for proper procedure to unfold.

Ready to believe.

He didn't know yet that the evidence wouldn't matter. That CADENS would classify portions of his case as national security concerns. That witnesses would be intimidated into silence. That months of proper procedure would lead to nothing—except a desperate leak in Month 8, followed by the devastating revelation that truth didn't change minds, that Kain's polling would actually rise when the evidence went public, reframed by fear into proof of his willingness to make "hard choices."

He didn't know that his faith in institutions would shatter. That Sarah's death would remain meaningless no matter how much work he poured into giving it purpose. That the system he trusted would fail him so completely he'd spend years rebuilding his understanding of how the world worked.

Ben walked into the FBI field office with hope.

It was the last time he'd feel it for a very long time.

# Chapter 10: Language Shapes Thought

## Scene 1: Organizing Materials

The penthouse felt almost normal in the afternoon light.

Coffee brewing. Ruth in the corner reviewing patrol schedules. Tess and Leta on the couch, laptop showing screenshots of something Leah didn't want to look at too closely. Victor at the main table, organizing materials spread out in front of him—tactics lists, logistics plans, volunteer coordination sheets.

Leah sat across from him, studying the protest planning documents for Kain's upcoming campaign stop.

Month 2. Kain in Caledonia. The team organizing resistance alongside their regular operations.

"Sit-ins, die-ins, traffic blocking, banner drops..." She read through the tactics list, frowning slightly. The traffic disruption section gave her pause—peak commute hours, specific intersections, maximum impact.

Victor noticed her hesitation but didn't push. Organizer instinct: let people voice their own concerns.

"Can I bring something up?" Leah finally said.

"Always."

"I support the goals completely. Kain needs to be stopped. The rights violations, the rhetoric enabling violence—all of it's serious." She took a breath. "I'm just uncomfortable with some of the methods."

"Which ones?"

"The traffic blocking." She pointed at the document. "These are working people trying to get to jobs. Parents picking up kids. Hourly workers who can't afford to be late. Seems counterproductive to make their lives harder."

Victor listened without interrupting.

"Couldn't we target Kain directly instead of disrupting random commuters who didn't do anything?"

"That's a fair question." He picked up a campaign flyer from the materials. "But before I answer—look at this. Kain's big announcement for this rally."

American Energy Independence Act. Artificial sun prototype. American jobs.

"This actually sounds good," Leah said. "Why are we protesting?"

"Because who's building it matters as much as what's being built."

"But fusion is real science, right? Not a scam?"

"Fusion is real. Kain's promise isn't." Victor leaned back. "Same tactic as other authoritarian promises. Real issue plus impossible promise equals manipulation."

Leah thought about this.

"Border wall: Real immigration policy, Mexico pays—that part was a lie. Here it's real technology, but ten-month timeline when scientists say three years minimum." Victor shrugged. "He's using legitimate science to make his manipulation sound credible."

"So he's lying about the timeline to win votes."

"And using clean energy messaging to do it." Victor's expression was measured. "Can't just dismiss fusion because Kain backs it. Should support fusion research AND oppose his dangerous approach."

"Both at once?"

"Both at once. Technology is good AND this implementation is deadly."

Leah looked at the flyer again. "At least it's cleaner than coal..."

"Long-term, yes. This construction process isn't." Victor set down his coffee. "Kain opposed traditional solar for years. Called solar panels 'liberal energy.' Wind turbines 'eyesores.' Now he's backing fusion because it sounds powerful. Dominant. Masculine."

"But it's literally the same process. Sun's energy either way."

"Right. The irony is fusion IS solar power—that's what stars do. But aesthetics matter more than physics to authoritarians." Victor ticked off on his fingers. "Solar panels receiving a gift from nature—unacceptable, weak. Fusion reactor dominating nature—acceptable, strong. It's about control over process, not outcomes."

"So it's not about clean energy at all."

"Never was. And the construction is causing massive environmental damage right now. Rushed timeline means waived protections. Habitat destruction, groundwater contamination, waste dumping."

"Clean energy project poisoning the region to build it." Leah shook her head. "Unsustainable sustainable energy."

"Perfect summary." Victor smiled slightly. "Could be done sustainably with proper timeline. Kain's approach destroys environment to build environmental solution."

"Both things true at once."

"Exactly. Which brings us back to what you were saying." Victor met her eyes. "About traffic disruption and supporting goals."

"I don't see how that—"

"Can you repeat exactly what you said earlier? Word for word if possible?"

Leah blinked. "Which part?"

"The whole thing about supporting goals but being uncomfortable with methods."

Strange request. She tried to remember her exact phrasing.

"I said I support the goals... but I'm uncomfortable with some methods. I understand the frustration but tactics seem alienating. I agree Kain is dangerous but disrupting regular people seems wrong."

"Notice anything about how you phrased that?"

Leah thought. Then saw it.

"I used 'but' three times."

"In thirty seconds." Victor nodded. "Same pattern as Kain's fusion rhetoric, actually. Let me explain."

---

## Scene 2: The Lesson

"Nothing wrong with the word itself," Victor said. "Just pay attention to what it does. 'But' creates a relationship between two statements. Specifically, it creates hierarchy."

"Hierarchy?"

"First part gets stated, second part diminishes or contradicts it. So when you say 'support goals but methods concerning'—what you're really saying is methods matter more. Or supporting goals matters less than your comfort with methods."

Leah shifted uncomfortably. "That's not what I meant."

"Then let's test it." Victor held up a finger. "'Understand frustration but tactics alienating.' Which gets weight—understanding or tactics concern?"

"The tactics concern," she admitted. "That's what I focused on."

"Exactly. The 'but' tells the listener what actually matters to you." Another finger. "'Agree Kain is dangerous but disrupting regular people is wrong.' Which truth gets priority—Kain's danger or commuters' comfort?"

Leah was quiet.

"You're prioritizing commuter comfort over stopping Kain. That's what 'but' does. It reveals your actual hierarchy."

"So what should I say instead?"

"Try replacing 'but' with 'and.' Same content, different word."

"Support goals and uncomfortable with methods." She said it slowly. "That... feels different."

"What changed?"

"Both parts stay equal weight somehow. Neither one cancels the other."

Victor smiled. "That's exactly right."

He offered more examples. "Understand frustration BUT tactics alienating" versus "Understand frustration AND tactics might alienate some people."

"First version," he said, "you pick which matters more, dismiss the other. Second version, you hold both truths simultaneously."

"But the second way is harder."

"Much harder. That's the point." Victor leaned forward. "'But' lets you collapse complexity into simple choice. 'And' forces you to stay in complexity without resolution."

Leah was silent, processing.

"This isn't just semantics," Victor continued. "Organizers train this specifically because it shapes thinking. Language structure reflects and creates thought patterns."

"So 'but' thinking is binary. Either/or."

"And 'and' thinking is complex. Both/and. Holding tension." He gestured at the protest materials. "Traffic blocking is disruptive—true. It might save lives by stopping policy—also true. 'But' thinking makes you pick which matters more. 'And' thinking makes you find a response honoring both."

"The white moderate pattern," Leah said quietly. She'd read Letter from Birmingham Jail.

"Yes." Victor's voice was gentle. "Agree with goals but not methods. What that 'but' reveals: prioritizing comfort over justice. Preferring negative peace—absence of tension—over positive peace—presence of justice."

Leah recognized herself in the description. Uncomfortable.

"Everyone starts here," Victor said. "I did too. This is about learning, not judging. Political maturity means learning to hold both/and thinking."

"So when do you actually use 'but'?"

"When one truth genuinely negates the other. 'Wanted to help but made it worse'—second truth cancels first. 'Tried my best but it wasn't enough'—limitation negating effort." He shrugged. "Most of the time, though, both truths coexist. Training yourself to say 'and' trains your brain to hold complexity."

"Is that why organizing is exhausting?"

Victor laughed. "Partly. Comfort lives in certainty. Growth lives in tension. Both/and thinking means living in tension permanently."

---

## Scene 3: Personal Application

Leah was quiet for a long moment. Then she tried applying the lesson.

"Leta. The harassment she's facing." Month 2, early signs of coordinated targeting. "I want to support her and..." She caught herself. "I just used 'and.'"

Victor smiled. "Yes you did."

"I want to support her AND I'm uncomfortable being public about trans issues." She let the sentence sit. "That feels different. Can't resolve it by just picking one."

"That's growth. Now you see both truths. Question isn't which matters more."

"It's how I respond honoring both."

"Exactly. You can support Leta while being uncomfortable. Discomfort isn't excuse for silence. Discomfort is just data about your growth edges."

"So feeling uncomfortable doesn't excuse inaction."

"Never has. Never will." Victor's voice was firm but kind. "Both/and: You're uncomfortable AND she needs support. Can't resolve by choosing comfort over support. Must act while uncomfortable."

"That feels really hard."

"It is hard. Supposed to be." He held her gaze. "Easy thing is choosing your comfort. Right thing is honoring both truths in your action."

"How do you live with that tension though?"

"You don't resolve it. You practice it." Victor spread his hands. "Gets easier with practice but never comfortable. Comfortable means you've collapsed complexity back to simple. Discomfort is sign you're still honoring both truths."

Leah looked across the room. Leta on the couch, laughing at something Tess showed her on her phone. Trans activist. Sex worker organizer. Living both/and existence every day.

"That's why she's threatening to people," Leah said slowly. "Not either identity specifically. The complexity itself threatens binary thinking."

"People want simple categories. She refuses to provide them." Victor nodded. "Your discomfort about supporting her publicly? That's your binary thinking bumping against her complexity. That's your work to do, not hers."

---

## Scene 4: Broader Application

Leah looked back at the organizing materials. Same documents, different eyes.

"Traffic blocking is disruptive AND might be necessary to save lives."

"How does that feel to say?"

"Doesn't resolve my concern." She paused. "But clarifies the choice. Not which one matters more. How do I respond honoring both truths?"

Victor nodded. "Maybe we block traffic AND provide clear communication about why. Disrupt the commute AND explain what's at stake. Make people uncomfortable AND offer them a path to understanding."

"The 'and' doesn't remove the disruption."

"Never does. Both/and thinking doesn't make hard choices disappear. Just makes you face the full complexity of them."

Leah sat with this for a while. The coffee machine finished a new pot. Ruth got up for a refill. Normal afternoon.

"I'm going to notice this everywhere now," she said finally.

"You will. It's everywhere once you start seeing it. News, social media, casual conversation. People using 'but' to hierarchize truths constantly." Victor smiled. "Including yourself. Including me."

"You still catch yourself?"

"All the time. Trained this for years and still slip. Difference is noticing when you do it. Then choosing: Is this actually negation, or am I collapsing complexity? Usually it's the second one."

"And then you rephrase."

"Try to. When it matters. Sometimes in the moment, sometimes later reflecting. Point isn't perfection. Point is practice."

---

## Scene 5: The Commitment

Leah watched Victor return to his volunteer coordination sheets. Normal work. Quiet afternoon. But something had shifted.

"I'm going to pay attention to when I use 'but,'" she said.

"Good. You'll notice it everywhere."

"Including in my own head."

"Especially in your own head."

"That's going to be uncomfortable."

Victor smiled. "It is, and that's the work."

Leah caught it. He'd just done it. Both of them laughing at the recognition.

Language shaping thought. Thought shaping language.

Seed planted.

---

She didn't know yet. This lesson would haunt her.

Month 5. Month 6. Month 7. Leta needing public support, harassment intensifying, Leah uncomfortable and choosing silence.

*Support her BUT stay safe.*

The 'but' would let her choose hierarchy. Safety over solidarity. Her comfort over Leta's need.

She'd remember this conversation. Too late. After.

Victor taught her well.

She'd fail to apply it anyway.

That was human. That was the journey.

Both/and: Good person AND makes harmful choice. Understanding the lesson AND struggling to live it.

Seed planted now. Painful growth ahead.
