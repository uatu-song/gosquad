The tip came through Ben’s contact at the precinct. 

He read it off his phone as it had arrived, “Weapons cache in warehouse district. District 7, near the docks. Kain’s people moving product tonight, 2100 hours. Minimal security. Window’s tight.”

Ruth had the schematics up before he finished: one open floor, two entrances, a loading dock facing the water, a catwalk running the length of the ceiling. She marked the sightlines and turned the tablet around for the table. “Thoughts?”

“Fits the pattern. Kain’s been moving operations since the warehouse raid. Makes sense he’d use the docks.” Victor tapped the catwalk on the screen as he said it.

It did make sense. That was the detail she would come back to afterward, how neatly all of it made sense. She assigned positions off the schematic: Victor and Ben at the loading dock, Tess and Leah on the roof, herself on the perimeter with the comms rig and a view of both exits. Twenty minutes of planning for a tight window, and it held.

By 2040 hours they were parked two blocks out with the engine off, and Leah had the thermal scope braced against the dash. “Three heat signatures inside. No vehicles in the lot yet. If they’re moving product, we’re early.”

“Good,” Victor said from the passenger seat. “Hit them during setup. Before they’re organized.”

Tess leaned forward from the back seat. “I like it when they’re not organized.”

Then there was nothing to do but wait, so Tess got out her phone, and the glow filled the back of the van.

Leah leaned over Tess’s shoulder to read and sat back slowly. “Oh. Oh no.”

“What?” Victor demanded from the front.

“Wait,” Ben cut in, sitting up in the driver’s seat. “How do they know our codenames?”

“There’s like . . . hundreds of posts using them. Battlea this, Night Knight that.”

Leah turned in her seat to stare at her. “Did you—”

“What? No!” Tess replied, a beat too fast for innocence.

“Gloom Girl.” Ben laid the name down flat like evidence on a table.

“I didn’t leak anything,” Tess insisted as the van got quiet around her. “I just . . . may have used them. In a few posts. Months ago. Hypothetically.”

For months they had been blurry shapes on parking-lot footage, an argument between strangers online. Now strangers in Caledonia knew Gloom Girl by name. A named thing could be counted on, and a thing counted on was owed; Ruth felt that shift land somewhere she had no time to examine, so she didn’t examine it.

“Firas is going to love this,” Ruth said dryly.

“Or kill me,” Tess said and put the phone away.

At 2055 they moved. “Everyone check in,” Ruth said into the channel. “Comms clear?”

Four check-ins came back, level and ready. The charge arrived the way it always arrived, somewhere between the van and the fence line, without permission and without a visible source, and she was glad of it anyway. Some experimental military spillover, Ben figured, and she had stopped interrogating it months ago. To her it read like a pressor drip on a monitor: pressure up, output up, the whole system abruptly sufficient.

The call had been Firas’s invention, from their first job, and it had outlasted his ability to be present for it. “No better place than here,” Ruth said over four open mics.

All four of them answered her together, and for a moment the night felt winnable. “No better time than now.”

“Moving in,” Victor called, low and even. Through her earpiece came breath, boots, the low roll of the dock door, while Ruth watched the lot from the fence line and counted exactly nothing moving.

“Clear inside,” Victor reported a few seconds later. “Setting up position.” Ten seconds of quiet went by, and then ten more.

“Something’s wrong,” Tess said from the catwalk. “There’s nobody here. Warehouse is empty.”

Three heat signatures, zero people. Ruth ran the math in one stride: signatures without bodies meant the signatures were staged, and staging meant somebody knew which instruments they trusted. “Abort. Everyone out. Now.”

“Too late,” Ben answered.

The lot went white. Floodlights from three sides, sirens layering over sirens, units pouring into the street she had marked as the exit. She quit counting at fifteen vehicles. SWAT was already out and kneeling behind open doors, and a megaphone opened up over all of it.

“This is CCPD. You are surrounded. Exit the building with your hands visible.”

“Shit,” Leah hissed from the roofline.

“Smoke and scatter,” Ruth ordered, already moving along the fence. “Formation Delta. IR goggles. Move.” Canisters went off inside and the warehouse exhaled gray from every seam.

Delta was the drill they hated most and ran monthly anyway, four bodies on four routes with staggered timing, nobody through the same gap twice and nobody stopping to look back. On IR she watched them come out under the smoke, one, two, three, four, every one of them cleanly through a perimeter of fifteen units that had been built to catch them. Training did that, and she allowed herself exactly one breath to be proud of it.

“Anyone else losing equipment?” Tess asked over the channel.

Ruth looked down at her rig and found the goggles showing a full battery and a dead screen, the GPS a black rectangle in her hand.

“IR’s down,” Victor confirmed from somewhere in the gray. “GPS too.”

“What the hell—” Ben snapped somewhere near the north fence.

“Powers are down,” Leah said, and her breathing changed. “I can’t—they’re just gone.”

It reached Ruth mid-stride, and it was physical. Hour thirty of a residency shift, the caffeine quitting all at once, the body calmly submitting its true balance for review. Her legs weighed what legs weigh. The night got colder by the exact amount her charge had been holding off.

Breathing went audibly ragged on the channel, three sets of lungs headed toward panic. “You don’t need them. You’re trained. Stay focused.” She said it because it was true, and because it was the only true thing she had left to transmit.

“Fall back to secondary positions,” Ruth told them, counting heads she could no longer see. “Stay in smoke. Use training. You’ve got this.”

“Contact. North side. What the hell is that?” Ben called from closer to it than she was.

She found an angle through the smoke and got her first look: seven feet of dark armor, coming on at a leisurely walk. An officer broke cover to run, and the figure caught him one-handed and threw him fifteen feet into the side of a patrol car. The man hit the door panel and folded into the gutter. Ribs, she thought. Probably a lung.

“New player,” Ruth called, keeping it level for the channel. “Enhanced. Real enhanced. Everyone stay clear.”

“It’s a goddamn tank!” Victor yelled, and then he did not stay clear. He came in low off the flank, fast and textbook. The tank caught his forearm without turning his head, rotated it past the point a forearm rotates, and set him on the concrete almost gently. Ruth’s eye did the intake from forty yards out: shoulder anterior, radius likely, out of the fight.

“Victor!” Tess screamed.

Leah was already inside his reach. Throat, knee, temple, three strikes placed where the manuals dream of placing them, and none of it registered. A backhand put her through a stacked pallet, and the pallet came apart around her.

Ben she never saw move. One second he was circling wide; the next the tank had him off the ground by the throat, at arm’s length, watching him kick.

“Put him down!” Ruth shouted, and broke position. He did put him down. He dropped Ben in a heap and turned toward her.

The triage took her two seconds: three down but breathing, fifteen units tightening the noose, one hostile now tracking the only member still on her feet. The arithmetic handed her the job before she had agreed to take it. “Everyone fall back. Get clear. I’ll draw him off.”

“Ruth, no—” Tess said on the channel.

“That’s an order.” She ran. A quarter mile at a dead sprint, out of the smoke, down the service road, into the container yard at the docks, where the stacks made steel corridors that promised exits and mostly lied. The corridor she chose ended at open water.

Black water, low-tide smell, a concrete apron with no ladder in sight. Her mind priced it without being asked: forty degrees, ten minutes of useful movement, hypothermia on a schedule after that.

“Team, sound off. Everyone clear?” Static.

“Anyone copy?” Static. The sirens were a quarter mile behind her and might as well have been in another city. She was alone with the water.

He came around the end of the container row unhurried, like a man crossing a parking lot to his own car, and reached up with both hands and took off the helmet.

Some part of her had braced for a monster under there. The face was maybe thirty, clean-shaven, forgettable, like the fourth face from the left in any jury box, and it offered nothing to remember it by.

“Dr. Carter,” he said in the tone of a man confirming an appointment. “You should have stayed in the ER.”

Her name. Her actual name, pleasant and level, out of that armor. The wall between her two lives had taken careful work, shift after shift, and standing at the edge of the city she had just heard it come down in four syllables.

“How do you know my name?”

“Everyone knows your name now.”

There was no menace in it, only a hired man making conversation on the clock. “Mr. Kain sends his regards. Says your research will be very valuable. Once you’re out of the way.”

“You’re enhanced,” Ruth said, working it out loud. “Real enhanced. Not like us.”

“Finally figured that out?” He rolled his shoulders, loosening up for work. “Your tricks stopped working the moment the EMP hit. Ours didn’t.”

Seconds were the only currency she had left, so she spent them on a clinician’s questions, mechanism and dose. “What did they give you?” She kept it clinical, history-taking at the bedside. “What kind of enhancement?”

“The kind that matters.” He took a step. “The kind that can’t be turned off with a magnet.”

Another step. The apron ran out barely a yard behind her heels. “You know what happens next,” he said, sounding almost sorry about it. “Make it easy. Don’t run.”

She ran. The water offered ten minutes and the man in front of her offered less, so she went for the gap between the stacks instead. She got two strides into it before an armored hand closed on her jacket and swung her back to the lip of the apron. Grit skidded under her boots and her heels found the edge.

“Brave,” he said with something close to approval. “Stupid, but brave.”

He drew the fist back deliberately, a man with all the time he needed. Below her heels, the black water kept moving.
