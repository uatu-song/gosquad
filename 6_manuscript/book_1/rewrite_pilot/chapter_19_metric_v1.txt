Ruth closed her eyes. Nothing in front of her was worth watching, and some old animal part of her preferred the dark for this. The fist came. She heard the wind of it arrive at her ear as a low whistle, the sound heavy things make once they have finished deciding.

Then the whistle stopped in the middle of itself.

The water quit slapping the pilings behind her heels. The yard lights held their hum on one flat note and stayed there. She waited three seconds for the pain, strictly out of habit, and the pain declined to arrive. So she opened her eyes.

The fist hung four inches from her jaw. Above it the snarl had locked into place, upper lip drawn off the teeth, and the eyes behind it stayed open and dry and still. Her own breath left her and hung in the cold air in a shape that would not drift. Twenty feet off, at the mouth of the container canyon, a small figure stood with one hand out.

Ruth knew that posture. Shoulders squared against something, chin up, one arm extended as though holding a door for somebody slower.

“Ahdia?”

The figure took a step, stopped, and took another, and the walk was wrong in a way Ruth catalogued before she could stop herself: weight loaded onto the right leg, the left arm swinging dead. “Don’t move,” Ahdia said, and the words crossed the yard flat and certain.

#

Ruth turned her head slowly, because slowly seemed to be the only speed the world would still accept. Six meters out from the small figure the air changed. She could see the seam. Inside it everything held; past it the night went on being a night, a light bar sweeping red across the far container tops, a gull tilting and staying tilted only until it crossed the line. Standing at the boundary was the difference between being underwater and surfacing, and she was standing at it. Her heels were on the last inch of the dock. Her shoulders, by perhaps four inches, were inside the seam.

“You’re at the edge of the . . . the freeze. If you step outside, you’ll unfreeze too. Just . . . stay still.”

Ahdia crossed to her, and the crossing cost. Blood from the left nostril, tracked to the jaw and dried once already. Tremor in the extended hand, fine and fast. Tactical gear, matte black and torn open at the shoulder, and a black domino mask hanging off one corner of her face. Six meters of field traveled with her, and the seam slid forward over Ruth like a tide coming in, and the dock edge stopped being a border.

She made herself look at the fist again. It had moved. A hair, perhaps less, in the time she had spent looking away and back. The world was not stopped at all. The world had been slowed to a rate at which a man’s whole punch would take a week, and her mind refused the number and accepted the fist.

Ahdia walked around him. She set two fingertips against the outside of his right knee, held them there, and lifted them away. She did it again under his left shoulder blade. She did it a third time at the solar plexus, and a fourth at the other knee, and by the second one Ruth had it, because she had done it herself a hundred times with a violet pen on a shaved abdomen. The girl was marking incision points.

Then the yard came back on.

For half a second the whistle resumed and the water slapped and the lights buzzed, and the fist crossed an inch of the four it had left. Then it locked again. Ahdia had a hand flat on a container and her head down.

“Sorry. Can’t hold it as steady as I used to.”

Nothing in Ruth’s training covered a reply to that. She kept her heels where they were.

“This is going to be weird,”

“When I unfreeze him, he’s going to keep punching. But you won’t be there anymore. I’ll move you. Just . . . don’t freak out, okay?”

“Okay,”

“On three,”

Ahdia stepped in and laid her palm against the man’s sternum and leaned on it, the exact pressure you use on a door you know is unlocked. “One . . . two . . .”

There were hands on Ruth’s shoulders. Then there was nothing, an interval with no content whatsoever, and then she was ten feet to the left with her back against cold steel, and her body reported no memory of having traveled. The gap frightened her more than the fist had.

The world let go.

The punch finished into empty air and carried him a stumbling half step, and in that instant the four touches landed together. His right knee folded sideways. Something across his back gave with a sound Ruth felt in her own teeth. He came off his feet, and the gentle hand that had leaned on his sternum arrived as a truck, and he went backward through the wall of a container and did not come out of it.

Then Ahdia dropped.

Ruth got to her before her knees landed, which was reflex. She went to the ground with her and turned her and the doctor took over, because the doctor always did. Pulse thready and far too fast. Skin burning through the torn suit. Pupils blown wide and slow to answer the light. Respirations shallow, fast, and getting worse while she watched.

“Ahdia. Look at me. Focus.”

“Saved you . . . like I saved all of them . . .”

Ruth’s mind did what it did with any presentation that made no sense, which was to quit arguing with the symptoms and go hunting the one disease underneath them.

Tess off the bridge rail into open air and delivered unhurt onto a rooftop four blocks inland. Somebody carried her. Victor’s hand closing on a rifle barrel and folding it like foil, in a man who could not deadlift what that takes. Somebody amplified him. Ben leaving a roof at three feet and arriving at thirty, always on the same crouch. Somebody put him there. Leah at freeway speed, shoulder first, in a woman Ruth had treated for a hamstring.

Her own callouts, the team a half beat early on every one of them. She reached that item, turned it twice, and set it down unsolved, because the rest of the list was answering.

Tonight an EMP had gone off in that lot and taken four powers off the board inside one second, and left her precisely as she had always been. She had wondered what that meant about her, and the answer was that it meant nothing about her. It meant the other four had been running on something outside their own bodies.

That something was lying in her lap, bleeding from the nose, twenty-three years old, and supposed to be dead since August.

“The powers,”

“Their powers. That was you.”

“They needed . . . to believe . . .”

“You’ve been dying to give us powers we never had,”

“Worth it.”

Ruth put the back of her hand against the girl’s forehead, a mother’s gesture and not a physician’s, and did it anyway. “You’re burning up.”

“How long have you been doing this?”

“Since the warehouse. Since the explosion. Since you all . . .”

The sentence quit and restarted somewhere else, as sentences do when the blood pressure has opinions. “Needed to matter. Needed to help. He said I was already dead inside, so . . . might as well be useful while . . .”

Ruth had been standing in that room. She had watched Firas say it and had taken him apart for saying it, and the whole time there had been nobody behind the blue foam vault, and the air in that corner had stayed empty.

Ahdia stopped breathing.

“Ahdia!”

“Stay with me. Come on.”

Her kit held a tourniquet, gauze, shears, and one ampule of epinephrine, and she had never been so out of ideas. There was no wound. There was no bleed she could compress and no airway she could open. This wasn’t an injury. This was collapse, a whole system closing its accounts, and the only intervention that mattered had already been spent on a woman kneeling at the edge of the water.

The radio at her collar came up out of its static. “Dr. Carter.”

“Who is this?”

“My name is Ryu. I’m here to extract the asset.”

“The ‘asset’ is a person,”

Ruth had two fingers under the jaw, hunting a pulse that kept declining to be found. “And she’s dying.”

“I’m aware.”

“CADENS can help her. But we need to move. CCPD three minutes out.”

“What the hell is CADENS?”

“Come with her and find out. Or stay and watch her die. Your choice. Clock’s ticking.” Something came down the canyon at shoulder height with running lights on it, unhurried and openly visible, towing a stretcher that carried itself. Behind it a man in a windbreaker jogged into the light, badge on a lanyard, a scientist doing fieldwork badly.

She made the drone show her the feeds first. Four green shapes north of the cordon, upright, moving together, away. Victor’s shoulder would want setting. Ben would breathe wrong for a week. All four had airways. The woman on the dock did not.

“I’m not leaving her side,”

“Then you’re coming with us. Hold on.”

Ruth got both hands under the girl and did not let go, and then the light climbed past white and the docks folded inward like origami, and every cell she owned received the correction on a slightly different schedule. She arrived on her hands and knees on polished concrete and threw up twice, while above her a man said something calm about protocols.

The room was a medical bay built by people with no budget ceiling. Articulated arms lifted Ahdia off the floor and laid her on a table without a human hand touching her, and screens came down on their own, and Ruth got herself upright by the wall and made her hands stop. “Tell me what’s wrong with her.”

“She’s dying. But you already knew that.” The woman had come in behind her without noise. Silver-gray, past fifty-five, dressed for a boardroom at an hour when boardrooms are dark.

“Who are you?”

“Director Overseer Bourn. CADENS. And you’re Dr. Ruth Carter—emergency medicine, Johns Hopkins, cellular regeneration research that conventional medicine laughed out of conferences.”

Ruth had spent three years learning to hear that sentence without flinching, and mostly she had succeeded.

“We need to talk. But first, let’s stabilize your friend.” The nearest screen wrote itself in white while the arms worked.

“Cellular Degradation Rate: Accelerating”

“Temporal Phase Variance: 47%”

“Telomere Integrity: 18% (CRITICAL)”

“Projected Viable Function: 18 months ± 6 months”

Telomere analysis took a lab three weeks and a grant application. This machine had done it in under a minute, and Ruth registered the professional awe, filed it for a night she could afford, and went straight at the number instead. “Eighteen months,”

“Best case scenario.”

“That’s if she stops using her powers completely,”

“Every use accelerates breakdown. Tonight’s intervention alone depleted another 4%.”

Four percent. A fist, a wall, ten feet of a woman moved sideways out of its way. She made herself hold the number where she could see it. “At her current rate . . .”

“Weeks. Maybe less.”

“Can you stop it?”

“Can you un-shoot someone? Put aged cells back together?”

“No.”

“Neither can we. We can only slow it. Make the time she has left . . . matter.”

Bourn indicated a chair against the wall with two fingers, like a surgeon indicates an instrument. “Sit,”

“I’ll explain everything. And then you can decide if you want to help her, or watch her burn herself out trying to save people who don’t even know she exists.”

An hour later Ruth stood at an observation window above a lit table with a small figure on it, and the broad strokes had come in the same flat register. An artifact in a warehouse. Drones that amplified whatever she gave them. Six weeks of monitoring, timestamped, unbroken, beginning the night Ruth herself had been dragged out of a burning building believing she had lost her.

“You saw what she can do,”

“Imagine that scaled up. Imagine using it against actual threats. Cosmic-level incidents. Things that would make Kain look like a playground bully.”

“You want to use her,”

“We want to keep her alive long enough to transition the team to truth. FAERIS drones are learning. Evolving. Eventually they’ll be able to provide real enhancements without her temporal manipulation. But we need time.”

“How much time?”

“Months. Maybe a year. If we’re lucky.”

Bourn stood at the glass beside her and did not look through it. “Your research,”

“Cellular regeneration protocols. Could they work on temporal degradation?”

Ruth ran it before she answered, honestly, as she would for a resident. “Possibly,”

“The principle is similar. Accelerate cellular repair. Reduce breakdown rate. I’d need to adapt the protocols. Test dosages. Monitor effects.”

“You’d have access to our resources. Full lab. Synthesis equipment. Isotopes you can’t get anywhere else.”

Three years of grant applications for isotopes, offered at two in the morning like a mint on a pillow. “You’ve been watching my research.”

“For two years. Since your first paper. We knew you’d intersect with our work eventually.”

“You knew I’d be useful.”

“We knew you were brilliant. And that brilliance would be needed.”

The word went in somewhere old and sat there. A room of men had decided years ago what her work was worth, and this organization had disagreed and never said so.

“We just didn’t know how.”

“What’s the offer?”

“Keep her alive as long as possible. Help transition Go Squad to truth when FAERIS achieves independence. Be our liaison—they trust you.”

“You want me to manage the lie.”

“Manage the transition. There’s a difference.”

Ruth let that sit, because there was a difference, and it was smaller than either of them was pretending. “I need to see your lab first,”

She put her back to the glass and negotiated as an equal or not at all. “And I need full disclosure on what you know about her condition. Everything. No secrets.”

“Deal. Dr. Ryu will give you the tour. Then we’ll discuss terms.”

“One more thing,”

She sorted it worst first, and the worst was on the table behind the glass. “I tell my team I got separated during the fight. I tell them I’m clear. They can’t know about this. Not yet.”

“Agreed. We’ll translocate you back near the Academy. Make it look like you escaped on your own.”

Her phone had survived the night. Her goggles had not. She typed it with her thumb.

“Had to split off. I’m clear. Meet at Academy.”

Then she looked once more through the glass, at the girl the whole city had already buried, and got nothing back from her own face in the reflection.
