Her legs went out from under her without any negotiation. One knee hit the pavement, then one hand, and the cold came up through her palm and stayed. The suit hung off her in every direction, the sleeves over her knuckles. Her lungs burned, and the edges of the intersection had gone soft and grey, and she let them go.

The four of them lay where they had dropped. Ogden. Philips. Edgar. Orlansky. Torn uniform with nobody inside it, flatter than men are, the shapes all wrong. Somebody in the ring had said we killed them, and Ahdia had been standing in the middle of that ring, close enough to know. It was Gloom Girl. Gloom Girl had translocated out with Firas before any of this, and the sentence was still lying in the street without her.

Kain stood over all of it at forty feet and had not stopped growing. His bones cracked like gunshots as they went, one and then another, a sound with no give in it. Under the clear skin the Heart pulsed in his chest like a dying star, red into gold and back, unhurried. Battlea and Crimson Sable had backed off the husks and were looking up at him.

“We have to move.”

Ruth had her by the arm and was already lifting.

“Now. Before he—”

Kain moved first: the arm came up, the whole intersection went dark under it, and the fist came down like a building falling.

Ahdia reached for the Seed.

She went down into the cold place behind her sternum and asked it for the thing it had always given her, and nothing came back. She had spent it getting them here, on surviving the impact, on changing her clothes in a blink.

Something took her in the hip and the pavement she was kneeling on stopped existing.

The fist landed where she had been. It took the asphalt out in a dish, the cracks ran twenty feet, and shrapnel came off the rim in a flat sheet. A chunk of road the size of a basketball went past her head close enough to move her hair. Leah was half on top of her, elbow in her ribs, already pushing off, already gone.

“Scatter!”

“Don’t let him track patterns!”

Leah went left because she was fastest and drawing him was the job. Crimson Sable broke right. Night Knight was up the face of the pharmacy toward something with an angle.

Ruth got a fist in her harness and hauled her up.

“Can you run?”

Ahdia nodded. It was a lie and Ruth took it, because there was nothing else on offer.

“Keep moving!”

“Don’t stop!”

The hand came down at Leah and closed on air. She had changed direction a half step before it committed, derby instinct, reading the momentum of something enormous and taking the angle off it.

Crimson Sable came in under the ankle with the rebar two-handed, a swing that should have taken bone out of a man. It bounced. The shock came back up the bar into his shoulders, and Ahdia watched his hands open and shut on the metal.

“It’s not working!”

There was grief in it, out where anybody could hear.

“He’s too—”

The backhand was almost courteous: Kain did not look down and did not wind up. The arm came across and Crimson Sable left the street in a flat low arc, went into the second floor of the pharmacy thirty feet away, and brick came down after him, and Victor did not move.

“Victor!”

Leah was already turning, and turning the wrong way: toward him, out into the open, straight across the front of the thing they had all been running from.

“Leah, NO!”

Ruth’s scream changed nothing. The hand came down and Leah went under it, took the landing on her shoulder, rolled out of it and came up running without losing a stride, and got to him. She had a fist in his collar and his ribs were moving.

“I’m okay,” Victor said, and it took most of what he had left.

“I’m—”

Night Knight came in from the blind side and did not go at Kain. He went at them, full body, both arms, and took all three of them out of the radius in a tangle of limbs and gear while the fingers shut on empty road.

“Futile.”

The word came down over the intersection and the glass left in the pharmacy front hummed with it.

“You’ve already lost. You fed me. Made me whole. Now watch what that bought you.”

Ahdia lay where Ruth had put her and listened to the sentence land on the people it was about. Night Knight had his head down over Victor. Leah had both hands full of his collar and had stopped moving. Nobody answered it, and there was nothing on the street that could.

She reached again.

The Seed answered her this time, cold and thin, a flicker, a fraction of what it had been an hour ago, and she held everything she had out to it, and the intersection did not slow. Ruth was running and was not going to get there. None of them were going to get anywhere, and the hands were coming down over them, and Ahdia knelt in the road with her whole self open and nothing coming through.

Then the sky screamed.

The air arrived before the sound, a shove of displaced pressure up through the pavement into her knees, and something crossed above the rooftops faster than the noise it was making. It went into Kain’s chest.

White, blinding, total, a small sun standing over downtown, and then the wave came out through the buildings and every window for three blocks let go. Forty feet of him hit the street like a falling building, and the road came up in a swell and put her down on her side.

She heard the boots before the dust gave them up, and then they came through it in a formation nobody had to think about, twenty of them, black kit and long guns, spreading around the crater and taking angles on a dead thing.

The man in front stopped at the lip and put two fingers to his ear. Ahdia knew him: she had sat across a table from him under four hundred feet of dirt, and he had been dry then.

“Overseer,” Mack said.

“Target is down. Repeat, target is down.”

The earpiece came back loud enough for Ahdia to hear it, a woman’s delivery with the affect sanded off.

“Confirmed kill?”

“Confirmed,” Mack said. He was looking into the crater when he said the rest.

“Kain is dead.”

Nobody had asked him for what came next. He gave it anyway, in the tone of a man reading a report he already knows the end of.

“Overseer wanted authorization before engaging,” Mack said.

“Pentagon wanted assurances. SecDef wanted contingencies. We stopped waiting.”

Then he turned his head an inch toward the comm.

“Some things are more important than careers, ma’am.”

Ahdia sat down in the road, legs splayed, breath going in and out too fast to do her any good, and looked across at the body in the crater.

Dead. They’d won.

A medic came at Ruth with a bag already open.

“Ma’am, we’re here to assist with—”

“I’m a doctor. ER. I’ve got this.”

Ruth did not look up from Ahdia’s arm.

“Where’s Firas? Where’s Gloom Girl? Did they—”

“Safe,” the operative said, checking his forearm.

“Translocated out before engagement. We tracked them to—”

The ground moved.

It was smaller than the blast and it kept time, spaced out and even, like a heartbeat, coming up through her tailbone out of the crater. Down there the body had gone into motion with nothing alive in it, the chest folding inward first, everything travelling toward the Heart.

“What the—”

Mack was already moving.

“Fall back!”

The formation came apart and re-formed twenty yards out.

“Everyone back!”

Forty feet of him went to thirty. The arms folded into the torso and the torso went on folding. Twenty, and the temperature fell off a cliff, and Ahdia could see her own breath in the middle of the street. Fifteen. Ten. Five. Then there was no body at all, only a point turning two feet above the crater floor, no bigger than a basketball, so dense the air bent going past it.

“What’s happening?”

Ruth had both hands on her again.

“I don’t know.”

She was looking at it and pulling at the same time.

“But we need to move. Now.”

Then it pulled.

The debris went first: glass, brick dust, a torn length of uniform, the rebar lying where Victor had dropped it. It all came off the road and went in and was not anywhere any more, each piece faster than the last.

“Singularity!”

“It’s forming a singularity! Everyone out! NOW!”

Ahdia felt it take hold of her body. The suit rippled around her in a wind that was not wind, every fold drawn one way, and her hair went forward across her face. Ruth had her under both arms and was hauling the other way and losing, an inch and then another inch, boots skidding through glass.

“We need immediate evac! The artifact is collapsing into—”

Ahdia was not looking at the point any more, but past it, at the bank.

It was leaning. It did it so slowly that for a moment she could tell herself she had invented it, and then she could not. The windows went out of the upper floors in order, one row and then the row under it. The frame let out a sound she felt in her teeth. Steel does not scream. What came out of it was lower than that and longer, and it did not stop.

It was going to come down. It was going to come down across the whole intersection, and everyone still standing in it was underneath.
