SETTING THE TRAP

The footage was on its fourteenth viewing, and Harding Kain read it like a quarterly report: patiently, from the back, hunting the one number that refused to reconcile. On the screen a figure went up the face of a parking structure and arrived at the roofline, thirty feet of sheer concrete in under two seconds. Halfway up, the image smeared, as if the camera itself had flinched. There. Every viewing, right there.

He cut into the lamb without looking down at it. The tray had come up at seven; the office smelled of rosemary and old leather. Forty floors below, the city glittered on services his companies billed it for monthly. His dinner rings clicked against the knife while he chewed, and the blur arrived again where it always arrived. “Run the scanning thing again,” he said.

Reed touched the console without comment, a young man who was expensive and precisely as useful as he cost, which in Kain’s experience made him rare. “Spectral analysis shows the same result, sir. Electromagnetic anomaly during the movement. Military-grade frequency signature.”

Military-grade. That phrase had a catalog price, and TRIOMF wrote the catalog. Kain set the knife down and gave the big signet a slow half turn on its finger.

The money under him ran three generations deep, and none of it had started in security. His grandfather pulled diamonds out of ground that fought him, and broke three strikes to keep pulling; the family albums called it grit. His father built the armored cars that moved the stones, and somewhere on those highway runs he noticed that the fear of losing a thing billed better, year over year, than the thing itself. Harding had taken that public — TRIOMF, the contracts, the candidacy. Monetize fear and call it safety. The stones were the blood, and the armor was only ever the arm. So the signature ruled out amateurs. That was the fourteenth viewing’s dividend: hobbyists did not smear on federal equipment. Somebody with resources had put capital into these people, and unaccounted capital was the only kind that had ever interested him.

The intercom announced Chief Whitford at half past eight, and Reed let himself out before being asked. Whitford came in with a folder held against his thigh. Kain appraised him across the desk: still a serviceable asset, but depreciating, the face above the uniform worn well past its book value. “You look troubled, John.”

“Read it.” The folder landed on the blotter, its CLASSIFIED faded from red to pink, which meant copies of copies and years of hands.

Kain did not touch it. He laced his fingers over his waistcoat and waited like a man at the top of a backswing. “Summarize.”

Whitford’s jaw tightened, and then he recited. “Tech signatures. Military-grade surveillance drones detected during seventeen separate Go Squad operations over the past six weeks.”

He turned a page he plainly knew by heart. “My people were right. Someone’s watching them. Federal-level equipment.”

“FBI? CIA?”

“Neither.”

Kain looked up from the screen for the first time since the chief had come in. “Then who the hell are they?”

“CADENS.” The name landed on nothing. Kain had spent thirty years mapping federal money down to the last classified dollar, and this one had never crossed his desk. Either irrelevant or terrifying. He kept his face shut and waited for the rest.

“Cataclysm Activity Detection and Engagement Network Sentry.”

“Explain.” The rings resumed their slow turn while Whitford found the page.

“Shadow group funded through Special Access Programs. Black budget. Completely autonomous operational authority. Only the Secretary of Defense has direct contact with their leadership, and even he can’t give them orders.”

Every budget had an owner, and every chart had a top box. “Someone can.”

“One person. Director. Codename: Overseer.”

A person above the Secretary of Defense, with no name and no budget line. Kain reached for a price. Nothing attached. The list of things he could not price was a short list, and he disliked every item on it.

“And how,” Kain asked, the rings going still on the desk, “did you acquire classified intelligence on a shadow government program that doesn’t officially exist?”

“Golf.”

“Excuse me?”

Whitford shrugged like the answer embarrassed him. “The Secretary of Defense and I golf regularly. Montclair Country Club, every third Thursday when he’s in town.”

Whitford reported it in exactly the tone of a business expense. “He was less than pleased about learning that CADENS tech was being used to help vigilantes beat up local precincts.”

The signet stopped again on its finger. “He told you this.”

“He implied it. Heavily. After his third whiskey.”

That part Kain believed, having bought better information for less in the same clubhouse weather. Or the Secretary had wanted it carried. That was likelier. Men that senior did not slip. They delivered.

Whitford turned the folder face down before he went on. “Apparently the Overseer doesn’t clear operational details with the Pentagon. Just does what they want. And what they want, currently, is to monitor enhanced individuals in urban environments. Go Squad qualifies.”

Whitford looked at the paused blur for a moment and seemed to dislike where his own sentence was going. “They’re using us as a test case.”

Kain considered the frozen figure on the screen and finished the thought for him. “We’re the rats in the maze. Go Squad are the subjects. CADENS gets to watch how enhanced individuals operate in real-world scenarios against organized opposition.”

“We’re opposition now? Not law enforcement?”

Kain let him keep the question, and the chief paced to the window and answered it himself. “My officers look like fools on every news cycle. ‘Vigilantes outsmart police again.’ ‘Community says Go Squad more effective than CCPD.’ ‘Where are our tax dollars going?’”

Kain watched the chief’s reflection in the dark glass and let him finish. “Your ego is showing, John.”

Whitford’s color was fully up now. “It’s not about ego. It’s about institutional credibility. Every time Go Squad makes us look incompetent, people ask why we can’t do what they do. Why we need military equipment and qualified immunity if a bunch of parkour enthusiasts can do it better.”

There it was, under the budget language. Obsolescence, not ego. A man afraid the market had found a way to route around him — and Kain recognized that fear from the inside and quietly priced the recognition for later use.

“So the Secretary of Defense is angry, but powerless. And in his powerlessness, he shared information he shouldn’t have.”

“Can’t be helped,” Whitford allowed.

He came back from the window. “CADENS operates under Overseer’s complete control, not his. But he did slip some information that could be useful.”

From inside his jacket he drew a second document, thinner, unstamped, deniable: specifications, operating parameters, and a last page with a single heading. VULNERABILITIES. Kain slowly read that page twice. Nothing reached his face.

Kain closed the second document and squared it with the first. “This changes things.”

“That’s what I thought.”

#

That morning had belonged to a different file. Reed had delivered it at seven: three hundred pages on Ruth Carter, bound like a prospectus, and Kain had gone through every page over breakfast and finished both at the same time. “Their leader,” Kain said, squaring the pages on the blotter.

Reed answered without opening his copy of the file. “The medical one. She’s the tactical center. Every operation flows through her decisions.”

Kain found the tabbed sheet and read from it. “Johns Hopkins medical degree,” he read, and tapped the line below it with one heavy ring.

He read on down the page. “Top of her class. Published groundbreaking research on cellular regeneration therapy three years ago. Presented at international conferences. Should be running a research department or leading clinical trials.”

Kain set the page down on the squared stack. “Why is she in an ER?”

Reed had the answer ready, which was why Kain kept him. “Politics, looks like. She challenged the chief of surgery over patient care protocols. Got sidelined. Been stuck in emergency medicine ever since.”

Which was not, Kain noted, what the woman herself would say. Her published account blamed the science — the conference, the reception, the doors that stayed shut. She had been told the reason was her work. The file knew it was a man she had crossed.

Kain knew the mechanism personally. He had rented it more than once. A committee seat. A quiet lunch. And a career went into storage with no document worth a subpoena.

“She’s wasted where she is,” Kain concluded. An appraisal, not sympathy. Mispriced assets offended him on principle.

Reed took the sheet back and squared it with more care than it needed. “Yes sir. But she’s also the tactical center of Go Squad. She’s the weak point.”

Kain turned back to the research summary and ran the figures without a pen: cellular regeneration, published, shelved, unprotected, worth eight figures to the medical subsidiaries if the patents were drafted with any appetite at all. Conservatively eight.

“I want her research,” Kain told him, turning the folder back to its first page.

“Sir?”

Kain laid the file flat and spread one ringed hand on it. “The cellular regeneration protocols. The experimental treatments. All of it. Once we’ve eliminated Go Squad, we acquire the research. Patent it under TRIOMF subsidiaries. She won’t be in a position to object.”

Reed’s pen stopped, and he looked at the carpet and then at a spot in the middle distance, choosing his footing. “Sir . . . she’s Black. You really want to—”

“I want to win.” The room held still around it. His grandfather would have refused her work on principle and died owning less. His father would have stolen it and paid a lawyer to call the theft providence. Harding acquired.

The rings were motionless while he said it. “Personal feelings are expensive luxuries. Her research is valuable. The fact that she’s Black is irrelevant to its value.”

Reed wrote it down, the pen moving carefully now. “Once the trap is set and Go Squad is neutralized, we move on her research. Have legal prepare acquisition documents. Medical board complaints. Patent filings. Make it look clean.”

He closed the folder on the badge photograph, a woman who looked directly into lenses, and slid it back across the desk. “She built something valuable while they kept her in an ER,” Kain went on.

He stood and buttoned his jacket over the waistcoat. “That’s the problem with institutional punishment. They suppress talent without capturing it. We’ll do better.”

#

By nine that evening Whitford had heard the shape of it. He stood at the glass with a scotch he had barely touched, watching the city light itself district by district below. Subtracting things.

“You want to pressure her.”

Kain laid it out in deliverables. “I want her to make a choice. Save herself or save her team. Make it public enough that CADENS can’t intervene without revealing themselves. Make it brutal enough that Go Squad questions whether they’re actually helping or just getting people killed.”

“Break the leadership, break the team.”

Kain turned his chair to face him fully. “Break the team, break the narrative. Break the narrative, remind people why they need order. Why they need someone strong enough to protect them from chaos.”

“You’re going to run on this.”

“I’m going to win on this.”

He said it in the tone he used for confirming wire transfers. Then he nodded at the screen, where the blur still hung mid-climb. “Set the trap. I want them confident. I want them thinking they’re protected. I want them walking into it believing they’re invincible.”

“I’ll coordinate with tactical.” The chief nodded slowly at the carpet.

“You’ll have what you need.” The screen light sat on his rings.

Whitford finally collected his folders and got as far as the door before he stopped. “There’s one more thing.”

“What?”

Whitford did not turn around when he said it. “The anomalies. The impossible movements. Even with CADENS watching them, they’re too good. Something else is happening.”

Kain waited, because the discomfort on the chief was worth watching; it meant the thought had been carried around for days. “Maybe they are enhanced,” Whitford offered.

He said it like a man testing ice. “Maybe CADENS gave them more than surveillance.”

“Or maybe someone else did.”

Kain heard his own sentence land and disliked it. Two unknowns were operating in his city now, a federal program with no address and whatever lived inside that blur, and he had built his fortune on being the man who knew. He preferred markets with fewer entrants. He preferred names.

Kain rose and came around the desk at his own pace. “If they’re enhanced, we’ll need more than conventional force to stop them.”

“What did you have in mind?”

Kain pressed the intercom with one heavy knuckle. “Get Dr. Jericho from holding. It’s time we had another conversation about his collection.”

#

The vault sat four levels under the tower, behind a door labeled CLIMATE ARCHIVE. Sixty-two degrees, forty percent humidity — Jericho’s own specifications, written years ago for the museum, and there was a certain economy in letting the man’s rigor furnish the man’s cage. Kain’s grandfather’s union men had taught the family that you did not break a strike, you broke the man the other men watched, and that isolation did the work of violence at a fraction of the unit cost. Six weeks of clean quiet had been doing that work here. Two guards brought Jericho in and sat him at the steel table. Six weeks ago the man had curated the sub-basement of the Natural History Museum. He had visibly lost weight since, and his collar stood away from his neck. His hands shook. He flattened them on the table and kept them there while he spoke.

“Walk me through them again,” Kain said. The vault door had closed behind the guards on its slow hinge. The cases stood in two rows, twenty-three of them, lit from below. Some of the objects inside sat at angles that did not properly resolve. Kain had stopped trying to make them resolve.

Jericho kept his eyes on the table. “Most of them are inert. Or appear to be. We documented electromagnetic anomalies. Temperature fluctuations. But no active properties we could test safely.”

“And the others?”

Jericho looked briefly at the corner case before he answered, then looked away from it, and Kain walked to the corner case and stood beside it. “The Heart of Tamois,” Jericho answered.

“Tell me.”

Jericho recited it like catalog copy. “Recovered from a dig site in the Maghreb. Private expedition funded by collectors who wanted answers the academic establishment wouldn’t provide. Carbon dated at 4.7 billion years.”

“Older than Earth.”

“Older than the solar system. The object shouldn’t exist.”

The thing in the case was the size of a fist, and crystalline. It pulsed. Red, then amber, then red again, like a slow heartbeat.

“What does it do?”

The shaking had reached his shoulders. “We don’t know. Not exactly. The electromagnetic readings were off the scale. Temperature fluctuations. Temporal distortions in its immediate vicinity.”

“Temporal,” Kain repeated.

“Time moves differently near it. Milliseconds. Nothing significant. But measurable.” Thirty feet of concrete in under two seconds. A camera flinching at nothing. He did not need it explained twice.

“Figure it out,” Kain told him.

“What?”

“What it does. How it works. Whether it’s useful.”

He set one ringed knuckle on the glass, above the pulse. “You have everything you need here. Equipment. Monitoring systems. Time.”

“I can’t just—we documented these artifacts for years without understanding them. You’re asking for answers we don’t have.” The flat hands had come up off the table.

“Then find them. You’re an anthropologist. You study dead civilizations, decode their technology. Do that.”

Jericho’s glasses had slid down; he did not fix them. “In how long?”

“Forty-eight hours.”

“That’s impossible.”

“Then you’d better work quickly.”

He was at the vault door when he stopped and turned around. Jericho had not moved. The red light crossed the man’s glasses and left them, crossed them and left them. “Dr. Jericho? You’ve spent your career studying dead civilizations. Objects from species that no longer exist. Now you get to discover if their solutions still work.”

The door sealed on the collection. In the corridor the air ran warmer, and Whitford stood waiting with the guards, out of earshot of the cases. “He’ll do it?” Whitford asked.

“He’ll do it.” Kain moved toward the elevator without waiting.

“And after?”

Kain did not slow down for the answer. “After, we test the Heart. See if the transformation can be controlled. See if we can create something strong enough to stop enhanced individuals.”

“You’re talking about human experimentation.”

“I’m talking about leveling the playing field. Go Squad has powers they shouldn’t have. We’re going to match them.”

“With artifacts from civilizations that died billions of years ago.”

“With tools that work. The source doesn’t matter. Results do.”

“When do we deploy?”

“Once we have the Heart. Once we’ve confirmed it functions. Then we set the trap.”

“And Carter?”

“Carter gets to watch her team fall apart. Gets to choose between saving them and saving herself. Gets to learn that powers and federal protection don’t mean invincibility.” The elevator carried them up in silence, and Whitford watched the floor numbers the whole way. Kain watched nothing at all.

#

The office was empty when he came back to it. He poured nothing and ate nothing. He sat with the skyline until the phone on the credenza rang at eleven, the one with no line into the building. His engineers had opened that wall twice and found no wire behind it, and it rang anyway when it chose to. This was the third call in six months. The first two had each carried one piece of information. Both pieces had been perfect.

He picked it up on the second ring and waited. “Mr. Kain.” A woman’s voice, precise and clinical, statements where questions should be, like someone who had learned English as a technical specification.

“I’m listening,” he said.

“The Heart of Tamois. You have acquired it.”

The vault was four levels down, sealed, on no network he had ever ordered built. “I have.”

“And Dr. Jericho is attempting to unlock its properties.”

That decision was an hour old, made behind a steel door in a room that recorded nothing. “He is.”

“He will fail,” she said. He deliberately turned the signet once and did not answer.

“The Heart requires specific activation protocols. Protocols his civilization does not possess.”

His civilization. He let the phrasing go past.

“You’re offering to help.”

“I am offering partnership. You require tools to neutralize enhanced individuals. I require the artifacts gathered and their properties tested. Our interests align.”

“What do you want in return?”

“The subjects eliminated. Permanently. No capture. No interrogation. Complete removal from the board.”

She kept calling the Go Squad “subjects.” He filed the word where she had set it, perfectly flat.

“Why?”

“That is not your concern. You wish to win your campaign. I wish to see these individuals removed. The Heart provides both outcomes.”

He weighed it like any position: cost, return, counterparty. No other bidders. The cost would be carried by other people, the return was everything he currently wanted, and the counterparty had been right twice already without once explaining herself, and that was itself a data point.

“What are the activation protocols?”

“I will provide them when Dr. Jericho has completed his preliminary analysis. Forty-eight hours, as you specified. After that, you will have what you need.”

As you specified. He had specified it an hour ago, underground, to a man and two guards.

“And you’ll have what you need.”

“Correct.” A pause opened. He waited it out.

“The subjects display capabilities beyond conventional enhancement. This complicates elimination.”

“CADENS?”

“No. CADENS observes. They do not enhance.” He had known that name for three hours. She used it without any weight at all.

“The source of their abilities is irrelevant to your objectives. Focus on elimination, Mr. Kain. Not comprehension.” He had not asked the question she was answering.

“You know something about them.”

“I know they must be removed. That is sufficient.” The rings lay still against the blotter.

“I’ll have Jericho proceed as planned,” he said.

“Excellent. I will contact you in forty-eight hours. The Heart will be functional. Your trap will be ready. The subjects will be eliminated.”

The call ended without a click. The line stopped carrying her. He set the receiver down and filed her where he filed all capital with unclear terms: usable, until the terms arrived. Then he restarted the footage. Fifteenth viewing. The figure went up the face of the concrete, and the image smeared where it always smeared, and the roofline arrived too early.

Problematic, she had called them on the first call, months ago. He was coming to think that was an understatement.
