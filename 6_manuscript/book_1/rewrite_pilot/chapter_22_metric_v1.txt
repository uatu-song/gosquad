The order reached her twice, thin on Ben’s apron cam and a half second late on the house system, and Tess heard both from two blocks east.

“Open fire!”

Twelve weapons at the near door, more in the corridor behind it, one man at the balcony window the office was not supposed to have. The west wall was mostly glass, and past it lay thirty feet of nothing, then gravel, then the fountain at the bottom of the drive. Between the bookcases on the long wall stood the plinth, and the thing from the vault sat lit inside its case on top of it, doing what it had done all night.

Leah reached the same conclusion first and solved it sideways. She got behind the wheeled chair and ran it out into the middle of the floor, and Harding Kain went with it, wrists zip-tied and turning slowly under his own chandelier, a nine-figure obstruction nobody had authorization to shoot through. The first volley went high, and the ceiling came down across the feed like snow.

Whoever hung the cameras in that office had hung them for a man who enjoyed being looked at. Twelve angles and every one of them pointed inward. He sat at the center of all of them now, revolving at whatever speed the casters gave him, and the room would come past him a piece at a time whether he wanted it or not.

“Gloom Girl,” Ben said from behind the desk, which was oak and the only honest cover in the room. “We’re pinned. Need an exit.”

She had one and it was bad. Four trips minimum, one body each, every arrival slower than the last, and the room still full of men when she came back for the last. Jericho lay out cold on a linen shelf. On her third laptop the copy bar sat at thirty seconds and declined to hurry.

“Copy’s not done,” she said, and her thumb pressed into the corner of the crate hard enough to leave a mark. “Hold them off.”

Victor tipped the champagne bucket. Goggles came out from under the catering linen. A dozen men lost the room in one breath, and only three did not.

Leah’s heels came off first, and they came off as ordnance. She got a hand down and took one by the strap on the way, put it through the eyebrow of the man nearest the door, and sent the second after him into the smoke without waiting to watch it land. She fought the rest of the night barefoot in the gown. She had told them the slit was a tactical decision and nobody at the table had believed her, and Tess was going to owe her about that for a long time.

Battlea took the chair apart and kept two armrests. The second one went back over her shoulder without a glance, into a hand that was already open for it. That was the part Tess would think about afterward. The hand had been open before the armrest got there.

After that the smoke gave Tess nothing to see by, and she read the fight like you read a storm from indoors: a rifle going up and away, a shoulder finding a sternum, men walking into furniture they could not see and into people they could see considerably less.

The audio carried better than the picture. She put the house feed in her good ear and listened to a dozen professionals lose an argument with a room. Chairs went over. Somebody called a corner and somebody on the far side of it called the same corner back. A man walked into a bookcase and said sorry to it. Under all of that the team made no sound whatsoever, and that was the trick, and it had cost four years of somebody standing at the mats saying do it again, softer. In a room full of smoke the guards were the only thing generating noise, and the noise told Ben and Victor where each of them was standing.

Ben came out from behind the desk and set his feet, and put his left forearm out flat at hip height, and held it there like a rail he expected somebody to use. Leah used it. She came at him out of the gray with her hand already reaching, took the forearm at a dead run, and he turned his hips into her and let go. She crossed that office in one continuous piece of movement, at a speed her own legs could not have produced. Tess had watched her do it on a flat track with a helmet on and a whistle going. She had never once seen it done in a gown.

The man at the far end took a bare heel in the sternum and the wall behind him did the rest.

Ben worked the room from the door end and Victor from the window end, and they met in the middle and stayed there, back to back, turning like one slow animal. There was nothing improvised about it. They had a name for that shape and had walked it on tape and drilled it in a gym with no smoke in it, and in the smoke it looked no different.

She should have felt better than she did. They were holding. They were holding without her and without Gale and without whatever it was that had been doing this for them since the docks, and they were good, and it turned out that had its own cold under it. On the third laptop the bar moved a little and remained in no hurry.

She kept the center camera up the whole time, because he was the center of it. He was not frightened. He sat in his chair inside his own smoke with his chin up, following the noise around the room, and his face did what her father’s face did at a car dealership. He thought he was watching equipment. Somebody had sold these people something, and he would find out who, and then he would buy two.

Smoke buys a minute. It had never bought more than that, and every person in that office who had planned anything had planned for the end of it.

The man at the balcony window had spent that minute finding a firing position and had just found one. Ben ran three steps up the paneling and came off it sideways, and a guard went through the west glass and stayed audible longer than she wanted.

The hole he left was four feet wide. Smoke poured out of the office and up the face of the house, and on her screens the room resolved into furniture and a dozen men getting their eyes back. Their advantage had been the weather. They had just broken the window it lived behind.

And they kept winning.

That was the thing Tess could not find a shelf for. They were in the clear now, in a lit room, against men who could see them and had rifles and expensive training. Three people with two chair arms between them went on taking the office apart at the same rate.

Victor made a step out of his hands and Leah went up it to the top of the bookcase. She came off the bookcase into two men who were still looking at the floor where she had been. Ben threw a guard, and the throw was not an attack, it was delivery; Victor took receipt of him at the far end and set him down. Leah got a knee onto Ben’s shoulder and Ben stood up under her. She went over the desk from six feet in the air with the second armrest already swinging.

The room worked with them. Leah pulled a drape down over a man’s head and left him wearing his own window. Victor took an armrest to a knee, then to a wrist, then laid the man out on the carpet with something close to courtesy. Ben got a shoulder into a bookcase and let it settle the argument for the two men sheltering behind it. Somebody went hard into the plinth. The case on top jumped on its base and came down starred across one corner. The plinth stayed upright, and Tess clocked it and lost it inside a second.

None of it ran in a straight line. Ben’s shoulder opened a lane and Leah was inside the lane before it finished existing. Leah crossing pulled two men off Victor. He spent the seconds that bought him stripping a rifle off a third and putting it out through the hole in the west wall. Every weapon that came loose went the same way. Nobody on any of her screens held one longer than it took to throw it.

By then the men still standing had stopped closing. They had drifted to the walls with their backs against the paneling, and a couple of them were not aiming at anything any more.

Firas built this. He built it in a gym with sour mats and a whiteboard nobody could read, and tonight the whole of it was working at full size, on the one night he could not get up the stairs. Tess sat on a crate two blocks east with her hands shaking and was proud of them in a way that was difficult to hold.

On the center camera, Harding Kain stopped moving his head.

He had tracked all of it until then, chin swinging with the noise, a man at a demonstration keeping up. Now he sat still while the room went past him, and he was looking at one place, and the place was not the fight. It was the plinth between the bookcases with the light going in its case.

He looked at it for a long while. Tess read that as a rich man taking inventory in the middle of a burglary, and went back to the copy bar, which said eleven seconds.

“Battlea! Incoming!” Victor called it from the far end, and the fresh ones came through both doors clear-eyed, taking their time about the angles.

A guard put his forehead into Victor’s face. Victor went down on one knee in the open. Leah got an arm around the nearest man’s throat and his rifle neatly into her free hand, and walked him to the wall, which bought ten seconds and cost her both hands.

“Gale?”

Nothing came back. Gale had been dark since the docks and everyone in that office knew it, and Leah asked anyway, because four years of asking does not switch off in a day.

Ben swept the roofline through the broken glass and found gutter and chimney and cold slate. Then he remembered. Nobody had known where she was since the water.

“Little help?” Leah’s hostage was working his chin down toward her forearm and getting somewhere with it.

Ben went under a man with a knife. The knife came up. Victor stood cornered against the plinth with a dozen barrels finding him, and there was nothing left to try. He put his hands out where they could be seen and came off the plinth into the open, three steps, because that was the only thing left that the barrels wanted.

Out on the carpet one of the new men got around at last to the obvious service. He got a knife under the ties at the back of the chair and sawed, with his eyes on the fight the whole time. It took him a moment. He went back to the line without once having looked at the man’s face. Kain did not get up. He put his freed hands flat on his knees and went on looking at the plinth.

Then the lights died.

All of them at once, the house and the lawn and the floods around the fountain, and the moon over the roofline seemed to dim with them out of politeness. One second of total dark.

She had left the crate two minutes before that. The first jump was the long one, structure roof to the boxwood on the west lawn, and it went as everything had gone since the docks: the finger up, the eyes set, a pause where no pause used to live, then the world stepping a foot to the left without her. She came down badly in the hedge with her stomach still two blocks east. The kitchen door took the second and the service stair took the third. The fourth put her at the wire-glass window of the electrical room, the fifth put her inside it, and she pulled every breaker in the box by feel.

The sixth took her to the linen shelf. The seventh took her and Jericho three blocks east to a loading dock. She left him on his side with his glasses folded into his shirt, and came off that dock tasting pennies. The eighth put her in the office, eight feet up, on the crown molding.

She crouched there with the goggles on while the second ran out. Below her the room lay open in green and gray, and she read it like a wall: the knife nine feet down and two feet left, the desk, the chandelier chain, the whole room a route instead of a fight. She had spent four years going around things, and from up here going around was the answer.

There were two things down there and one second to spend on them. The knife was the near one. The other was past the desk, where a man who had sat in a chair all night was on his hands and knees at the plinth with his face turned up into the light.

She picked Ben.

She came off the molding onto the man with the knife and put him into the carpet with her whole weight, and the ninth had Ben out from under him and across the room before his hands understood they were empty. The chandelier she did not strictly need, and took anyway. It hung there and swung where she wanted, and the tenth dropped her off it onto the two men under the doorway, who went down without ever seeing what arrived. Then Battlea, off the wall and onto the desk, her hostage left blinking. Eleven.

The emergency circuit came up yellow and half-hearted. The chair by the desk lay on its side and empty, the ties cut through, and on the plinth the light had changed color while she was not watching it.

“Jericho?” Ben asked, on his feet with his back to the window.

“Got him,” Tess said. “Data drive?”

“Downloaded.” He had it closed in his fist.

“Then we’re leaving.”

“Hands up!” The near door had re-formed into a dozen men with weapons leveled.

There were two ways out of that office. One of them ran through those guns. The other was a four-foot hole in the west glass with thirty feet of cold night under it and a fountain still running this late in the year. She took Ben’s collar in one hand and Leah’s in the other.

“Now!”

They went through together and the night took them. Fifteen feet down she left them and came back up through the glass for Victor, who had stopped at the frame with the drop in front of him, doing the honest math of a man with nothing left in his hands.

“Trust me,” she said, and took his sleeve, and jumped them both off the sill.

After that it was only parkour, which is the art of refusing the straight line down. She did not stop the fall, because nobody stops a fall. She bent it. Two jumps put the four of them sideways across the drive, the next took the worst of the speed off the angle, the last dropped them into three feet of green water at a slant, and the fountain did what water does with anything handed to it at a slant.

She surfaced last, ears ringing, mouth full of pennies, hands not willing. Victor got her over the rim.

The alley ran three blocks east, and they reached it at a limp, soaking, in the first cold snap of the year. It had arrived that week and meant it.

“What the hell just happened?” Victor asked the alley generally.

“Gloom Girl happened,” Ben said.

“That was insane,” Victor said, and sat down heavily on somebody’s milk crate.

“That was fun,” Tess said.

Leah wrung a pint of fountain out of the ruined red dress and laughed.

Firas came into the alley mouth on foot, slower than she had ever seen him move, like a man carrying something breakable. One flat hand stayed pressed over his ribs the whole way. He had walked it. Walking was the only way any of them ever went.

“Everyone alive?”

“Mostly,” Ben said.

“Jericho?”

“At the extraction point,” Tess said. She got the words out in the right order, privately proud of it. “Unconscious but breathing. Got all his research too.”

The back door of the row house opened, and a woman in her sixties came out into the wet in a bathrobe and slippers, arms folded tightly.

“Go Squad?”

Nobody answered her. In four years nobody had ever had to.

“I knew it. I told my daughter you operated in this neighborhood.” She got a phone out of the bathrobe pocket. “Can I get a picture? She won’t believe me otherwise.”

“Ma’am, we really need to—”

“Please? Just one? You saved my nephew last month. The thing with the guys and the—” Her hand made a small circle in the air that finished the sentence for her. “You know.”

Leah crossed the alley in the wrecked dress, stood next to the woman, and put her head close while the phone came up unsteadily and did what phones do.

“Thank you,” she said, and gave the rest of it to all five of them. “For everything you do. We see you. All of us. We see you.”

Then she went back in. The screen door shut, and the latch eventually caught on the second try.

“That was weird,” Victor said.

“That was nice,” Tess said.

“Then let’s get out of here before—”

The light came up behind them, red and gold, across the whole east face of the house. Tess felt it in her back teeth and in the marrow of both shins, and she knew it, because it was hers. Space folding, run backward: an arrival with no leaving in front of it.

“What—”

Harding Kain came out through the front wall of his own house at a walk. The stone stayed stone and he came through it regardless, without a door and without a hole. Tess knew what she was looking at. That was space folded and let go, the same trick her own body did, done to a building by a thing sold to her as a clock. The Heart sat sunk in the middle of his chest, red, then gold, then red, patient about it. His eyes flickered rapidly between one state and another. He looked up at the sky for a while, unhurried, a man checking the weather before a drive. Then he turned his head three blocks east and found them in the dark, and smiled, and walked off through the side of an apartment building.

“We need to call someone,” Ben said.

“Who?” Leah asked.

“The cops? The cops who work for him?” Tess had her phone out already, holding it in both wet hands with absolutely nowhere to send it.

It rang. Unknown number.

“Hello?”

“No better place than here,” Ruth said in her ear.

Four years she had answered that call in a gym and on a rooftop and once with her mouth full, and it had never made her want to sit on the ground. Gloom Girl was a name she had picked at nineteen to be funny about the worst thing in her life. Standing in an alley in wet armor with her hands shaking, she decided she wanted a different one.

Ruth did not wait for the response.

“I saw what just happened. Get somewhere safe. I’m sending help.”

The line went. Then the air developed an edge it had not had, and something the size of a bus stopped being invisible forty feet up, and the engine pitch her ribs had been reading arrived at her ears. A ramp came down. Ruth stood at the top of it in full tactical gear, matte black and armored, none of which anybody had seen her wear.

“Need a ride?”
