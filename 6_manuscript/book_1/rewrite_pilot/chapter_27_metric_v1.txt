They came off the buildings at him from four directions at once. One off the bank cornice, one off the fire escape above the pharmacy, one out of the alley mouth, one up over the roof of the dead bus. The answer came down ahead of them, screamed raggedly and out of time with itself, the other half of whatever the girl had put into the air.

“No better time than now!”

Kain did not adjust his stance. Five people dropping at something twenty-three feet tall priced out as suicide, and he had watched men buy it before, generally at the end and generally cheap.

The girl stood under it in black armor he had not seen her put on. It sat badly on her. Too wide at the shoulders, the sleeves eating her hands, the harness slung low, the collar gone black where the blood had run down into it. Her feet were still bare. Somewhere in the noise she had acquired a costume. He set it down without turning it over.

Then the drop stopped behaving like a drop. Between the second-floor windows and the pavement the five of them gathered speed the fall had never given them, and this time he could see where it was coming from. The girl swayed. Her hands shook inside the long sleeves, her jaw worked, and her breathing had gone shallow and quick and visible from forty feet away. Whatever this cost her, she was paying it openly. Then the Go Squad hit the street as blurs.

Battlea took Ogden at the hip like a car coming through an intersection, and Ogden left the ground. Crimson Sable landed on Philips with the rebar coming down two-handed, and the asphalt broke cleanly in a ring around them both. Edgar read Night Knight’s line and moved off it correctly, and Night Knight corrected mid-strike at an angle no shoulder should offer and caught him centre mass. Orlansky got a guard up. Nightingale went through it as though it were a door somebody had left unlatched.

Gloom Girl hit nobody. She went out of the air halfway down, arrived neatly on the sidewalk beside a man in a t-shirt who had no business standing there, raised her middle finger at the whole intersection, and took them both out of it.

Four down. Five standing in a ring around the girl in the middle, and the girl had not thrown a punch.

“What are you?”

She gave him nothing. He watched her fail to answer and understood that she had nothing left to answer with.

Ogden came up out of his crater and Crimson Sable’s staff found his temple on the way. Philips crawled toward the nearest pair of legs and went down under a low sweep, and Gloom Girl arrived on top of him out of clear air. Edgar surged at Nightingale and did not make three steps; they closed on him before he had chosen his stride, Night Knight into a restraint hold, Crimson Sable following it in, the medic left standing behind them. Orlansky was last. The young one had watched all of it happen and swung wild anyway, and all five converged on him, each of them opening the next one’s line. It was competent work. They were taking people down rather than killing them, carefully, and he found the care faintly sentimental.

Then it was over, and they were standing there breathing hard and bleeding in several places and alive. Ozone. Copper. Something burnt. Pavement dust hung over the intersection and would not settle.

The light came up out of Ogden first. Sickly green, the shade of the thing sunk in Kain’s chest, and it left the body the way marrow leaves a bone. Ogden’s back arched off the asphalt, held, then went slack, and what lay there afterward was smaller than the man had been. The light crossed the pavement like iron filings crossing to a magnet. It hit Kain wet. It was organic, and it went in.

Philips, then Edgar, then Orlansky. Four streams converging on one body, and the air chilled as each of them passed.

He had cut the Heart and distributed the fragments himself. He had made four weapons out of four men and put them in the street to be broken open by whoever proved strong enough, and the Go Squad had done it beautifully. There was no pleasure in it worth the name. There was the flat satisfaction of delivery on a thing already paid for. The Heart came back together behind his sternum, whole.

Nightingale saw it before the others did.

“NO! STOP!”

Her arm was out toward Crimson Sable, who still stood over Philips with the rebar raised. There was nothing to stop. The light was already moving and it would move whether anybody swung again or not.

“You’re feeding him!”

Somebody in the ring said the other thing, low, without lifting their head.

“We killed them.”

He could not tell which of them it was. He did not much care to work it out.

The husks lay where they had been dropped, four sets of torn uniform with the men no longer in them. Before he had opened those men up they had been officers, with names and mortgages and wives who packed lunches. The Go Squad were looking at that now, and coming visibly apart over it, and he watched a team lose the fight they had won three seconds earlier. It was inefficient of them. Grief is a luxury good, and they were buying it in the worst hour of their lives.

“Thank you,” he said.

The words went out slowly over the intersection with weather in them. He meant them entirely, and he was polite about it, because courtesy is the cheapest instrument in the case and he had never understood why a man would leave it shut.

“I was wondering how to reclaim those fragments.”

He came up past twenty-five feet while they stood there. Twenty-eight. Thirty-one. The pharmacy sign went under his elbow and the second floor of the bank went under his ribs, thirty-five, thirty-eight, forty, and the growth ran on past the point where more of him did him any good. His skin had gone clear enough to read by. The bones stood in it plainly, dark and shifting, and the Heart pulled at him harder than it had an hour ago, taking its fee out of the muscle nearest it. He had priced that before he ever put it in his chest. He had decided it was worth the fee.

Below him, in the middle of her people, the girl swayed. She was drowning in armor built for a bigger body, the sleeves over her knuckles, the collar dark and shining wetly, her bare feet planted apart on broken glass. She had made five ordinary people into weapons and thrown them at him, and every one of them was still alive down there. Nobody said the rest of it out loud. She had saved all of them, and she had fed him doing it.
