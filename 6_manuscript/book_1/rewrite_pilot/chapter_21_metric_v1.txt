The first rule of losing your superpowers was that nobody talked about losing their superpowers.

They had been not-talking about it downstairs since the docks, so Tess came up here. The room over the Academy office held a mattress, a duffel, and nine energy-drink cans. She left the cans in frame deliberately. She squared the phone in the tripod. “Hey guys, it’s your girl, Gloomy. If you’re watching this, I’m either dead or I finally got the guts to post it, and I genuinely don’t know which is more terrifying.”

She sat on the mattress. Nobody was coming up. “It’s been one day since the docks. Since the tank cop almost killed us. Since Gale disappeared and our powers just . . . stopped working.” She let it sit two seconds and then ruined it. “Well, most of them.”

She stood and raised her middle finger at the lens, slowly, like a magician showing both sides of a coin, and aimed her eyes at the door frame. Nothing happened. It used to be instant. Then the room moved a step to the left and she was at the door with the tripod six feet away, her stomach arriving late. She held the jamb until the floor agreed to be a floor. Outside the window a drone hung at roof height with its lights off, the way they always did, and it stayed there a while after she had stopped moving. “See? Mine still works. Knight thinks it’s because mine was always tech-based—FAERIS drones doing the actual teleporting. Which means maybe all of it was tech. Maybe we were never actually super. Just regular people with really expensive invisible support.”

She sat back down. “That’s what’s breaking everyone. Not that we lost our powers. That we maybe never had them to begin with.”

Watching people was the one skill she owned that predated the mask, and she turned it on her own team now, quickly and mercilessly. “Sable’s strength? Gone. He’s dealing with it by being pissed at literally everything. Battlea’s speed? Gone. She’s gone quiet, which is way worse than when she’s yelling. Knight’s jumps? Gone. He’s over-planning every detail because if he stops thinking he’ll have to start feeling and I think that might actually kill him.”

Then the one she had been circling. “And Gale . . . Gale sent one text saying she was clear and then went dark. Nothing since the docks. She won’t answer and we don’t know if she’s hurt or hiding or—” Her thumb found the stop button and did not press it. “Can’t think about that. Focus on what we can control.”

The list of things they could control had gotten very short. Firas had made one anyway. “Firas suggested we rescue Dr. Jericho. The artifact expert who got kidnapped from the museum. The guy who was researching the same artifacts that gave Kain his powers. The Tamois Heart. Turned him into whatever that thing at the docks was.” She pulled her knees up. “If anyone knows what’s happening to us, it’s Jericho. Problem is, Firas is downstairs right now bleeding through his bandages because he won’t admit his chest wound isn’t healed. He suggested the plan, worked out half of it, but he can barely walk. So it’s just us. Four people. Three without powers. Going after a billionaire with private security and whatever cosmic bullshit Kain’s got now.”

“Great plan, right? Super tactical. I’m sure nothing will go wrong.” The laugh she hung on the end of it did not survive the trip out. “If I don’t make it back . . . tell my dad I tried. Tell him I was doing something good even if I sucked at it. And tell him there’s a photo folded inside that picture frame on his desk. Maybe he should actually look at it sometime.”

She stopped. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, hard. She did not say his name. She never did. Folded behind her school photo in that frame was another one, both her parents in it and a cake. She had put it there years ago. He had never mentioned it. “Team’s meeting downstairs in five. Time to find out if we’re brave or just idiots.” She reached for the phone. “Probably both.”

The camera came downstairs with her, because holding it gave her hands a job. Documentary mode. Nobody objected, which told her more than anything anybody said.

Firas had the laptop and a chair he was obviously pretending not to need. Victor sat on a plyo box, taking each item out of the bag and putting it carefully back. Leah had the couch and her arms crossed. She had not spoken since breakfast, and Tess had read enough rooms to know precisely what that meant on Leah. It was not attitude. It was terror with absolutely nowhere to go.

“Okay,” Ben said, and turned the laptop around. A house assembled itself in gray, drone-mapped. “Kain’s hosting a gala event at his mansion tomorrow night. Wealthy donors, politicians, the usual corrupt assholes. Heavy security on the perimeter, but inside? Just catering staff and house staff.” He set three fingers on the schematic and moved them one by one. “Three entry points. Front door—obviously not happening. Service entrance here, kitchen access. And loading dock basement entrance here.”

Firas leaned in, and the lean cost him something across the ribs, and Tess watched him spend it silently. “We need someone inside distracting Kain,” he said, and he was already looking at the couch. “Someone who can pass as a wealthy donor. Someone who can keep him occupied while the rest of us search for Jericho.”

“No.”

“Leah—” Ben tried.

“I said no.”

“You’re the only one who can pull it off.” Victor delivered that to the gear bag, which was plainly cowardice, and Tess filmed it anyway.

“Pull off what, exactly? Playing dress-up for a billionaire creep while you guys search his mansion? Hard pass.”

“We need you,” Firas said.

“You need me to put on a tight dress and smile while Kain ogles me for an hour. That’s what you need.”

Nobody rushed to fill that. It was accurate and everybody heard how accurate it was, and Firas took the weight of it and declined, visibly, to set it down anywhere clever. “Yeah. That’s what we need. And I hate asking. But Jericho might be our only shot at understanding what’s happening to us. What happened to Gale. Why our powers are gone.”

Leah studied the ceiling. “Fine,” she said, and uncrossed her arms. “But I’m picking the dress. And if he touches me, I’m breaking his hand.”

“Deal,” Firas said.

“Hey.” Tess lowered the phone, which she hardly ever did. “You’re not just bait. You’re getting us in. You’re the reason we have access. That’s not nothing.”

“It’s also not punching cops in the face, which is what I’d rather be doing.”

“We can punch cops after we rescue Jericho.”

Victor had come in with the zip ties and had evidently forgotten what they were for. “Wow.”

“I’m just saying—”

“I know what you’re saying. Don’t.”

“You look good. That’s all I’m saying.”

“I look like I’m about to seduce a Bond villain.”

Victor considered it honestly. “Well,” he said. “Technically you are.”

Ben came through with a comms case, mouth already open. “We need to go over the comms plan—” He stopped. “Oh.”

“Yeah,” Victor agreed.

Leah turned from the mirror with her weight on one hip, and Tess got all of it, and would eventually cut two frames after the eyebrow. “Oh. Everyone get a good look? We done objectifying me?”

Firas was in the doorway by then, one shoulder on the frame, standing at a cost nobody would itemize. “You’re not being objectified,” he said. “You’re being weaponized. There’s a difference.”

Leah breathed out slowly through her nose. “I hate that that makes me feel better.”

“We work with what we have,” Firas said. “Right now what we have is Knight’s planning, Sable’s muscle, Gloom Girl’s teleportation, and your ability to make Kain forget we exist. That’s the play.”

“If this goes wrong and I die in this dress, I’m haunting all of you.”

“Noted,” Ben said.

“I may be fine with that,” Victor said, and took a shoe for it.

“Okay,” Firas said. He pushed off the frame and stood unsupported, because some sentences could not be delivered leaning. “Let’s rob a billionaire.” He gave them the call quietly, the way he had given it for four years. “No better place than here,”

“No better time than now.”

It came back from all of them at once. Then Leah turned to the mirror and said the whole thing herself, both halves, to her own face.

“No better place than here. No better time than now.”

Tess kept filming after the rest of them turned away.

The parking structure east of the mansion was empty after nine. Tess had its northwest corner: three laptops on a milk crate, and a sightline to the mansion’s front elevation. Four feeds: Ben’s apron cam and Victor’s, both dark and jouncing; the house system, ninety dollars and one of Ben’s favors; and Leah’s clutch, a pinhole behind the clasp, currently showing a car ceiling.

Twenty minutes to showtime, and Tess did what she always did with them. The bodega clip was still running: a ceiling camera at Rodriguez’s Bodega on Morrison Street, a blur in the doorway, a man with a bat sitting down abruptly beside the chip rack. She read the comments the way other people take vitamins. “These are the real police.” Under that, “Squad saved my cousin last week. We owe them.” Under that, “Why aren’t we funding these people?”

Somebody had cut a compilation, scored to strings. “Go Squad Saves Caledonia - 47 Confirmed Interventions This Month.”

@CaledoniaNights had the one that got her: Mr. Rodriguez holding out a paper cup, Battlea half turned away, caught taking it. “Stopped a robbery. Wouldn’t take payment. I gave her coffee anyway. #GoSquad #CaledoniaHeroes”

None of it was true this morning. Some of it, apparently, had never been true. The city loved them honestly for a power they could not use anymore and possibly had never owned. She was the one who would have to write that post.

The last was from @WestSideWatch. “Go Squad spotted entering vehicle near Academy. Think something’s happening tonight. Stay safe, Squad. We got your backs.”

The reply count under it read 47 and climbed like a fare meter. She screenshotted it to show Leah after, because Leah would need it after. On the clutch feed the hired car swung through the gates and stopped.

“You’re on,” Tess said. “Looking good, by the way.”

“Feeling like bait,” Leah said, barely moving her mouth.

“Hot bait.”

“Not helping.”

Two nights back Ben had laid it out like arithmetic. “Battlea enters through the front as Sophie Clement, wealthy French donor. Sable and I go in through the service entrance as catering staff. Gloom Girl stays on the parking structure two blocks out, monitors comms and security feeds.” He tapped the basement. “We’re looking for Dr. Jericho. Artifact expert. Last seen at the museum before Kain’s people grabbed him.”

“If he’s anywhere, he’s in the basement research level. That’s where Kain keeps his collection.” That was Firas, from the chair.

“What if he’s not there?” Tess had asked.

“Then we get whatever intel we can and extract. Hard drives, documents, anything that tells us what Kain’s doing with those artifacts.”

Now Victor’s apron cam swung through a service door held open by a man with a clipboard who never looked up, and two caterers walked into a billionaire’s house on two rented aprons and a stack of warming trays. “We’re in. That was . . . easier than expected.”

“Don’t say that,” Ben said. “You’ll jinx it.”

Tess had filmed the loadout while Ben walked the table. “Thermal goggles under the tablecloth. Smoke grenades in the champagne ice bucket. Data drives in the bread basket. Zip ties in the napkin dispenser. Everything hidden in plain sight.”

“We’ve done the catering staff thing before. Guards never look twice at waiters.” Victor had said that cracking knuckles, taking comfort where comfort was cheap.

The kitchen was four cooks and a sous chef screaming at a salamander, and nobody had time to be curious. On Ben’s feed the service stair went down past the wine room, and the light went industrial. The corridor was concrete and cold, with a door at the end of it like the front of a bank. “Vault should be at the end of this corridor,” Ben said.

Firas had put the cloner in his hand the night before, holding it the way he held anything he built. “RFID cloner. Get close enough to any guard’s security badge, this’ll clone the signal. Should open most of the electronic locks including the basement vault.”

“Should?” Victor had asked.

“Ninety percent chance. If there’s biometric locks too, you’ll need to improvise.”

On the feed the cloner’s light went green, and the panel with it. The door did not move. “Shit,” Victor said. “It’s not working.”

“Biometric?” Ben was already on one knee at the panel.

Victor had the thermal goggles up. His feed washed out and came back in false color, and Tess leaned over the crate to see it: heat where heat had no business being, a long cold room, a shape in the middle of it putting out warmth on a slow regular pulse. Nothing about the shape was a person. “Something’s in there,” Victor said. “Artifacts, maybe. But we can’t get in without an actual guard’s fingerprint or retinal scan or whatever.”

“So we improvise,” Ben said.

Victor held up both hands and looked at them. There was a whole day in it. “My powers are more, like, momentum based . . . ?”

Nobody laughed. Ben put his palm flat on the vault door, briefly, the way you check a hood for heat. Whatever sat behind it was warm and there was a lot of it, spread out in rows the way a collection sits on shelves, and none of it was moving. Cases, then. The intel had promised a research level and given them a warehouse instead, which meant the work had gone somewhere else and taken the man with it. “Yeah, this isn’t happening. We need to find another way. Let’s search the rest of the basement.”

They took eleven minutes on the rest. Storage, a wine cellar, empty offices with the chairs still wrapped. No cell. No cot. No Jericho. “He’s not down here,” Victor said.

“Then we go upstairs. Get what intel we can from his office.”

The clutch feed had been climbing the steps. Kain came down his own staircase in shirtsleeves, doing her the favor of being early. “Ms. Clement,” he said, and took her hand and kept it past any schedule Tess recognized. “Welcome. I’m sorry the place is in such a state. We weren’t expecting you for a few more hours.”

“I should have called ahead. But I was in the area and thought, why not?”

“Not at all, not at all.” He held on a beat longer than politely necessary. “It’s a pleasure anytime.”

Leah got her fingers back and turned toward the clasp. “He’s so gross.”

She had asked the practical question the night before. “So I just . . . keep him occupied? Talk about rich people stuff?”

“Art,” Firas had said. “He collects it. Especially ancient artifacts. Egyptian, Greek, Amazigh. If you mention you’re interested in antiquities, he’ll talk your ear off. Guy loves showing off his collection.”

“Great. I know nothing about art.”

“You know it when you see it, right?” Tess had offered, which felt helpful for a second.

“That’s porn.”

The clutch came out onto the west lawn, past caterers laying a hundred and forty covers, and stopped at nine feet of bronze Harding Kain gazing nobly at nothing. “It’s . . . magnificent,” Leah said, in an accent that was holding.

“I know,” Kain said, the way other men say the time. “It’s nothing, really. You should see my place in the south of Marseille. Ever been?”

Ben’s whisper came flat and fast from a linen closet downstairs. “He’s fishing. That’s in Sophie Clement’s cover story. He might’ve read up on her.”

“Often,” Leah said. “But I prefer to stay in Paris, where I can keep a close eye on my business.”

“A woman who understands priorities. I like that.” He put a hand on the small of her back, and the frame jumped where her arm went tight and then, deliberately, loose. “Come. Let me show you my office. I have something that might interest a woman of your . . . refined tastes.”

“Wait, that worked? He’s taking you to the office?”

“Lead the way,” Leah said.

“Second floor office,” Tess relayed, hands flat on the crate.

The office ran the whole west end of the house, and the window ran the office. On the far wall hung a painting of a monument Tess did not recognize, with a long bright tear opened in the sky above it. “What a view you must have.”

“To see the sunset,” Kain said. “Very nice choice, isn’t it?”

Ben came onto the net. “We’re coming up. Stall him.”

A man sat at a workstation in the corner with three monitors and a cold coffee, older, thin, wearing a cardigan in a house kept at seventy-two degrees. “Dr. Jericho, take a break. We have a guest.”

“But the resonance calibration—”

“Can wait,” Kain said, and nothing in his delivery moved, which was how you knew. “Give us the room.”

“The temporal stability is still fluctuating. If I leave now—”

“Ten minutes,” Kain said. He gave the second half to Leah, smiling, sharing it like a joke about staff. “Then you can get back to making me a god.”

Jericho went. The door closed. Nine seconds later Tess heard a soft double thud in her right ear, hardly louder than a book landing, then Ben breathing hard, and she let out air she had been holding since the gates.

“So,” Kain said, crossing to a glass chamber alone on a plinth. “You mentioned an interest in antiquities?”

“I did,” Leah said. The chamber lit as he neared it, and Tess’s screen bloomed white and then found its level, and even through a pinhole two blocks out it was hard to look at directly. Red, then gold, then red, on a rhythm slower than a heartbeat and considerably more patient. “This . . . what is that?”

“The Tamois Heart,” Kain said. He set one ringed knuckle on the glass above it, fondly, like a man tapping a fish tank. “The Amazigh worshipped it as divine. A gift from gods. But it’s technology. Dr. Jericho has been researching its properties. Learning how to replicate it. Scale it. Imagine—power like this, mass-produced. Distributed to whoever I choose.”

Tess wrote the word on her hand. Replicate. He did not want to be the one who had it. He wanted to be the supplier, and he said so out loud to a woman he had known eleven minutes.

Leah moved. “Sorry about this.”

“What—”

The clutch went sideways and showed carpet, then wall, then Kain’s face at an angle no photographer had been allowed. She had his wrist behind his shoulder blade and her knee in his back. She got him down unhurriedly. She broke nothing, and Tess watched her decide not to.

The door opened on Ben and Victor in catering whites, and Ben went past Kain to the workstation. “Holy shit. This is it. The artifact from the museum. And all his research.” Drives came out of the bread basket, three of them, and the monitors filled with directory trees. “Resonance patterns, cellular integration protocols, temporal manipulation theories . . .”

Victor had stopped a yard short of the plinth, hand half raised. “That thing’s . . . it’s doing something. I can feel it.”

“Don’t touch it,” Ben said, without turning around.

Kain worked his cheek carefully off the carpet. “Who are you people?”

“Concerned citizens,” Victor told him, kneeling to run the zip ties. “And we’re taking Jericho and all this research.”

“You have no idea who you’re robbing—”

“TRIOMF executive, presidential candidate, corrupt billionaire experimenting with cosmic artifacts,” Leah said, from directly above him.

Victor pulled the last tie tight. “Yeah, we know.”

Ben read the summary with his thumb on the eject. “Got it. Research files, ledgers, everything. Including proof the police chief is on your payroll.”

Tess had already switched screens. The house system carried twelve cameras and eight went busy at once. Stairwells. The service corridor. Two men crossing the lawn past the bronze at a jog, four more moving steadily behind them.

“We need to get Jericho and go,” Victor said.

“Wait—I’m seeing security mobilizing. Multiple guards heading to the second floor. Did someone—”

On the clutch feed Kain’s shoe lay flat against the leg of the plinth, on a plate the size of a light switch. His heel was still on it. “Shit,” Ben said.

Leah saw it too. “He called security.”

“How many?” Victor asked.

Tess counted the moving shapes twice, because the first count could not possibly be right. “All of them,” she said. “You need to get out NOW.”

Both office doors opened at once. Ben’s camera swung and filled with men. Twelve weapons at minimum, more along the corridor behind them, one at a balcony window the office was not supposed to have. Nobody in that room had powers. Tess sat on the parking structure two blocks away with four feeds and no way in, and watched every screen stop moving.
