The staging roof was four stories of cold tar paper with a clean line on the warehouse, and they had reached it the way they reached everything, on foot, over rooflines, in the dark. Ruth knelt at the parapet with the schematics folded on her thigh. Wind came off the water and went through the armor like it wasn’t there. She had pulled enough winter shifts in her life to know the trick, which was to quit negotiating with cold and simply itemize it. Ben crouched beside her and delivered the tip from memory, flat as a chart note.

“Weapons cache in warehouse district. District 7, near the docks. Kain’s people moving product tonight, 2100 hours. Minimal security. Window’s tight.”

“Thoughts?” Ruth kept her eyes on the warehouse while she asked it.

“Fits the pattern. Kain’s been moving operations since the warehouse raid. Makes sense he’d use the docks.” Ben laid it out as settled fact, which was how his precinct contact always sold things, and the man had been right about enough of them that nobody in the room asked twice.

Leah lay stretched along the parapet with the binoculars. “Three heat signatures inside. No vehicles in the lot yet. If they’re moving product, we’re early.”

“Good,” Victor agreed, cracking each knuckle in turn. “Hit them during setup. Before they’re organized.”

“I like it when they’re not organized.” That was Tess, hood up against the wind.

Ruth ran the assignments the way she ran a board of incoming trauma, worst first and nearest second. Victor and Ben would take the loading dock. Tess and Leah would hold the roof. She would keep the perimeter and call it from comms, because somebody had to see the whole patient. Twenty minutes of planning and it sat right. She checked her watch against the window and found nineteen to spare.

“Oh. Oh no.” Tess had her phone out, the screen lighting her chin blue.

“What?” Victor leaned in from her other side.

“Wait,” Ben said, looking over her shoulder. “How do they know our codenames?”

“There’s like . . . hundreds of posts using them. Battlea this, Night Knight that.” Leah had her own phone out now, thumb moving quickly.

“Did you—” Victor let the accusation finish itself.

“What? No!”

“Gloom Girl.” Leah turned her screen around as evidence.

“I didn’t leak anything,” Tess insisted, and then honesty caught up with her, roughly a block behind. “I just . . . may have used them. In a few posts. Months ago. Hypothetically.”

“Firas is going to love this,” Ruth said.

“Or kill me,” Tess muttered, pocketing the phone.

Ruth let them keep the laugh, because a laugh before an op was cheap sedation and she genuinely believed in sedation. The arithmetic underneath she kept for herself. Strangers had names for them now, and a named thing draws eyes, and eyes raise the price of every mistake. She noted it and filed it and went back to the window they were burning.

At forty minutes to the hour she brought them onto the net.

“Everyone check in,” Ruth said. “Comms clear?”

They came back crisply and one at a time, and under the check-ins ran the feeling she had privately quit auditing: the team was right tonight. Everyone half a step early, tight on her callouts, the way a good trauma bay gets ten minutes before a bad arrival, when the room already knows its shape. Whatever her own part in that was, she had finally stopped asking. It worked, and tonight working was the whole chart.

“No better place than here,” Ruth said, because Firas had built the call four years ago and traditions were load-bearing.

“No better time than now.” It came back from all of them at once, quietly and exactly in step.

“Moving in,” Victor reported, and the entry was clean: two shadows crossed the lot, took the loading dock door, and were gone inside it while the night stayed quiet enough that she could hear her own pulse under the earpiece.

“Clear inside,” Victor said. “Setting up position.”

Eight minutes to the window. The lot stayed empty, and empty had stopped feeling early.

“Something’s wrong,” Tess reported from the roof. “There’s nobody here. Warehouse is empty.”

Three heat signatures, and nobody home. Ruth trusted the patient over the chart, every time, in every building.

“Abort. Everyone out. Now.”

“Too late,” Ben answered from the dark.

The floodlights arrived all at once, three sides of the lot going white as an operating room. She counted because counting was the job: fifteen units at minimum, SWAT vans seeded among the cruisers, deployment already out and moving, every exit her plan had liked already spoken for. Nobody stages that in eight minutes. That force had been parked in the dark since before her team crossed its first rooftop, which meant the tip had never been intelligence. It had been an invitation, and she had RSVP’d for five.

“This is CCPD. You are surrounded. Exit the building with your hands visible.” The megaphone managed to sound bored.

“Shit,” Leah breathed.

“Smoke and scatter,” Ruth snapped. “Formation Delta. IR goggles. Move.”

The canisters went down and the lot became weather. She held her position and called the lanes, and the team threaded them exactly as drilled, four shadows moving through white on choreography they owned, crossing gaps the flashlights kept guessing at and kept getting wrong. All four out of the cordon clean, on training. She allowed herself one full second of professional pride, because a thousand hours of dark gym had just paid out at face value and the pride was earned.

Then her goggles died. Full charge, mid-sweep, dead glass. She toggled the power twice, the way you re-check a flat line before you believe it, and got nothing back. The GPS unit followed it down. The radio lived, barely, gone thin and scratchy.

“Anyone else losing equipment?” Tess sounded more puzzled than scared, which would not last.

“IR’s down,” Victor confirmed. “GPS too.”

“What the hell—” Victor again, and the rest of it went wherever it went.

“Powers are down,” Leah said, and the static could not hide what rode in on it. “I can’t—they’re just gone.”

Gone was nearly the wrong word for what Ruth heard after that; it was more like altitude, four strong people still moving and still trained but suddenly breathing thinner air, heavier inside their own bodies than they had been thirty seconds before. She caught it in the footfalls and in the half beat everything now landed behind. In herself she felt no change at all, and she had no idea what that meant, and no time to spend on it.

“You don’t need them. You’re trained. Stay focused.”

“Fall back to secondary positions,” Ruth ordered. “Stay in smoke. Use training. You’ve got this.”

They steadied, and she could actually hear it happen, which mattered more than whatever had been subtracted, because panic killed faster than anything else the night had shown her yet.

“Contact. North side. What the hell is that?” Ben called, and the smoke gave her nothing to confirm it with.

Through a seam in the smoke she found it. Seven feet of armored figure walked out of the floodlight wash at a stroll, entirely wrong in scale against the cruisers. An officer broke cover to run, and the figure caught him one-handed and threw him, and the man crossed fifteen feet of air and folded a patrol car door around himself. Her eye billed the landing automatically: scapula, ribs, spleen if he was unlucky. He stayed down, and nobody went to him.

“New player,” Ruth warned. “Enhanced. Real enhanced. Everyone stay clear.”

“It’s a goddamn tank!” Victor yelled, already flanking left with his staff up, because staying clear had never once been Victor’s discipline.

The Tank let him commit, took the staff on a forearm, and closed one hand over Victor’s wrist. The turn he made was small and terrible. Ruth read the arm the way she read films in a lightbox, elbow driven past its stop, shoulder rolling out of station, and then Victor was on the concrete and not using that arm to get up.

“Victor!” Tess screamed it from the roofline.

Leah was already in. Two textbook strikes with full hips behind both, and then she was through a stacked pallet and down among the broken boards, and the whole sequence had used less time than the naming of it.

Ben went low and never finished. The Tank lifted him by the throat one-handed, boots clear of the ground, and held him there like a man holding a clipboard he meant to read later.

“Put him down!” Ruth shouted, and she was off her perch and dropping through the fire escape before the sentence ended.

The Tank turned toward her and opened his hand. Ben hit the ground breathing. That was the trade on offer, and she took it.

The board sorted itself in one pass, the last clear thinking the night allowed her. Victor down, arm gone. Leah down, status unknown. Ben down, airway bruised at minimum. Police tightening from three sides, and one enhanced problem standing in the middle of her wounded. The math had only the one answer.

“Everyone fall back. Get clear. I’ll draw him off.”

“Ruth, no—” Tess again.

“That’s an order.”

She ran east and the docks took a quarter mile and she spent everything on it, lungs pricing every block, cold air coming down her throat like blades. Behind her he followed at his own pace, and the sound of him never fell away. The container yard let her into its steel canyons, right and left and right on memory and guess. Then the canyon quit, and there was no more east. Black water at the toe of her boots, breathing against the pilings. It would be forty degrees this month. Ten minutes to hypothermia for a strong swimmer, less in armor, and she had priced it before she finished stopping.

“Team, sound off. Everyone clear?”

Static.

“Anyone copy?”

The channel hissed, empty. Alone then at the edge of the water, with the sound of him coming through the containers, unhurried as a man walking his own property.

He came around the last stack at a walk, and under the yard lights he undid the helmet and lifted it off, and the face underneath was the worst thing the night had produced. Thirty or so, and clean-shaven. A face assembled from features nobody would swear to, offering nothing to remember. She would have lost it in a waiting room inside a week.

“Dr. Carter,” the Tank said.

Her name. She held her feet still and her face closed while it landed, because armor that knew her name changed the size of everything behind it.

“You should have stayed in the ER.”

“How do you know my name?” Ruth asked, and hated needing to.

“Everyone knows your name now.”

Which answered nothing. The city knew the codenames — Tess had seen to that months ago, and none of them were hers. Somebody had put a file together, and this man had read it.

He set the helmet on a bollard, taking his time, a man on shift with the job well in hand.

“Mr. Kain sends his regards. Says your research will be very valuable. Once you’re out of the way.”

Her research. Three years on a shelf, and it surfaced here at the edge of black water, in a hired mouth. She put it away carefully with everything else she could not use yet.

“You’re enhanced,” Ruth said. “Real enhanced. Not like us.”

“Finally figured that out?” He sounded mildly interested. “Your tricks stopped working the moment the EMP hit. Ours didn’t.”

An EMP, then. She set it beside the dead goggles and the thin radio and left it there, because it bought her nothing at this range.

“What did they give you?” Ruth asked, buying seconds with the only currency left. “What kind of enhancement?”

“The kind that matters.” He rolled his shoulders once, loosening them. “The kind that can’t be turned off with a magnet.”

“You know what happens next,” the Tank said, and he said it without any weight at all. “Make it easy. Don’t run.”

She looked at the water and then at the man. The water was forty degrees, ten minutes, and a current she did not know, and the man was certain. She went for the gap between the containers, low and fast, and she was fast.

His hand closed on her arm before her third step landed. He turned her effortlessly, walked her backward, and set her at the dock edge with her heels on the last inch of it.

“Brave,” the Tank said. “Stupid, but brave.”

The fist came back. The water waited.
