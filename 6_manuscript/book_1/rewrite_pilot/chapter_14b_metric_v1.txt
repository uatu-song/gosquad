The mug had survived the night, and the next afternoon Ahdia came back for it, rested and reasonably caffeinated and ready to negotiate.

“Freeze,” she whispered at it.

The mug declined.

She squared her shoulders and worked down the full menu. “Stop. Pause. Time out. Za Warudo!”

The last one honestly felt the most promising, since her only working reference for stopped time was an anime villain, and the universe had already shown a taste for her references. The mug sat there. She tried the Force-choke hand next, fingers clawed at empty air, elbow cocked, and held the pose long enough to catch her reflection in the microwave door and feel exactly as stupid as she looked.

Under the old bullet wound the Seed hummed along, a cold ember, awake and interested and useless. Beneath the hum ran the instinct that had ridden with her since the docks: this costs you. She didn’t know what it charged or in what currency; she only knew the feel of a meter running quietly somewhere out of sight.

She dumped yesterday’s coffee and brewed a fresh cup, reasoning that if she meant to spend the afternoon threatening a beverage, the beverage should be worth drinking. Then she threatened it for another hour. Commands. Gestures. Meaningful eye contact. Nothing worked, and the not-working stacked up in her until it hit the floor of her patience and went through.

“Come ON!” Her palm cracked down on the counter—

—and the world stopped.

The steam over the fresh cup hung mid-curl, a white ribbon parked in the air. The stove clock said 2:47 PM and went on saying it. The refrigerator hum was gone, and the silence where it had lived had a texture, thick and total. Dust hung in the window light and did not fall.

“Oh,” she said.

Nothing on Earth moved except her.

“Oh shit.”

She tried to un-slam the counter. She tried the commands again in reverse order, then in a calm indoor voice, then in the wheedling tone she saved for dying electronics. The freeze ignored all of it as thoroughly as the mug had ignored her. Panic arrived punctually, because the first freeze, at the docks, had ended on its own timetable, like weather, and she’d had no vote. This one might never end at all: the steam might never land, and the clock might read 2:47 PM for the rest of a very long life, hers, alone in a world-sized diorama.

The panic crested, broke, and rolled back out, and it dragged the anger with it, and for one hollow second she was empty of everything but tired.

Time snapped back, the steam finished its curl, and the refrigerator resumed its low argument with itself. The clock still said 2:47 PM, which meant the entire terror had billed the world exactly nothing.

She stood very still and did the math, because math was a thing you could hold. Mad pulled the trigger. Calm released it, or whatever hollowed-out cousin of calm she had just fallen into.

She practiced until midnight. Getting angry on purpose turned out to be the easy half, since she owned years of material; letting go on purpose was the work. The holds ran five to ten seconds, seventeen freezes by the end of it, each release slightly less of an accident, and when she finally quit she felt scraped hollow, and the cold spot under the old wound ached like a pulled tooth.

---

The theory arrived with the next morning’s coffee, fully formed: buy time by spending time. If frozen seconds cost the world nothing, then frozen time was free practice space, a private gym the size of the universe, and she was apparently the only member.

By midmorning she could hold past a minute, and shortly after that she got scientific about it. She froze the kitchen and lobbed a pillow across it, and inside the stillness the pillow flew and flopped exactly as pillows do, obeying her private physics without complaint. When she released, nothing dramatic happened at all; the pillow was simply somewhere new, with no history of having traveled.

Then the variation. She threw the next one hard and let the freeze go while it was still mid-flight.

The pillow crossed the living room like it had been fired out of something military, cracked into the wall, and brought the framed photo down with it in a burst of glass.

“Oh shit!” She was already moving toward the wreckage. “Yes!”

Both reactions were true, and neither one cancelled the other. She swept up the glass and logged the finding underneath it: whatever went in gentle came out enormous. A fingertip push inside the freeze arrived, in real time, as a shove from something far bigger than her. She spent the afternoon calibrating on furniture that couldn’t sue, cushions and cereal boxes and one long-suffering ottoman, until she could dose it, tap to thump to launch.

The exchange rate she worked out by the clocks: an hour inside came out to roughly three minutes outside, an accounting she wrote down and declined to understand. By day’s end she had banked something near six frozen hours, she could drop into the stillness at will and hold it for minutes at a stretch, and the cold under the old wound had crossed the border from temperature into pain. The meter she couldn’t see ran on.

---

What she did with the stillness next was go visiting.

She told herself it was reconnaissance, which was true, and that it was for their sake, which was also true, and that it had nothing to do with wanting to stand inside other people’s lives for a while, which made her two for three.

Ruth first, obviously, because Ruth was the only one of them she actually knew. The lobby guard was frozen mid-yawn when she walked in, a yawn wide enough to count fillings by, and she went past it at her leisure. Upstairs, the apartment door was unlocked. The woman fought crime on a volunteer basis and did not lock her front door, and Ahdia stood in the hallway a moment feeling personally offended on her behalf. Inside: The New England Journal of Medicine on the nightstand, bristling with sticky notes, a trauma surgery text underneath it. Tactical gear hung behind the winter coats, squared away with terrible neatness. The fridge wore photographs of Ruth and Firas, years of them, and Ahdia lifted one free, held it, and set it back precisely where it had been, corner to corner, and did that until her hands were steady and nobody living could have said she’d come through.

The tiny studio across town had its fire-escape window cracked two inches, so she went in that way. Its tenant was asleep on the futon in her work clothes. The pharmacy bottle on the bathroom sink read WHITFORD, TESS, and under the name, Sertraline 100mg. Ahdia knew that one personally; it hadn’t done much for her either. Another depressed girl making rent, sleeping where she landed. Recognition, not pity. She eased the window back to its two inches on her way out.

The community center said “nonprofit” from every surface, in the dialect that translates to “we’re one grant away from closing.” She came in past a rubber doorstop wedged for the evening crowd, past a janitor frozen mid-mop, past flyers for a food drive and ESL classes taped up at heights suggesting the ladder had been optional. The donation jar by the front desk held six dollars, all of it coins. In the back office a framed wedding photo stood on the desk, and the woman’s face in it had been scratched out, gouged deep enough to mark the glass. Ahdia didn’t touch that one; she left it exactly where it lived and went back out through the propped door.

The last apartment belonged to a barista, per the name tag on the dresser: the Bean Post, then LEAH, then a smiley face added in Sharpie. The helmet on the wall hook was stickered over every inch and carried a star, which meant jammer, a fact Ahdia possessed for exactly one reason: that Ellen Page roller derby movie, 3 AM, a box of Thin Mints, no witnesses. A punching bag hung in the corner, held together with duct tape and spite. On the shelf sat anger-management books, and under them workbooks with actual handwriting in the margins, page after page of somebody doing the assignment anyway.

She froze the room, set two fingers against the bag, pushed the way you’d test a door, and released from across the apartment. The bag went off. It swung like it had taken a full baseball swing, the chain snapped taut, and a ceiling bolt bent visibly out of true.

“Oops,” she told the empty apartment.

By the end of the second day she could hold a freeze for thirty minutes, come and go without leaving a fingerprint of herself anywhere, and dose force from a nudge to a wrecking ball. She carried out one other finding, the kind she hadn’t gone looking for. Sticky notes, six dollars in coins, duct tape, handwriting in a workbook: none of these people were special because of what their bodies could suddenly do. They were special because they kept showing up.

---

Two nights after the warehouse, she learned what the practice had been for.

The Frederick Douglass Bridge, from her side of it: floodlights, a helicopter hammering the air white, and Gloom Girl up on the rail with the whole department closing in. Then she was off the rail, one middle finger raised on the way down.

Ahdia froze the night with her still in the air above the river.

She was most of the bridge away when it happened, so she sprinted the deck flat out in the stillness, hooked one arm through the cables, and hauled a frozen, folded woman in out of the dark, which mostly took nerve. The carry was the education: four blocks inland with a grown adult over her shoulders, then up a fire stair toward a roof where Ruth stood fixed at the parapet, binoculars up. Halfway up the climb her lungs went from burning to bargaining, and her body remembered the stairwell, the trick with the heart, how narrowly that trick had missed being her last idea. Wrong dial. She didn’t need the whole engine slowed, only the burn. So she put her attention into her own screaming legs the way she’d put two fingers on a punching bag, and asked time to run cold there, and only there.

“Oh,” she said.

The burn in her legs folded up obediently and went quiet mid-stride.

“Oh that’s way better.”

She topped the climb sweating through everything she wore, because apparently the universe billed effort somewhere no matter what the muscles agreed to.

“For such cosmic power,” she said, “it sure is a sweaty one.”

She left Gloom Girl hanging a foot above the rooftop, feet down, arms arranged, close enough that Ruth’s binoculars would be beside the point. Then she crossed three rooftops and let the night go. Somewhere behind her a girl dropped out of an empty sky, unhurt, and the story would assemble itself without help from anyone. Teleportation. The vanishing girl. Let them have it.

A low whistle brought the conference room back, and the whistle was Mack’s.

“She figured out selective time dilation. Her own muscles. Mid-climb.”

Ahdia’s hands had found the water bottle without consulting her. “Is that . . . hard to do?”

“FAERIS,” Bourn said. “Previous Temporalist development timeline for selective biological manipulation.”

“Average development time: 12 to 47 years post-bonding,” the drone reported.

“You did it in under an hour,” Bourn told her. She let the number finish landing before she went on. “That’s what made us decide you were worth recruiting. You problem-solve with cosmic abilities the way most people problem-solve with everyday tools.”

It was a compliment shaped like a lab result, and Ahdia filed it carefully away to have feelings about later.

The display split into three feeds, three gray miniatures of one long night.

“We had eyes on all three ambush sites,” Bourn said. She nodded at the feeds in turn. “Ruth on the Frederick Douglass Bridge. Battlea and Sable at the construction site in Little Poland. Gloom Girl and Night Knight near the waterfront.”

In one feed a police dog hung mid-lunge and then was simply elsewhere, aimed at nothing, while a small gray Ahdia leaned her weight into other people’s punches half a second before they landed. In the second she watched herself gather Gloom Girl out of the air like fruit off a high branch. In the third, Night Knight left a rooftop at a doomed angle, and a hand closed around his ankle in the stillness and corrected his arithmetic by two degrees.

“Coordinated perfectly,” Mack said, and he wasn’t praising anyone. “Someone fed Chief Whitford their locations. All three teams kettled at the same time.”

“Then you showed up,” Bourn resumed.

“Three simultaneous interventions within twenty minutes,” she added. On the middle feed the river hung in gray pleats below the bridge. “Ensuring no one died—police included.”

Mack turned to the drone. “FAERIS,” he said. “Total power usage duration for police ambush intervention.”

“Six hours continuous temporal manipulation across multiple sites,” the drone answered. It paused briefly, for effect or for math. “Force amplification applied 47 times. Life force expenditure: 23% of total reserves.”

The blood left her face and took its time coming back.

“Twenty-three percent? In one night?”

Nobody in the room softened it for her, and she sat back slowly until the chair took her weight.

“I wanted them to be okay. I wanted them to think they could do this without me.”

“You gave them confidence,” Mack offered. “Made them believe in themselves.”

“While exhausting yourself,” Bourn said.

The display had moved on to an alley, where a small gray figure slid down a wall between dumpsters, folded up, and cried into her sleeve while the timestamp in the corner kept counting anyway.

“Ten minutes before you could stand,” Mack murmured. “You limped home. Took eight ibuprofen. Passed out on the couch.”

“And woke up the next day to do it again,” Bourn finished.

The feed changed again and gave her the Academy, the day after: the whole floor stopped at midmorning, Firas caught mid-sentence beside a mat, Victor frozen nodding along. A small gray Ahdia leaned in and stuck her tongue out at her brother, the full childhood version. Then the figure stumbled. A hand went flat against her chest, and even in grayscale the color went out of her.

In the conference room the gray light lay across her hands, and Ahdia leaned forward into it.

“I could feel it. Like something was burning through me. Every time I used the powers, it got colder.”

“Colder?” Bourn asked.

“The Seed. In my chest. When I freeze time, it’s cold. Really cold. But after—when I let go—it feels like it’s taking something from me. Like I’m emptying out.”

“That’s when you realized,” Mack prompted.

“That the power wasn’t free,” Ahdia said. Her eyes stayed on the small gray figure. “That it was costing me. I didn’t know how much or what exactly, but I could feel it. Like . . . like running on a battery that doesn’t recharge.”

Bourn turned to the drone. “FAERIS, bio-sign analysis from that session.”

“Cumulative degradation detected across all power usage instances. Pattern consistent with permanent cellular depletion, not temporary fatigue.”

“Permanent,” Ahdia repeated.

On the display, the gray Ahdia sat down on the Academy floor and stayed there a while. Then she stood, looked for a long moment at her brother, still frozen in the middle of whatever he was telling Victor, unfroze the room, and walked away before he could turn around.

---

The display went dark. For the first time since the roof, nothing was playing anywhere, and the quiet had intentions.

“That was two days ago,” Bourn said. It didn’t sound like an accusation, which made it worse. “You figured out your powers were draining you. That you couldn’t keep using them without consequence. And then you hid behind a foam obstacle to stick your tongue out at your brother.”

“I was processing,” Ahdia managed.

“You were regressing,” Bourn corrected. There was no cruelty anywhere in it. “After everything you’d accomplished—saving your brother’s team twice, mastering abilities that should’ve taken decades, preventing multiple deaths—you heard what he really thought of you. And it broke something.”

Her hands, she noticed from a slight distance, were still shaking. She flattened them on the table and reached for the one question that outranked the rest.

“My parents,” she said. The words needed a running start. “You’ve been monitoring this thing—the Hyper Seed. Did they . . . was their work in Montana connected to this?”

“Your parents,” Bourn said, and for the first time she seemed to weigh each word before spending it, “were working with CADENS when they disappeared in Montana. Or rather, working adjacent to us. They were tracking reports of a Hyper Seed manifestation. We were monitoring from a distance.”

“They died looking for this thing? For the thing that’s inside me now?”

“They died protecting it,” Mack answered. “From people who wanted to use it. People like Kain.”

“The Montana excavation wasn’t an accident,” Bourn told her. “Your parents discovered the Seed’s location. Someone else did too. There was a confrontation. An explosion. By the time our team arrived, your parents were gone. The Seed was gone. We thought it was destroyed.”

“Until Kain had it,” Ahdia finished.

“Until Kain had it,” Bourn agreed.

The echo sat on the table between them, and Bourn collected it before it could cool. “We don’t know how he acquired it. But we’d been tracking him for months before the warehouse. When you bonded with it—when you became it—you inherited your parents’ legacy.”

“Lucky me.”

Bourn rose and crossed to the long interior window, the one that looked down into the well of the facility: levels of it descending, screens tracking storms and sinkholes and every other way a planet can go wrong, people moving between them with somewhere to be.

“Our analysis suggests you’re running on a finite energy source,” she said to the glass. “The Hyper Seed bonding replaced approximately half your life force with . . . something else. Every time you use your powers, you’re draining what remains. At your current rate of expenditure—six hours of active manipulation, force amplification, multi-site intervention—you have maybe eighteen months. Less if you keep pushing yourself the way you did during the police ambush.”

“Eighteen months,” Ahdia said. The number came out flat, from somewhere far away.

“Unless we can find another way,” Mack put in. “CADENS has been studying Temporalists for decades. We have resources, research, ancient texts. Maybe there’s a way to slow the drain, or restore what’s been lost.”

Bourn came back to the table and sat. “But we need your cooperation. We need you to work with us, not hide from us. We need you to trust us.”

“Trust the shadow government that’s been spying on me?”

“Trust the organization that knew you were at critical energy expenditure and intervened before you killed yourself helping people who don’t even know you exist,” Bourn shot back. Then the edge went out of her, all at once. “We extracted you from that academy because if we’d waited any longer, you would’ve spent the rest of your life—however short—watching your brother mourn you while you hid in the shadows. That’s not living. That’s dying slowly.”

The drone hummed at its shelf height while Ahdia looked at her hands until she could finally ask it.

“So what do you want from me?”

“First, we want you to stop killing yourself. Second, we want you to let us help you figure out how to survive this. Third—” Bourn let the third one stand in line. “Kain is still out there. Chief Whitford is still hunting your brother’s team. And you’re the only one who can help them without exposing what they really are.”

“No pressure,” Ahdia muttered.

“Exactly the right amount of pressure,” Bourn returned. “The question is: are you going to keep hiding and burning through your life force alone? Or are you going to work with us?”

“Work with you how?”

“Training. Research. Mission support.” Bourn stood. “Kain is escalating. Chief Whitford is escalating. We need eyes on both of them, and you’re the only asset who can operate in ways they can’t detect or counter. In exchange, we give you every resource we have to extend your life expectancy beyond eighteen months.”

“You want me to spy for you.”

“We want you to stop Kain from acquiring more artifacts. Stop Whitford from weaponizing the police against enhanced individuals. And let us study your capabilities so we can understand what’s coming.” Bourn opened one hand. “Your personal life—your brother, his team, whatever family drama you’re processing—that’s yours to handle or not handle. We don’t care. We care about preventing the next cataclysm.”

Mack leaned forward. “But we do need an answer. Are you in or not?”

“What happens if I say no?”

“We keep monitoring,” Bourn said simply. “We intervene when you’re about to do something that gets you killed. We track Kain and Whitford without your help. And when whatever cosmic threat is coming finally arrives, we hope we’re prepared enough without a Temporalist on our side.” She let that settle before she spent the rest. “But you keep burning through your energy alone. And you die in eighteen months, give or take. Alone.”

There it was. Alone was not a threat you could aim at her; alone was the apartment she had lived in for years. She thought of the alley and the sleeve. She thought of the thirty-four flights she had climbed on ruined legs rather than spend one minute of herself on her own behalf, and of every person she had saved who would never know she existed, who would never get the chance to show up for her the way they kept showing up for each other.

“I’m in,” she said.

Nobody moved. The drone’s light held on her patiently, recording, presumably for some future footage session she would also get to sit through.

“But I have conditions.”
