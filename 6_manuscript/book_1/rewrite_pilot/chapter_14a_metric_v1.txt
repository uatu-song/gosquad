“Knew what for certain?”

Bourn stood. She rose like an executive ending a meeting, as if the chair had been her idea.

“I’d rather show you the rest at our facility. The rooftop drone projection has limitations. And we should get you off this roof before your brother’s team finishes their discussion downstairs.”

“Your facility.”

“CADENS operations center. Secure. Climate controlled. Better coffee.”

Mack’s face registered a brief professional dissent on the coffee point.

“Do I have a choice?”

“You always have a choice, Ms. Bacchus.” Bourn paused long enough for that to finish being technically true. “But if you want answers about what we know, what’s happening with Kain and the police, and what your parents were actually doing in Montana—you’ll come with us.”

Montana. Fifteen years of no rangers, no wreckage, no theory that survived daylight, and this woman had dropped the word into a sentence as casually as a room number. CADENS knew something about her parents. Bourn had just turned that something into bait and she knew it and Ahdia knew she knew it. She hated that it worked. It worked completely.

“Fine,” Ahdia said, standing before her legs could be consulted. “But if I throw up on your fancy facility floor—”

“We have protocols,” Mack said.

Somehow that was scarier than anything else on the roof. The drone drifted closer and its light climbed all three of them head to foot, brightening toward business.

“Oh no,” Ahdia managed. “Not again—”

“Deep breath,” Bourn advised. “It doesn’t help, but people feel better if they try.”

She tried. Space inverted anyway.

---

The second trip was worse, the way a second bee sting is worse: this time she knew exactly what the universe planned to do with her and dread made a lousy anesthetic. Reality pulled her file, crossed out “rooftop” and penciled in “somewhere else” instead and every cell she owned received the correction at a slightly different moment. Star Trek still owed her an apology.

She reassembled on her hands and knees on concrete polished to such a corporate shine that she got to watch her own reflection throw up. They both did a thorough job.

“Protocol Sigma,” Mack told his radio. “Command center, level three. Standard cleanup.”

A janitor appeared entirely unbothered inside the minute, steering a mop cart around her with the serenity of a man doing a familiar chore. Her vomit had a protocol. This happened here often enough to need a name and she was too busy shaking to decide how she felt about being a recurring event.

Once she could lift her head the room was every underground command center she had ever watched from her couch, except real and humming. Holographic displays hung in the air with nothing holding them up. People in tactical gear crossed paths with people in lab coats, and nobody saluted anybody. One whole wall ran live feeds: a storm rotating off some coastline, a sinkhole collecting a crowd, something burning under a headline in a language she couldn’t read.

“Welcome to CADENS,” Bourn said. “This is where we actually work.”

“You said better coffee.”

“I said we had coffee. Better is relative.” Bourn was already moving. “Conference room this way. The footage is already queued.”

The conference room offered a long table, a wall-sized display, and chairs with opinions about posture. The drone floated in after them and settled at head height like a housecat claiming a shelf.

“Water?” Mack held one out and she drained half the bottle in one pull, and sitting stopped being optional.

“The police ambush. Three simultaneous engagements across the city. Every member of the Go Squad kettled by coordinated police response. We were monitoring their communications. They knew exactly where your brother’s team would be.”

“How?”

“That’s what we wanted to know.” Bourn tipped her head toward the drone. “Show her.” She looked back at Ahdia. “Before the ambush makes sense, you need to see what you were doing that night.”

---

Gray light rose off the drone’s shell and a street she knew assembled itself over the table, and then the conference room quietly stopped existing, because memory has better production values than any hologram.

The night of the warehouse. Hours after the crate, after the light, after the ice moved into her chest and unpacked. She ran for home, ten blocks uphill at a dead sprint, because the alternative was waiting at the docks to explain the artifact-shaped hole in somebody’s inventory. Home meant the couch. The couch was where she intended to have her breakdown, in comfort, with the TV on.

The weirdness arrived on an installment plan.

First a dog hung mid-bark, silent, all four paws off the ground and staying there. She filed it under adrenaline. A man stood checking a very expensive watch whose numbers never moved, and she filed that under broken watch and rich guy and not her problem. On the next corner a couple had their argument on pause, the woman’s finger parked an inch from the man’s sternum, neither of them blinking. A taxi idled at a green light with its driver slumped over the wheel and she nearly stopped for him, and some deep animal instinct voted no and she kept running.

And always behind her, strictly peripheral, the world kept twitching back on. The dog landed. Horns resumed. People snapped their heads down at their phones, hunting for the thirty seconds they could swear they’d just lost. She was carrying a bubble of stopped time roughly the size of a modest living room and didn’t know it, and everyone she passed got their evening docked half a minute for standing too close.

Her working theory was hallucination. Her backup theory was that she was the Flash now, which genuinely troubled her for half a block on jurisdictional grounds, because the Flash was DC and the X-Men were Marvel, and whichever universe had drafted her hadn’t sent any paperwork.

“Wait, I was freezing time while running? I didn’t even know I was doing that.”

The conference room reassembled around her question. Bourn glanced at the drone.

“FAERIS,” she prompted. “Duration of unconscious freeze during subject’s return trajectory.”

“23 minutes continuous,” the drone reported. “Radius: approximately 6.1 meters. Subject maintaining temporal field unconsciously while in motion.”

“Twenty-three minutes,” Bourn repeated. “Maintaining a time freeze while running at full speed. Without even realizing it.” From her, flat delivery was evidently what awe looked like.

The gray light swelled again, and Ahdia let it pull her back under.

Her building. The doorman stood frozen mid-wave and she waved back out of reflex, a courtesy she could feel guilty about at her leisure. Inside she jabbed the elevator call button and got nothing, jabbed it again in the special rhythm people reserve for elevators and the button stayed dark while the truth put itself together in pieces she did not want. Electricity was a thing that happened over time. Time, at present, was her fault. She lived on the thirty-fourth floor.

“You’ve got to be kidding me.”

The stairwell door accepted her without comment and shut behind her onto perfect dark — the emergency lights were electricity too, and electricity was hers now. The first eight floors went fine anyway, railing and count, which she would later understand was the cardio equivalent of the first eight seconds of a fall. By ten her thighs had opened negotiations. By twelve she was hauling herself up the railing hand over hand, past the door her count said was fifteen, where a sign she couldn’t see went on advertising the residents’ gym she had never once visited, information her body received as pure sarcasm regardless. By fifteen she was dying, not decoratively but actually dying: a heart attack at twenty-three built from years of gas-station food and twelve-hour television days. Her heart had given up on rhythm entirely. A hummingbird in a jar.

On the landing of twenty-two, folded over the railing, she had the idea that saved her life and complicated everything after it. The bubble had an edge; she had felt it dragging behind her all night like a bridal train made of stopped clocks. A thing with an edge had a size. A thing with a size could shrink. Just her, maybe. Just her heart. She pressed a hand over the cold spot where the Seed sat and asked her own heart, silently and sincerely, to slow down. Please. Just a little.

The cold answered. Her pulse stuttered, then settled into a calm she had done nothing to deserve.

“Oh,” she said as the stairwell swam gently sideways. “Oh that’s—”

She hadn’t fixed anything, was the thing. Heart calm, legs suffocating: she had simply redistributed the crisis, the way you move a debt between cards. Silver linings. Floors twenty-three through thirty-four happened on willpower and railing and a count spoken out loud into the dark. At the top she released her heart like a held breath, and her pulse came slamming back with interest, every postponed beat delivered at once, worse for the delay. She sat on the top step until her legs re-enlisted.

Her key found the lock on the third try. The penthouse was dark and the TV was off, and the TV was never off; the wrongness of that reached her before the shape on the couch did. Firas lay on his side, one arm trailing to the floor, still in yesterday’s clothes.

He wasn’t breathing.

“Firas?”

It came out at a pitch she didn’t recognize as hers. She crossed the room with no memory of crossing it.

“Firas!”

Nothing moved. Her brain, helpful to the end, laid out the math. She had run home inside a bubble of stopped time. She had carried it through her own front door. She had parked it on her brother, and her stupid cosmic time powers had done to Firas what a shotgun couldn’t and she had killed the only—

The clock. The wall clock above the TV, the one that ran six minutes fast on purpose, held its second hand perfectly still.

“Oh you’ve got to be kidding me.”

Apparently panic pulled the same trigger as everything else. Her fear for him had frozen him, frozen the room and the clock and the air, and kneeling over her maybe-dead brother she could not work out how to let go. She tried the mental equivalent of Ctrl-Z. She tried unfreeze, undo, cancel, whatever the opposite of a scream was. She ordered the clock to move and the clock declined, and the ice in her chest sat there, patient and bright, waiting for something she didn’t know how to hand over.

“Please,” she whispered, and the word carried none of the command she’d been attempting, which turned out to be the trick. “Please please please I can’t—I can’t do this again, I can’t lose—”

The clock ticked.

Firas pulled in a breath, long and rude, and let it go. Asleep. He was asleep on her couch, dead to the world in the ordinary reversible way, and she knelt there with her hands hovering over him, relearning how to exist.

So that was the lesson, delivered at the maximum available cost because that was evidently the only tuition she ever paid: emotion pulls the trigger. Calm releases it. A device keyed to panic and unlocked by serenity, issued to a nervous system already running hot. What could possibly go wrong.

Firas mumbled something with no consonants in it and rolled over into the cushions.

She stood up on legs with strong opinions and went to the kitchen, and there on the counter sat that morning’s coffee mug, an inch of cold coffee poured by a woman who had never frozen a city block or a heart or a brother. She had to learn to control this thing. Because next time he startled her, dropped a glass or banged a cabinet or laughed too suddenly behind her, the freeze might not ask permission and might not let go, and she could freeze him to death for the crime of saying good morning. She stood in the dark kitchen a long while, staring at the mug.
