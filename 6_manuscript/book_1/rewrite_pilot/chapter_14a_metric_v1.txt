“Knew what for certain?”

Bourn buttoned her jacket, and the gesture adjourned the rooftop. The projected street sank obligingly back into the drone, taking its light along.

“I’d rather show you the rest at our facility. The rooftop drone projection has limitations. And we should get you off this roof before your brother’s team finishes their discussion downstairs.”

“Your facility.”

“CADENS operations center. Secure. Climate controlled. Better coffee.”

Mack developed a sudden professional interest in the skyline. Whatever the coffee was, his face had already filed a dissent.

“Do I have a choice?”

“You always have a choice, Ms. Bacchus.”

A pause opened, sized for fine print.

“But if you want answers about what we know, what’s happening with Kain and the police, and what your parents were actually doing in Montana—you’ll come with us.”

Montana. The word went in under her guard and stayed. Her parents had been camping; that was the story, the whole story, fifteen years of official story, and this woman had just spent it like loose change. Actually doing, two words, as if the camping had only ever been a cover sheet. She searched Bourn’s face for a crack and got the usual weather. Whatever CADENS knew about her parents, it was bait, and Bourn could see that the hook had set. She filed Montana in the drawer of colder hours directly next to relevant, and noted distantly that the drawer was filling up.

“Fine,” she said.

She stood out of the folding chair and left the terrible coffee on the gravel for whoever collected the furniture.

“But if I throw up on your fancy facility floor—”

“We have protocols,” Mack said.

He delivered it the way other men deliver condolences.

The drone lifted and unfolded its light over the three of them, a wash of white climbing from the gravel.

“Oh no,” Ahdia managed.

The light reached her waist.

“Not again—”

“Deep breath,” Bourn advised.

The white went total.

“It doesn’t help, but people feel better if they try.”

Space took the advice on her behalf and then it inverted.

---

The second trip was worse, and knowing was why. Her body took the correction cell by cell, every atom separately notified that “rooftop” had been a clerical error and the file actually read “somewhere else” now. She had time, mid-erasure, to reflect that foreknowledge improves translocation the way a schedule improves a flood.

She reassembled on her knees, on concrete polished to a gray shine, which meant she got to watch her own reflection throw up. The reflection handled it with more composure than she did.

“Protocol Sigma,” Mack said into a radio that was somehow already in his hand.

Something crackled back at him.

“Command center, level three. Standard cleanup.”

A janitor arrived before she had finished wiping her mouth, pushing a yellow cart and wearing the unhurried face of a man on his fourth one this month. This happened often enough here to have a name. He gave her a small nod, one professional to another, and got to work.

She stood up into the room and briefly forgot to be sick. Displays hung in the open air with nothing holding them, tilting politely toward whoever walked past. People in tactical gear argued quietly with people in lab coats. One entire wall was a grid of live feeds, and every square of it was somewhere in the world having a genuinely terrible night.

“Welcome to CADENS,” Bourn announced.

She said it in motion, without any detectable ceremony.

“This is where we actually work.”

“You said better coffee.”

“I said we had coffee. Better is relative.”

Bourn was already three steps down the corridor ahead of it.

“Conference room this way. The footage is already queued.”

The conference room had been furnished by somebody homesick for normal: long table, rolling chairs, one oversized display on the far wall. The drone drifted in behind them and settled at the head of the table like a chairman.

“Water?” Mack offered.

She drained half the bottle in one pull before her manners could weigh in.

“The police ambush. Three simultaneous engagements across the city. Every member of the Go Squad kettled by coordinated police response. We were monitoring their communications. They knew exactly where your brother’s team would be.”

“How?”

“That’s what we wanted to know.”

Bourn turned to the drone.

“Show her.”

The wall lit. A gray street assembled itself, and a small figure ran up it in pajamas, and then the room went away, because memory has better resolution than any sensor, and hers chose that exact moment to assert its rights.

---

The night of the warehouse, hours after the crate, she had run home. Ten blocks uphill at a dead sprint, because the alternative was waiting around at the docks to explain the artifact-shaped hole in Kain’s inventory, and she had watched enough procedurals to know how that interview ends. So she had run home in pajamas, recently dead, and it had honestly felt like the sensible option.

The weirdness arrived in installments, and she declined every single delivery.

Block three: a dog frozen mid-bark, all four feet off the pavement, an insult held in suspension; a strange dog, she ruled, and kept running.

Block five: a man consulting a heavy silver watch whose numbers were not participating. A broken watch, she decided, since rich people wore those all the time as a statement.

Block seven: two people paused outside a bar, fingers in each other’s faces, a statue commissioned by divorce lawyers. Block eight: a taxi driver slumped sideways at a green light, and nobody honking. And always behind her, at the edge of vision, the world twitching back to life: pedestrians startling, patting pockets, interrogating their phones about the thirty seconds their evening had quietly misplaced. She noticed all of it the way you notice a smell in someone else’s house, distinctly and with a firm commitment to drawing no conclusions.

Her working theory, to the extent that a woman doing wind sprints uphill had one, was that the city was having an X-Men situation and she was the only Flash in it. Then she lost the thread deciding whether you could even be both, since one was Marvel and one was DC and mixing them felt legally unwise, which was roughly the level she was operating on. The whole run, something in her chest had been cold, a small glacier where the Seed had taken up residence, and she had blamed the cold on the night air and asked no follow-up questions.

She was carrying a bubble. She would not learn this for days.

The conference room came back around the water bottle sweating in her fist.

“Wait, I was freezing time while running? I didn’t even know I was doing that.”

“FAERIS,” Bourn prompted.

The drone’s shell brightened a degree, the machine equivalent of leaning in.

“Duration of unconscious freeze during subject’s return trajectory.”

“23 minutes continuous,” the drone reported.

It paused, which a machine should not need to do.

“Radius: approximately 6.1 meters. Subject maintaining temporal field unconsciously while in motion.”

“Twenty-three minutes,” Bourn repeated.

Her delivery never lifted, and somehow the flatness held more awe than shouting could have.

“Maintaining a time freeze while running at full speed. Without even realizing it.”

Ahdia barely heard her, because the wall had her again, or memory did; the two had quietly stopped being different things.

Her building’s doorman stood frozen mid-wave, palm raised to greet nobody for as long as she cared to allow, and past-her waved back anyway, on autopilot. Then the elevator: she pressed the call button and got nothing, so she pressed it several more times, applying the escalating force that famously repairs electronics. The button ran on electricity; electricity, it turns out, runs on time, and time was currently being held hostage by the woman pressing the button. None of this occurred to her. She simply concluded the elevator had died, tonight of all nights, and turned to the gray door at the end of the lobby, the one with the little stair icon and thirty-four floors behind it.

“You’ve got to be kidding me.”

The first four floors went fine, which she took as a sign. Floors five through eight introduced her thighs to the concept of consequences. By ten she was hauling on the railing hand over hand, like a mountaineer in pajama pants; by eleven she passed the door of what might have been a building gym, which she had lived above for years and never once investigated, and made it a sincere promise. By fifteen she was dying. Actually dying, she was fairly sure. Her heart had abandoned rhythm for a hummingbird’s entire lifestyle, and a heart attack at twenty-three was statistically unusual, but she had years of gas-station dinners and twelve-hour television days on her side, and she had always been an overachiever.

On the landing of twenty-two the idea arrived, wearing the false modesty all terrible ideas wear. The bubble could shrink. She had felt its cold edge flex on the run. So: smaller. Just her. Just her heart. She leaned on the railing and asked her own heart politely, the way you would ask a stranger, to slow down. Please. Just a little.

The cold folded inward. Her pulse stuttered like a record catching, then settled into something slow and stately and absolutely wrong, and the stairwell swam sideways.

“Oh,” she said.

The wall did a lazy quarter-turn.

“Oh that’s—”

She understood, dimly, what she had actually accomplished, which was nothing. She had not fixed the crisis; she had redistributed it. Her heart was now calm as a monk at sunrise, and her legs, denied the blood the monk declined to hurry along, were quietly suffocating. Silver linings: she could no longer feel most of them.

Floors twenty-three through thirty-four happened on willpower and railing. At the top she released the little bubble, and her pulse slammed back in twice as hard, everything it had been postponing arriving at once, and she spent a minute folded over her knees relearning how to be an animal.

Her key found the lock on the third try, and the apartment inside was dark with the TV off, which had not been true of her apartment in years. There was a shape on her couch: Firas, lying on his side, facing the cushions.

He was not breathing.

“Firas?”

Nothing moved.

“Firas!”

She crossed the room in a stumble, already certain she had killed him; her ridiculous new cosmic whatever had leaked out of her and switched her brother off, because that was exactly the model of luck her family ran on. Then her eyes snagged on the wall clock above the TV. The second hand stood at attention, going nowhere.

“Oh you’ve got to be kidding me.”

Panic had done it. She understood the mechanism instantly and completely, the way you understand a trap from inside it: fear had slammed the world shut, and now she could not open it. She ordered herself to be calm, which worked about as well as it ever does. She tried Ctrl-Z. She tried picturing the opposite of freezing, and her brain unhelpfully offered soup. She tried the four-count breathing that television therapists swear by, in, hold, out, and the clock ignored every rep, and Firas lay there with his ribs perfectly still, and the failing fed the fear and the fear fed the freeze.

Until she finally quit operating machinery and just meant it.

“Please,” she whispered.

Her forehead came down against the arm of the couch.

“Please please please I can’t—I can’t do this again, I can’t lose—”

The clock ticked.

His ribs rose. Fell. Rose again, with a small buzzing snore. He was asleep on her couch, shoes lined up under the coffee table, and the not-breathing had been hers, her freeze, holding even his lungs on pause. She sat down on the floor beside him and let her body shake through the leftovers.

The mechanism laid itself out for her right there on the rug, horribly clear. Trigger: emotion; release: calm. Fear locked the world and peace unlocked it, every time, no appeals. Somewhere a committee had designed a power operated entirely by feelings and issued it to a woman with clinical depression and generalized anxiety disorder. What could possibly go wrong.

Firas mumbled something into the cushion and rolled over, alive and oblivious, here for reasons she was too wrung out to interrogate.

She got up carefully and went to the kitchen without turning on a light. The morning’s coffee mug still sat by the sink where she had left it, set down that morning by a woman she now considered a distant relative. She stood at the counter and looked at it for a long time.

She had to learn to control this. Genuinely learn it, drills and reps and the whole boring apparatus, because the freeze was wired straight to her feelings, her feelings had never once been wired to anything reliable, and the next time her brother padded up behind her and startled her, she could freeze him to death without ever touching him.

The mug just sat there, holding cold coffee, going nowhere.
