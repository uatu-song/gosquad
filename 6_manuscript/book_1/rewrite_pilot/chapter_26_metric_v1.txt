The asphalt gave her feet everything it had been saving up all night: cold, wet, and studded with small sharp things. She was barefoot in an intersection on Main Street, in a gown the color of a hospital’s idea of calm. Blood came down her arm and onto the pale blue and spread there freely, the way spilled things spread on television.

She watched it happen to the girl in the gown. That was the trick and it had never once failed her. You stand slightly to the left of yourself and you do the voiceover. The girl in the gown was in serious trouble, and somebody really ought to help her.

Kain stood twenty-three feet up and was plainly not done. His skin had gone translucent, so the light of the thing sunk into his sternum came out through the whole torso, red sliding to amber and sliding patiently back, a coal in a paper lantern. His bones moved while he stood perfectly still. They made the sound a house makes settling, sped up and turned up, and every so often the street got a little smaller around him. He had left “human” behind somewhere around the fifteen-foot mark.

Four of them waited at his heels in the ruins of CCPD uniforms. Ogden, with the scar running cleanly through his left eyebrow. Philips, thick through the neck, whose hands she had watched close on Ruth’s throat on a monitor four hundred feet underground. Edgar, bald, knuckles already wet. Orlansky, the young one, eyes too wide for the work. Ryu had put their faces on a tablet and talked through them rapidly, and now here they all were, off the screen and out in the weather.

“Well,” Kain said. The word arrived slowly, with a delay in it, like thunder that had to travel. “This is unexpected.”

“Yeah,” she said. “I’m getting that a lot tonight.”

The two kids were out of the intersection. Somebody in black had carried them off during the run and she never saw who. Her hands shook badly enough to be interesting. She let them.

“You look like you escaped from psychiatric.” His head tipped, and the movement travelled unhurriedly out to the far end of him. “Or possibly intensive care. Hard to tell with that fashion choice.”

She plucked the gown out at the hip and gave it a small model’s turn, badly.

“It’s called ‘hospital chic.’” The turn nearly put her on the ground. “Very in right now.”

“She’s delirious,” Philips said. His hand had drifted casually to rest near his holster. “Blood loss. Look at her.”

“I’m looking,” Kain said. “I’m looking at the girl who should be dead. The one who took a bullet to the chest. Who fell three stories into an explosion. Who by all rights should be a smear on warehouse concrete.”

“I got better,” she said.

“Clearly.” He let the word sit and then leaned heavily on the rest of it. “But not much better. You’re barely standing.”

While he was enjoying himself she read the roofs. Gloom Girl lay flat along the bank cornice with her chin on the stone. Battlea crouched behind the overturned bus, weight forward on her toes the way she went into a jam. Night Knight had the fire escape above the pharmacy and a canister in each hand. Crimson Sable held a length of rebar low and easily in the alley mouth. Ruth stood at the corner in gear nobody in this city had ever seen, talking steadily into her collar, and all four of them moved when she spoke, and not one of them looked directly at her while they did it. Ahdia found that with her attention the way your tongue finds a chipped tooth. She had absolutely no idea what she was looking at.

“I’ve had worse Tuesdays. There was this one time I tried to make pad thai from scratch and ended up setting off the smoke alarm for like, six hours straight. My neighbor called the fire department. Twice. This is maybe top five bad Tuesdays. Top three if you count that thing you’re doing with your face.”

“You’re stalling,” Kain said.

“Am I?” She rocked back onto her heels and stayed up, mostly. “I thought I was making conversation. You know, pre-fight banter. It’s traditional. I’ve seen it in like, every action movie. The hero and the villain exchange quips. Usually the hero has better one-liners but I’m working with limited material here. Blood loss and exhaustion aren’t great for comedic timing.”

“You’re no hero,” Kain said.

“No,” she said. “I’m really, really not.”

It went by him like weather. He had come here to insult her and she had handed him the sentence cheerfully, and there was nothing in it for him at all. It was the truest thing she had said out loud in weeks.

Something moved at the far end of Main Street, past the barricade of dead traffic. Headlights, low and quick, coming the wrong way up a street the whole city had agreed to leave alone. A gray sedan, civilian, with a front-quarter dent nobody had ever gotten around to fixing.

The Seed was cold in her chest, the way it was always cold. The other thing that arrived was warm, and it hurt worse.

Because he had stood in a room and told everybody in it that she had not made it, and then said the other part, the part about her being already dead inside, and she had heard all of it from behind a foam vault with her hand over her mouth. She had filed it away under closed, permanently. And here came the dent in the front quarter panel, faster than the street deserved, aimed at a thing the size of a church.

She carefully kept her eyes where they were and did not let them travel.

“What are you?” Kain said. She gave him nothing, so he supplied it himself, generously, the way a man does when he likes the sound. “A girl in a hospital gown who should be dead?”

“Something like that,” she said. “I’ve definitely had better days. My feet are cold. This gown doesn’t cover nearly enough. And I’m pretty sure I’m concussed. Or possibly having a stroke. Hard to tell the difference at this point.”

She smiled up at him with red teeth.

“But you’re about to have a really bad fifteen seconds.”

Kain moved two fingers, and the four of them came.

Getting angry on purpose used to be work. Now the anger lay right there on the top of everything, and she picked it up like a dropped set of keys, and the street stopped.

Sound went first. Then the cold came up her forearms and set its teeth in her elbows, and it cost her more than the last one had, because it always did now.

She walked. She went in among them, past Edgar’s shoulder, under Ogden’s arm, into a forest of enormous men leaning into a charge and going nowhere at all. She moved Ogden’s forearm gently, a few degrees left. She turned Edgar’s hip fractionally, until his weight sat wrong. Orlansky she left entirely alone, because the kid was already going to miss. Philips she barely touched, since Philips was carrying the most speed and the rest of it would take care of itself.

Getting back out of the way took longer than everything else put together. Then she let go.

Saturday morning, a bowl of cereal going soft: the anvil, the tunnel painted onto a rock face, the coyote arriving at a wall with a fresh idea. Four very large men ran into each other in the middle of Main Street and made a sound like a filing cabinet coming off a truck. Ogden went down under Edgar. Philips took Orlansky off his feet, and the two of them slid into the base of a mailbox and stopped there.

The cold left her elbows and went off to do damage somewhere less convenient. Her nose was running. She did not look at her hand afterward.

Kain came around slowly, the way weather systems come around, and the fist that had been full of teenagers a few minutes ago came down at her out of the dark.

She stopped the street.

Then she walked out of the strike path, ten feet, fifteen, in no particular hurry, around a hydrant and past the bus, and stood there with her arms folded to keep her hands from being visible. Everything had gotten remarkably heavy. Her legs had opinions. She let go anyway.

The fist finished its trip and took a bite out of the pavement where she had been standing, and Kain’s arm went in past the wrist. He stumbled forward onto a knee, and the whole block rang like a struck pipe.

Philips came up off the mailbox with his sidearm out.

“Stop—” Kain said, because nothing that size wants lead flying around the thing it is about to pick up.

She took the word out of the air along with everything else.

Getting the pistol away from a frozen man is mostly a problem of levers. She worked his fingers open one at a time, dropped the magazine into her palm and set it on the sidewalk, racked the slide, and let the round that came out of it hang there in the air like a wasp in amber. Then she put the empty weapon back into his hand, closed his fingers around it, and squared his grip up neatly, the way you fix a kid’s hold on a bat. She stepped clear. She let go.

Click. Click.

Philips looked at the gun with the honest bewilderment of a man whose entire personality had just failed him.

Then her body quit arguing.

The grey came in from the edges of everything, and the intersection went narrow and soft, and she was suddenly down on her knees with her hands sliding off them. Air everywhere. None of it useful. She was drowning standing up in a street that was full of the stuff.

Kain did not swing. That was the genuinely frightening part, that he had learned something. He came in close, folded down, and closed a hand around her middle and lifted her off the ground, and then he squeezed. Her ribs creaked and complained and something in there let go with a noise she felt more than heard. She reached for the cold and the cold was a long way off with the lights out.

“Not so clever now,” Kain said.

The headlights kept coming.

Here was the problem, and it was a mean one. If she stopped the world now, her brother would arrive at a statue at speed and be a red idea on a windshield. If she waited, there would be nothing left of her to stop anything with. There was no version where she got both, and she hung there in a giant’s hand and made herself wait for the one she wanted.

“You . . .” she said.

Kain leaned down to hear it, delighted, the way people lean toward a dying joke.

“you . . . had . . . enough?”

The sedan came into him.

She pushed. It was not the blue thing, the careful direction, the pulling she had been doing her entire brief career. This one was red and it went forward, and it took her and the car and her idiot brother and shoved all three of them out ahead of the world.

Everything held. Steam stood up off the crumpled hood in a shape that had no business staying put. Glass hung in the air in a spray, each piece with its own little light in it. Kain’s face was caught partway through working something out, mouth open, eyebrows on their way up. The Tanks lay scattered wherever they had put themselves. The hood was folded against his shin, and the whole tableau sat there being nothing but a photograph of the loudest moment of her life.

The impact had done one useful thing before the world quit. It had taken Kain forward off his heels, and the hand around her had gone loose in the same instant everything stopped, so the fingers stood there in stone, not quite closed. She worked an arm out, and then a shoulder. She braced both feet against the thumb and shoved, and the rib that had let go earlier told her about it in a voice she could not turn down, and then she was out of the hand and falling. The pavement came up and took her hands and knees. Glass stood around her in the air, unbothered, and cut nothing on the way down.

She got up slowly and got a fist into her brother’s jacket through the open window and brought him with her.

He came out of the car sideways and landed hard on pavement that had stopped caring about time, rolled once, and came up on his hands with a dead earbud swinging at his collar, looking at a world where nothing dripped. He looked at the glass. He looked at the steam. Then he stopped looking at any of it, because she was standing there in a paper-thin gown, bleeding heavily.

And the voiceover cut out. She was not standing to the left of anybody now. She was in her own feet, on her own street, being looked at by her brother.

“Hey, Ahdia.”

“Hey, little bro.”

His mouth worked. There was a bandage showing at his collar and he was breathing wrong, and he was doing the thing Ruth does, running his eyes methodically over her ribs, her arm, her bare feet, filing injuries.

“You look—”

“Gonna stop you right there.” She held up a finger. “I just have a pet peeve when family you haven’t seen in a while immediately start with how fat or skinny you’ve gotten?”

“That’s not what I—” He stopped. He put a hand over his face and left it there, and when he took it down he had gone somewhere much worse. “I’m sorry. This . . . this is all because of me. I shouldn’t have come to your place, I should have just gone to the hospital.”

“I know,” she said.

She did not tell him it was okay, because it was not okay. What he had said in that room went through her like rounds through drywall and confirmed the whole case she had been building against herself since she was a kid. And he had driven his car at a kaiju anyway.

“We need to talk about it,” she said. She looked at the hanging glass rather than at him. “Later. When we’re with more polite company.”

“Later.” He nodded once, slowly, the way a man nods at a contract.

“Later,” she said.

He was already pulling his jacket off, and then his shirt, and under the shirt was black armor, plates and matte panels and a harness gone shiny at the buckles from years of use.

“Why are you—”

“Habit.” He got the shoulder strap open with his teeth on one glove. “Wore it under my clothes. Just in case. Stupid, probably. But—”

“But you’re the guy who packs a go-bag,” she said. “Even when you’re benched.”

“Yeah.”

He got the last of it off and stood there in a t-shirt, thin, bandaged, a little ridiculous, holding out the only thing he owned that anybody had ever taken seriously.

“You can’t fight in a hospital gown.”

“Firas, you need—”

“I’m benched. You’re not.” He pushed it at her, and then he heard how that sounded and reached for something better, and what he came back with was a shrug and a small awful grin. “It’ll be loose. You’re smaller. But it’s better than nothing.”

She took it. It weighed more than she expected and smelled like her brother, sweat and cheap detergent and the inside of a gym bag. Lifting it hurt on the left side, low down, in a way that arrived late and then stayed. She held it up at arm’s length between them, considered it, considered him, and swung it down hard to slam it onto the frozen street.

She was already wearing it.

It sat too big in the shoulders, and the harness hung low on her hips, and there was a hard plate lying directly over the thing in her side that had let go, so that every breath now had to come in through it, and her feet were still bare on the cold pavement, and Firas made a sound like the beginning of a question and then did not ask it. Nobody explained anything. The street stayed stopped around the two of them, glass hanging, steam standing, everything patient.

“The team?”

“Positioned. Rooftops. Fire escapes. Ruth’s coordinating.” He was backing away toward the sidewalk with his eyes still on her, unarmored, wearing a t-shirt in a street with a monster in it, wholly untroubled by the arrangement. “Waiting for your signal.”

“Okay,” she said.

“Okay?”

“Yeah.”

She took her air in small careful helpings while he crossed. He went into the doorway of the sandwich place across the street and put his back against it, and there was nothing between him and the rest of the night but a door frame and his opinion of her.

She walked out to the middle of the intersection, into the space between the man on his knees and the four on the ground, in armor built for somebody else, breathing shallowly around the left side of herself, and let the whole street go.

Sound came back in a wave. Steam broke, glass fell, and Kain finished the thought his face had been halfway through.

“Let’s give them an opening.”

She pulled a breath that hurt everywhere it went and put everything she had left into her lungs instead of her legs.

“No better place than here!”

And it came back off the roofs, the other half of it, screamed down at her from the cornice and the fire escape and the alley and the wreck of the bus, and the Go Squad came down off the buildings, all of them, yelling.
