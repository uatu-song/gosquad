The movies had promised her a “high-tech command center”, and the movies had lied again. Real surveillance had no soundtrack and no quick cuts, and nobody helpfully leaned in to say enhance. It had an elevator ride down that lasted long enough to pop her ears twice, twelve-hour shifts, and chairs built by somebody who actively hated the human back. On paper, the whole facility was a “government office building that happened to have alien technology in the basement.” The paper seriously undersold the basement.

Days blurred down there until she quit tracking which was which. The Academy feeds ran in night-vision green from four angles, and she watched her brother’s team for entire shifts, the way she used to watch television. Except that now she was also the network.

The Seed kept its cold under the old wound, the Hyper Seed, Power Extempore, whichever name the files were using that day. It never warmed. She had stopped waiting for it to.

Ryu held down the next chair, fingers drumming between commands. His badge hung crookedly from his shirt pocket: “Dr. Ryu Matsuda - FAERIS Operations.” He was the only person in the building who could interface with FAERIS without the drones going haywire, neurodivergent pattern recognition shaking hands with fourth-dimensional tech, which the rest of the staff treated as a miracle and Ryu treated as a Tuesday. She got that. Her brain didn’t work the way brains were supposed to either.

The week came with a deadline. CCPD paperwork sat open on the far screen: a raid on a community center in the Old Caledonia district, filed under “probable cause”, which in Caledonia translated to “we don’t like the people who gather here.” The squad already patrolled three blocks from it most nights, on Ruth’s idea that the smart place to walk was wherever the police were hunting. So the bunker had exactly two jobs. Teach the squad its triggers, and be ready when the paperwork grew boots.

---

Tess went first, and Tess failed.

The bridge story had hardened into squad canon by then: she had “teleported”. Off the rail, into thin air, out of an empty sky four blocks inland. On the feeds she spent two nights trying to do it again. She jumped off the vault with her eyes shut and held her breath until she wobbled. She clicked her heels three times, the full Dorothy method, no ruby, no Toto, and landed squarely in Kansas. The heels worked strictly as shoes.

Nothing worked, because nothing could work while her trigger sat unchosen in a bunker under a government office building, between two people with a checklist, and the flashiest power was getting the most care. Ahdia watched her drop off the climbing wall and stalk away, visibly done with the universe, and felt the full Oz of it. She was the wizard. She was also the man behind the curtain. She was also, per the biometrics, paying for every ticket in the house.

---

Ben was easier. Ben already had a habit.

On the feed he reset into his athletic crouch, fixed on a point above the rafters, and jumped for a ceiling he had no business reaching. He had been doing it for days, and Ryu pulled the clip and let the crouch loop endlessly.

“That one?”

She nodded; it was simple, repeatable, already his.

On Ben’s next attempt she reached for the cold and put a bubble around him. A dilation, not a freeze: time ran slow and thick in a sleeve of air with Ben inside it, and FAERIS leaned into the sleeve and amplified. Three feet became thirty. He rose past the rafters like a man on wires nobody had rigged and came down whooping on the high mats.

“That actually worked.”

“You create the time bubble,” Ryu said, already making notes. “FAERIS amplifies within it. Symbiotic.”

“This is much better than what I thought I’d have to do.”

“Which was?”

“Much sweatier.”

Nights later the payoff arrived at the community center. Ben came through a second-story window into a room of drawn weapons, landed in the middle of the cops, and scattered them like pins, then went up into rafters no flashlight ever thought to check.

In the bunker the pull arrived in her chest, low and slow. Her fingers gripped the armrest until it creaked. The armrest held, and so did she.

---

Leah tried comics logic first. She stood very still on the feeds and concentrated at a foam block, Jean Grey style, then tried running starts and cape physics and dramatic fists. The universe politely took a pass on all of it.

Then a sparring drill caught her wrong, an elbow found her ear, and the sound she made was older than any comic. It was a battle cry, rage under it and forward momentum behind it. Ahdia answered before she had properly decided to. The bubble took Leah mid-stride and velocity multiplied inside it, and the training dummy left its chain and met a wall. The gym went reverently quiet.

Ryu typed it into the log: “Vocalization plus forward momentum.”

At the raid, a cop swung his barrel toward the corner where the kids were. The cry reached him out of the dark first, and then Leah did, at freeway speed, shoulder first, so that the rifle went one way and the cop went the other.

A vein rose at Ahdia’s temple and kept its own time.

---

Victor took a full evening in the weight room, stacking plates, lifting what a strong man lifts and nothing more. Then he quit adding weight and stood a while working one hand, and the gesture he settled on came out of memory: fingers curled, wrist cocked, a grip shaped for rebar. He closed it on the loaded machine.

“That works,” Ryu said.

He typed: “Specific hand position plus grasping motion.”

The next grip tore the machine off its bolts, all six hundred pounds relocated like patio furniture. Victor stared respectfully at his own hand.

At the raid, that hand closed around a rifle barrel. The barrel folded like foil. The cop who owned it got lifted, carried, and set down gently on the gym mats, and behind the whole transaction an organizer sprinted a clear lane to the exit.

Sweat came up on her everywhere at once. She gritted her teeth and held. The bubbles held too.

---

Tess stayed unsolved until Ruth asked the right question. Everyone else had asked what she’d tried; Ruth asked what she had been doing the one time it worked.

Tess remembered the bridge, went still on the feed, and then, solemnly, raised her middle finger to the sky.

Ryu turned from the console and gave Ahdia the look he did with both eyebrows. She shrugged and reached for the cold, because the gesture was already the truest sentence Tess owned.

The mechanism was almost respectable. Inside the bubble, FAERIS read her iris and her focal depth, took the exact place she was looking as a destination, and folded the space between. Tess vanished off the mats and reappeared at the top of the climbing wall, screaming happily.

Ryu typed: “Gesture plus eye-tracking.”

At the community center the lead cop had the worst night of his career. Tess, unbound, appeared at his shoulder and was gone before he turned, arrived at his left elbow and left it, the finger raised first every time, a targeting ritual he could not have invented. By the time he understood anything he was zip-tied to a bench and she was three rooftops away.

The ladder topped out there. Warmth slid over Ahdia’s lip. Her fingers came away red.

---

Ryu was holding out a box of tissues, and he had also been holding her biometrics on his center screen for days without mentioning it.

“Your hemoglobin is dropping,” he said. “Temporal manipulation is causing microbleeds. Capillaries can’t handle the stress.”

“I’m fine.”

“You’re not.”

He put a graph up. The line went down and did not come back. “Each use accelerates breakdown. Your body is aging microseconds faster than it should. Compounds over time.”

She looked at the line instead of at him.

“How much time do I have?”

Ryu’s fingers stopped. He pulled the ledger up beside the biometrics, and for once he didn’t soften it with a reference. “You bought yourself a year in the training room. This week you spent it on them.”

“At this rate? Eighteen months baseline. Maybe two years if you’re conservative with usage. But . . .”

He let the rest of it stand there.

“Quality of life degrades faster than lifespan. You’ll feel it before you die from it.”

The tissue in her hand had stiffened. She folded it smaller.

“Can you fix it?”

“I’m not a doctor. I can monitor, warn you when you’re approaching critical thresholds. But stop it?” He shook his head. “Only you can do that. By not using the powers.”

“That’s not an option.”

“I know.” He watched the graph a moment longer. “I’ll keep monitoring. Tell you when to stop.”

“And if I don’t stop?”

His fingers went back to drumming the console edge. “Then I’ll document what happens. For science.”

---

Ruth got no trigger, because Ruth had no power. Everyone agreed on that, Ruth included: whatever the rest of them had caught, hers hadn’t manifested. The feeds kept disagreeing: on her callouts the team moved half a beat early, tighter than the drill, correct before the correction.

Ryu watched four sessions of it, then typed: “Leadership vocalizations plus team proximity.” He didn’t type a question mark, but she could hear it anyway.

Maybe FAERIS was treating Ruth’s commands as targeting data. Maybe Ruth was simply that good and always had been. Ahdia ran it both ways, got two perfectly clean answers, and left them both standing.

Firas was on the feeds too, every session, at the edge of the mats where the light thinned, present for all of it and part of none. He wore an expression she recognized from mirrors: somebody being left behind by the people he loved. She knew it intimately.

---

Assembled, the raid ran thirty seconds end to end.

Five cops came through the front doors at 2 AM with weapons drawn, on a room that still held kids and organizers from a program that should have ended hours before. Ruth stood a rooftop two blocks out and calmly called the play, FAERIS threading her voice into every earpiece half a breath ahead of need. Ben came through the window and the cry came out of the dark. The rifle folded. Tess was everywhere and nowhere, finger first. Ahdia held bubbles layered across the whole team at once, the expensive mode, fields stacked like panes of slow glass, and the cold poured out of her for the entire thirty seconds.

It played like a Hong Kong action movie somebody had shot in one long take. Nobody died. The five cops ended the night zip-tied in a neat row on the gym mats, breathing and humiliated. The civilians saw only blur and salvation. The squad was gone before the radios finished asking their questions.

In the bunker her nose had opened again. This time it did not close. Her hands shook against the console, and Ryu handed her tissues without a word and kept handing them.

He typed the mission log one-handed: “Full combat application successful. Team synchronization optimal. Power reliability: 100%.”

Then he opened the assessment file, and the screen sat angled so that she could read every line from her chair: “Subject demonstrates increasing physical deterioration. Continues operation despite clear signs of life force depletion. Pattern consistent with vindication-seeking behavior superseding self-preservation.”

He didn’t tell her to stop. He only documented. She filed that under kindness and left the rest of it alone.

---

Tess cut the footage that night in her apartment, hood up, lit by the laptop, a FAERIS feed watching her watch herself, and the post went up while Ahdia watched it go: “They said we couldn’t make a difference. They said Caledonia was lost. They were wrong. #GoSquad #CaledoniaHope #WhenNobodyElseWill”

The comments came in hundreds inside the hour. Then they came in thousands. Somewhere in the scroll a stranger asked why she kept risking it, and Tess typed back instantly: “People need to see this. They need to know someone’s fighting for them.”

#GoSquad trended locally by morning and regionally by the next night. And in other buildings, on other screens, people with titles ran the same videos frame by frame, closely, and took notes of their own.

---

By the end of the week, three beliefs were running at once. The squad believed in its powers. The city believed in its heroes. Her brother believed he wasn’t good enough to stand with either one. She had manufactured all three, and none of the customers knew the factory existed.

Then Ryu pulled up a new file, and it held mission parameters, a target, and a risk column long enough to need scrolling. Something big was coming, bigger than a community center, bigger than anything the squad had trained for. The Seed sat colder and heavier under the old wound, and the blood kept up its thin patient trickle at her lip.

They were going to be fine, she told herself.

On the screens the team was laughing at something Ben had done, loose and in sync, easier together than they had ever been before her. She watched them in night-vision green. The dread kept its seat through the whole celebration.
