The tip came in shortly after seven, routed through Ben’s contact at the precinct, and by half past they had it up in the back room of the Academy with the lights low and the mats smelling faintly of the evening classes. Ben read it off his phone flat and unhurried, like a man reciting coordinates. “Weapons cache in warehouse district. District 7, near the docks. Kain’s people moving product tonight, 2100 hours. Minimal security. Window’s tight.”

“Thoughts?” Ruth asked, spreading the schematics across the folding table and weighting the corners with a water bottle and a roll of tape.

“Fits the pattern. Kain’s been moving operations since the warehouse raid. Makes sense he’d use the docks.” Victor traced the access road with one finger, down toward the channel.

Ruth built the plan the way she built a differential, worst case first. Victor and Ben would take the loading dock. Tess and Leah had the roof and its skylights, and Ruth herself was the perimeter, comms rig and medical bag, because somebody had to be the one standing outside whose entire job was catching whatever fell out of the building.

By quarter to nine they were staged on the overlook across the channel road, engine off, windows cracked to diesel and low tide, and Tess was already on the scope. “Three heat signatures inside. No vehicles in the lot yet. If they’re moving product, we’re early.”

“Good,” Victor said, settling back with the calm he saved for exactly these occasions. “Hit them during setup. Before they’re organized.”

“I like it when they’re not organized.” Leah was checking the straps on her gloves for the third time that hour, which for her was practically prayer.

They had twenty minutes to burn, and Tess spent them on her phone, which was standard, until she suddenly stopped scrolling in a way that was not. “Another one.”

“Another what?” Leah asked, leaning over.

“Kid wants an autograph. Posted on the Academy’s public page.” Tess turned the screen toward them, a nine-year-old’s crayon drawing photographed by a parent and dropped into the comments of a parkour school that had, as far as the internet knew, absolutely nothing to do with anything except teaching children how to fall.

“That’s the third one today,” Ben said.

“Fifth,” Tess corrected, still reading. “And someone left flowers at the Academy. Note says ‘Thank you for my daughter.’ No name.”

Ruth did the arithmetic she did not particularly want to do. Half the city was leaving flowers lately on any doorstep that felt adjacent to good news, firehouses and church steps and a gym that taught kids to land without breaking themselves, and none of those people actually knew a thing, and even so it put a low hum under the evening. People were saying thank you to shadows now, which meant the shadows owed them something. It changed the price of a mistake, was all.

Tess had gone completely still. “Oh. Oh no.”

“What?” Victor asked.

“Wait,” Ben said, sitting forward. “How do they know our codenames?”

“There’s like . . . hundreds of posts using them. Battlea this, Night Knight that.” Tess delivered it with the elaborate care of someone walking past a thing they had personally buried in the yard.

“Did you—” Leah demanded.

“What? No!” Tess was holding the phone against her sternum, as if the screen might testify.

“Gloom Girl.” Victor used the codename the way a mother uses a full name.

“I didn’t leak anything,” Tess said, and then, into a silence that plainly did not believe her, “I just . . . may have used them. In a few posts. Months ago. Hypothetically.”

“Firas is going to love this,” Ruth said, and let the flatness do the work.

“Or kill me,” Tess replied, sliding the phone into her jacket like evidence.

The clock did what clocks do when you are waiting to break the law, and at nine exactly Ruth keyed the rig and watched four status lights come up green. “Everyone check in,” she said, listening to them count off. “Comms clear?”

They were clear, and under the routine of it she felt the other thing arrive as it always arrived, like the good hour of a night shift, two in the morning, when the coffee and the adrenaline finally reach equilibrium and the whole department runs like a machine that likes you, every read faster and every reach surer, an extra tenth of a second folded into everything. Whatever the military had buried in their gear or their blood or the air, it made her feel like the best resident she had ever been, and she had learned to stop interrogating the gift and simply bill against it.

“No better place than here,” Ruth said, because Firas had started that call on their first cold night out and it had outlived his ability to say it.

“No better time than now.” The reply came back low and overlapped, all four of them together.

“Moving in,” Victor reported, and she tracked his dot and Ben’s along the loading dock while the roof team ghosted the skylights. “Clear inside,” he added, then, “Setting up position.”

Tess came on from the roof and something in the transmission was already off. “Something’s wrong,” she said. “There’s nobody here. Warehouse is empty.”

Three heat signatures at eight forty and an empty floor at nine meant the signatures had been planted, and Ruth’s read arrived whole, the way a rhythm strip declares itself. “Abort. Everyone out. Now.”

“Too late,” Ben answered.

The lot went white. Floodlights opened from three sides, and she counted engines and doors, fifteen units at least, two SWAT vans blocking the access road Victor had traced two hours earlier. The megaphone was almost leisurely. “This is CCPD. You are surrounded. Exit the building with your hands visible.”

“Shit,” Leah breathed, barely above the channel noise.

“Smoke and scatter,” Ruth ordered, already moving down the fence line with the bag slapping her hip. “Formation Delta. IR goggles. Move.”

The smoke bloomed across the lot, and the four of them ran the pattern they had drilled forty times in a cold gym until everybody hated her, crossing lanes on the count, four bodies threading a closing net in zero visibility because each of them knew where the others would be without looking. All four came out clean through the north gap while CCPD was still shouting at an empty warehouse, and Ruth allowed herself one professional observation as she counted heads, that this was training and not luck, and that the training was hers.

Then her goggles died on a full charge, both filaments, dead between one stride and the next, and the GPS on her wrist went with them, and she was still processing the odds of a double failure when Tess asked, “Anyone else losing equipment?”

“IR’s down,” Victor confirmed, then, “GPS too.”

“What the hell—” Ben said, and did not finish it.

Ruth felt the rest of it leave her, and her body knew the sensation before her mind would license it, because it was the wall from residency, hour thirty of a thirty-six, when the body finally stops negotiating and presents its invoice all at once. Her legs were suddenly only her legs. The night resolved back to ordinary human vision, grainy and slow, and the extra tenth of a second she had been billing against for months was simply gone, withdrawn, account closed.

“Powers are down,” Leah said, and the panic in it was young. “I can’t—they’re just gone.”

“You don’t need them. You’re trained. Stay focused.” Ruth kept her cadence level, the one that worked on interns and on mothers in waiting rooms, while her hands felt like they belonged to a slower woman.

“Fall back to secondary positions,” she said, moving them by voice alone now, blind on every screen she owned. “Stay in smoke. Use training. You’ve got this.”

“Contact. North side. What the hell is that?” Ben called, in the register he kept for answers that mattered.

Through the smoke came a shape she measured at seven feet, armored and wide as a doorway, and it caught a fleeing officer by the harness and threw him fifteen feet into the door of a patrol car, the panel folding around him like foil.

“New player,” Ruth warned, running the perimeter toward them. “Enhanced. Real enhanced. Everyone stay clear.”

Victor did not stay clear. “It’s a goddamn tank!” he shouted, flanking left, low and textbook, and the armored figure took his wrist without even looking at him and turned it, and Ruth heard the shoulder go from thirty yards and catalogued it against her will, anterior dislocation at minimum and a spiral fracture on the table, and then Victor was on the ground and not getting up.

“Victor!” Tess screamed.

Leah was already in motion, and Leah’s strikes were the best on the team, and it was over before Ruth had properly parsed it, one perfect combination landing on the armor to no effect, then a backhand, and Leah went through a stacked pallet and did not rise. Ben came in from the blind side, and the figure took him by the throat and lifted him like a bag of saline, boots clear of the ground, and Ruth broke position at a dead run because triage does not include watching.

“Put him down!” she yelled, and he did, dropping Ben in a heap, ribs and airway to be assessed later, and turning toward her with the unhurried interest of a man whose evening had finally produced something. Behind him the CCPD cordon was tightening, three of her people down inside a closing ring, and the math was short and ugly but the answer was still the answer, three immobile patients plus one mobile decoy equals one direction he had to be made to walk.

“Everyone fall back. Get clear. I’ll draw him off.” She was moving before she finished saying it.

“Ruth, no—” Tess had made it to Victor and was hauling him up by the good arm.

She gave it the tone she kept for interns about to argue: “That’s an order.”

Then she ran, and he followed, which was the plan working and also easily the worst thing that had happened to her all night. A quarter mile along the waterline with her lungs burning honestly for the first time in months, into the container yard where the stacks rose in black rows, left and right and left along corridors of steel until the corridor ended and there was only the dock edge and open water. She priced the water on reflex, black and moving, forty degrees maybe, ten minutes of useful muscle before hypothermia took the vote away, less with the gear dragging her down.

“Team, sound off. Everyone clear?” she tried, and the radio gave her static. “Anyone copy?”

Nobody copied, and the stacks stood around her like a closed differential, and he came around the end of the row without hurrying, filling the corridor, and took the helmet off, which was worse, because the face underneath was nothing at all, thirty or so and clean-shaven, a face assembled from features nobody would ever swear to, smiling at her with mild professional pleasure.

“Dr. Carter,” the Tank said, as if they had met at a conference. “You should have stayed in the ER.”

Her name, out of that armor, on that dock, landed harder than the floodlights had. “How do you know my name?” she asked.

“Everyone knows your name now.” He rolled the helmet in his hands like a man passing time. “Mr. Kain sends his regards. Says your research will be very valuable. Once you’re out of the way.”

Her research, the years of cellular regeneration work the establishment had laughed out of committee, was valuable now, to that man, on those terms. She made herself study what was actually in front of her, the load he carried without effort, the mass moving wrong for any gear she knew.

“You’re enhanced,” she said, reading him like a monitor she did not trust. “Real enhanced. Not like us.”

“Finally figured that out?” He set the helmet on a crate with a small metallic knock. “Your tricks stopped working the moment the EMP hit. Ours didn’t.”

“What did they give you?” she asked, buying seconds and sliding her weight toward the gap between the stacks. “What kind of enhancement?”

“The kind that matters.” He moved with her, lazily, closing the angle. “The kind that can’t be turned off with a magnet.”

“You know what happens next,” he said, and there was no cruelty in it, which was somehow the coldest part. “Make it easy. Don’t run.”

She ran. She went low and left for the gap, because the water was ten minutes of borrowed time and the man was probably four, and a hand caught her jacket at full extension and swung her back against the container wall hard enough to empty her lungs.

“Brave,” the Tank said, considering her. “Stupid, but brave.”

Her heels found the dock edge behind her, the water working its black arithmetic below, and his fist came up.
