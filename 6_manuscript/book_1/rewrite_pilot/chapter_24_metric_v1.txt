The rain was falling upward, thin silver lines climbing off the pavement into a sky the color of an old television, and the people walked backward through it without seeming to mind, a man unspilling his coffee out of the gutter and neatly back into the cup, a car sliding backward past her. It was a movie on rewind with the remote in nobody’s hand, and when she reached for the Seed to stop it, nothing happened, just blue light pulsing under her skin with her heartbeat, every pulse rewinding another second, the whole street breathing in when it should have been breathing out.

The feeling came up through her the way weather comes, without language, and what it meant was Wrong direction. Then, patiently: You only ever pull. Slow. Stop. Backward. What if you pushed?

The world inverted around her like a glove being pulled off a hand.

She fell through layers of frozen moments stacked like photographs, the warehouse explosion caught mid-blast, Gloom Girl suspended above the bridge, the police ambush with its bullets hanging like ornaments, and each one dissolved as she dropped through, going soft like a picture losing signal. The feeling followed her down, unhurried: You can’t freeze moments. Only delay them.

She landed in a hallway longer than hallways are allowed to be, blue doors down the left and red doors down the right, and she understood, dream-fashion, that she had only ever opened the blue ones. One was open on Montana, on the campground, where on a log by the firepit sat a girl of eight wearing her face.

The girl looked up at her with the patience kids reserve for slow adults and said, “You’ve been stuck here. We all have.” She picked at the bark under her hands and asked, in the tone of somebody reporting a score, “You keep looking back. What if you looked forward instead?”

Behind her a red door had swung open, spilling light and movement and forward momentum, everything she had spent years avoiding.

“I’m scared,” she said, and it was possibly the truest sentence she had produced in years.

The girl smiled the way Ahdia remembered smiling before everything, and told her, “Good. That means you’re moving.”

Then something too vast for the hallway came through it anyway, curious, not stopping, the way an ocean is curious about one shell. The red door pulsed like a held note, and when she reached toward it everything cracked open into red light, hot and forward and fast, and something she had no shelf for touched her mind, neither hostile nor kind, and she saw herself spread across infinite moments, every possible Ahdia moving forward, reaching for the red door. Then it withdrew like a tide, and a voice with nothing of the Seed in it, cold and final, said, “Wake up.”

She came up gasping under a white ceiling, monitors screaming, the CADENS medical bay assembling itself one fluorescent fact at a time, the IV already torn out of her arm with no memory of doing it. Ryu was there, close, glasses crooked, looking like a man who had not slept.

“Hey, hey—” He caught her wrists before she could claw at the sensor pads, his hands careful and not quite steady, “You’re okay. You’re—” He abandoned the sentence, since okay was visibly not on the menu. She made it halfway upright before her arms quit, and he braced her shoulder, saying, “Whoa, easy.” He offered her the only number he had, “You’ve been out for three hours.”

She pressed a hand against her eyes until the hallway stopped overlapping the ceiling, and asked, “What happened?”

He fussed a sensor lead straight and said, “You passed out. Ruth said—” He stopped, adjusted his glasses, and picked a different road into the same place. “You were in pretty bad shape when they brought you in.”

There was too much empty in the room where people should have been, and she asked, “Where’s Ruth?” He hesitated long enough to read a lab report, so she said it again, slower, “Where’s Ruth?”

He gave up and admitted, “She left. With the others.”

She was looking for shoes that were not there when she asked, “Left where?”

Instead of answering he pointed, and the wall monitor did it for him.

Downtown was on fire the way disaster coverage is always on fire, smoke standing off the skyline, buildings with pieces missing. In the intersection stood Kain, or something wearing Kain’s proportions at better than twenty feet, person-shaped but wrong, and his skin was translucent, there were too many joints in each arm, and the Tamois Heart pulsed in his chest like a second sun. While she watched, he picked up a car, casually, one-handed, and put it through a storefront.

She heard herself ask, “What the hell is that?”

Ryu looked where she was looking and said, “Kain.” His throat moved, and he made himself keep going, “He used the Heart. The Tamois Heart. He’s been growing for the past hour.”

“Ruth and the team went after him,” he said, and she watched him wish the rest would stay in his mouth, “About thirty minutes ago.”

“They don’t have powers,” she said, which was not commentary, it was arithmetic.

He looked at the floor and murmured, “I know.”

The math finished itself, and her own mouth delivered the conclusion, “They’re going to die.”

“Ahdia—” he tried, but she was already swinging her legs off the bed, and the floor was cold and definite under her bare feet.

She took inventory of what still worked, found enough, and said, “I need the treatment.” She stood, which cost more than she showed, and put her whole weight behind the next word, “Now.”

He answered before she finished standing, “You can’t. The treatment isn’t stable yet.” He moved between her and the door, arms half spread, “Using your powers will—” but she was past listening and into logistics, the cabinet, the tray, the distance.

She said it the way you say things to a locked door, “Give me the treatment.”

“You don’t understand,” Bourn said from the doorway, in tactical gear the war had apparently sent for her, “You need eight more doses. Three weeks between each one. That’s six months.”

“I don’t have six months,” Ahdia said, and tipped her head at the monitor, where Kain was considering his next car.

Ryu took her by the logic instead of the arm, saying, “You collapsed at the docks. Cellular degradation. You’ve been unconscious for eighteen hours.” He gestured at the IV port and kept going, “Ruth developed a treatment. We administered the first dose. You’re stabilized, but still severely damaged. Your cells need time to integrate each dose. If you take them too soon—”

“We’re out of time,” Bourn said, and the phrase stayed in the room. On the monitor Kain closed his hand around a second car, and somewhere between here and there the team was closing the distance.

Her hand found the Seed where it sat in her chest, and behind it the dream turned over in its sleep, red, forward, push. She shoved past Ryu, who could not make himself actually stop her, took all eight autoinjectors from the cabinet, and lined them up like the world’s worst tasting flight. She sat, closed her eyes, and asked the Seed for the other direction, and it flared red.

It was nothing like the blue, and she poured it inward, accelerating her own seconds, handing her cells the three weeks between doses while the monitors climbed past screaming.

From the monitors Ryu shouted, “She’s accelerating!” and then, working it out even as he read it, “She’s giving herself the three weeks!”

The first dose went in clean, and the second, and Ryu planted himself in front of her with a tablet he had typed on, RATIO = 1:2000 filling the screen, and she pinned the number, which held for exactly two doses, while his fingers counted with the glacial grace of kelp. He tapped a pen for a metronome, and she heard it as if underwater, then through a wall, then through an ocean. The tablet came up again, TOO FAST = CELLS BURN, then TRY 1:1500, and she chased the ratio and felt it slide away like soap, dose five, dose six, her body screaming in every language it had, the Seed flickering red-blue-red.

He read the numbers off the monitors and told Bourn, “Energy expenditure is exceeding regeneration. It’s a net loss.”

She found enough air to shout back, “It’s not like there’s a dial I can turn!” and then, staring at hands she could barely feel, “Or a needle gauge to monitor!”

The tablet swam at her through the red haze, Ryu waving it, SLOW DOWN in letters that took her three tries, and she could not slow, or would not, and by dose seven the distinction had stopped mattering.

“Even at perfect ratio, she’s only getting fifty percent max,” he told Bourn, in the tone of a man reading his own autopsy, “It’s a zero-sum problem.”

The eighth dose went in, and she let go of the red all at once and too late, like dropping a live wire, and folded forward into their hands.

Ryu had one side and Bourn the other, a sculpture about bad decisions, and when she could make sound again she asked, hoarse, “Did it work?”

He checked the monitors twice and read her the totals, “First few integrated. Last ones barely worked. Maybe thirty percent total regeneration.”

Bourn looked from the monitors to the wall feeds, where the team was making first contact with the thing that used to be Kain, and asked, “Is that enough?”

He gave her the honest answer, “I don’t know.”

She got her feet under her, hospital gown and bare feet and maybe thirty percent of a person, and said, “It has to be.”

Then she reached for the Seed, and the red answered, and she was gone. The med bay held its new emptiness, monitors flat, cabinet open and empty, and Ryu and Bourn turned to the feeds and waited.

The light came down before she did, a FAERIS drone building itself out of empty air above the intersection, its beam sweeping the asphalt. Then came the pull, the sense of the street being a page picked up and folded, and where the beam met the ground there was suddenly a girl in a hospital gown, barefoot, hair loose, hands spread, the Seed flaring blue through the thin fabric.

Kain stopped mid-step, fist raised, his enormous hand halted around the two teenagers he had scooped off the sidewalk, who hung there suspended, terrified, alive. The Tank cops froze with him, Edgar with Sable locked under his arm, Ogden’s fist an inch from Battlea’s face, Philips and Orlansky caught mid-charge, and everything else kept moving, the smoke, the sirens, the loose paper in the wind. For three whole seconds nobody moved anywhere, and then the civilians remembered how, and ran.

The Go Squad stayed where it was. Knight had been helping Battlea up and stopped, her arm still over his shoulder, Sable stayed against the wall he had been thrown into, Gloom Girl was on her knees, and Ruth stood among them with a bruise darkening her throat.

“Is that—” Knight got that far and no farther.

“Ahdia,” Ruth whispered, and the name barely made it out through the bruise.

She hung at the center of it all, holding five massive bodies inside her freeze while the world moved on around them, and then she opened her eyes and found her team, and they could see her counting, the split lip, the limp arm, the throat, itemizing damage the way other people say hello. They stared back at the girl who should still have been unconscious, barefoot in the smoke, blue light burning through a hospital gown, and nobody moved at all.
