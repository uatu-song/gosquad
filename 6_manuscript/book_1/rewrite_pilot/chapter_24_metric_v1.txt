The rain was going up.

She stood on a street she half knew, and the rain rose off the pavement in long silver threads and climbed back into the sky. A pigeon crossed the intersection tail-first, wings folding neatly where they should have spread. On the corner a man’s coffee jumped out of the air and poured itself back into his paper cup, and he lowered it unsipped and walked briskly away in reverse.

She reached for the Seed and nothing answered, the reach coming back empty, like knocking at a house you had already moved out of. Then the cold in her chest moved. It was not a voice and it did not use words, but it had a direction and it leaned.

The feeling said: Wrong direction.

The whole street ran patiently backward, a tape rewound by somebody with nowhere to be, and under her hoodie the blue light pulsed in time with her heart, and every pulse took the world back another second. The pulse was hers, her light and her heartbeat, running like breathing, without permission asked.

You only ever pull. Slow. Stop. Backward. What if you pushed?

She tried to stop the street instead. She grabbed for the rain and the pigeon and the man with his coffee, held the whole rewinding block the only way she knew how, and for a moment everything hung obediently still. Then the moment ended because moments do, and the street went on running backward through her hands. The feeling: You can’t freeze moments. Only delay them.

The pavement quit pretending to hold her, and she fell. She fell through stacked moments, frozen flat like photographs in a box: the warehouse caught mid-blast with its fire solid and orange, Gloom Girl hung above the bridge with her arms wide, the ambush with its bullets parked in the air, patient as pins. Every one of them was hers, and every one of them had waited, and none had ever stopped waiting.

She landed in a hallway. Blue doors ran down the left wall, red doors down the right, and the hallway went out of sight both ways. She understood, as you understand rules in dreams, that she had only ever opened blue.

One blue door stood open on Montana. Pine, woodsmoke, the campground, herself at eight years old on a log with a stick she was too proud of. The girl looked up without surprise.

“You’ve been stuck here. We all have.”

The fire popped and its sparks went up slowly and stayed up.

“You keep looking back. What if you looked forward instead?”

“I’m scared,” Ahdia said.

“Good. That means you’re moving.”

Behind her, on the right-hand wall, a red door had swung open. Warm air came through it, and light, and a low sound that could have been traffic or blood or applause. Everything she had spent years carefully arranging her life to avoid was on the other side of that door, and all of it was alive and none of it was safe.

Something came down the hallway then. It was too big for the hallway, and the hallway held it anyway. It moved patiently and it was old and it was curious. It was not hostile and it was not kind. It looked at her like she had looked at the photographs, and then it passed and every door shivered in its frame.

She put her hand out for the red door and everything cracked: red light poured through the seams, hot and forward and fast, and she was spread across every possible moment at once, and all of them were moving, every one, forward.

Another voice answered from nowhere near the warmth, cold and final, and it did not belong to the Seed.

“Wake up.”

She came off the bed gasping. White ceiling, monitors, a room loudly disagreeing with itself. The IV was out of her arm and she had no memory of tearing it.

“Hey, hey—” Ryu was there, glasses crooked, wearing a week without sleep. “You’re okay. You’re—”

She swung her legs off the bed anyway.

“Whoa, easy.” His hands hovered uselessly and landed nowhere. “You’ve been out for eighteen hours.”

“What happened?”

“You passed out. Ruth said—” He caught himself on the name. “You were in pretty bad shape when they brought you in.”

“Where’s Ruth?”

He looked at the door before he looked at her. The look took too long and he knew it, and knowing it made it longer.

“Where’s Ruth?”

“She left. With the others.”

“Left where?”

He did not answer. The wall monitor behind him had been answering the whole time, and she saw it now. Downtown, from a rooftop feed: smoke, sirens crawling faintly between the buildings, and in the middle of Main Street a shape that read as a man until the scale resolved. Twenty feet of him and more, still person-shaped and wrong everywhere, translucent at the edges, too many joints working, and in his chest the Heart pulsing like a second sun.

“What the hell is that?”

“Kain.” Ryu said it flatly, a diagnosis with a name. “He used the Heart. The Tamois Heart. He’s been growing for the past hour.”

On the feed, the shape put a fist through the corner of a building without slowing down.

“Ruth and the team went after him,” Ryu said. “About thirty minutes ago.”

“They don’t have powers,” she said.

“I know.”

“They’re going to die.”

He did not argue. In a room full of bad information, that was the worst piece.

Her bare feet found the floor. The cold of it climbed her ankles and she stood up into the sway and let it pass through her.

“Ahdia—”

“I need the treatment.” She was already moving. “Now.”

Ryu got to the door ahead of her and put his back against it. “You can’t. The treatment isn’t stable yet.”

“Using your powers will—”

“Give me the treatment.”

The door opened behind him. Bourn came in wearing the suit she had stood in while five people ran at Kain without her, and she read the room in one pass.

“You don’t understand,” Bourn said. “You need eight more doses. Three weeks between each one. That’s six months.”

On the monitor, Kain took a sedan off the street by its roof and threw it through the front of a bank.

“I don’t have six months,” Ahdia said.

“You collapsed at the docks. Cellular degradation. You’ve been unconscious for eighteen hours.” Ryu dealt the facts out with open hands, a man laying down cards he hated. “Ruth developed a treatment. We administered the first dose. You’re stabilized, but still severely damaged. Your cells need time to integrate each dose. If you take them too soon—”

“We’re out of time,” Bourn said.

She had come in to say no. The whole shape of her had come in to say no, and everyone in the room heard the sentence change sides. The cabinet stood against the far wall, and nobody stopped Ahdia from opening it. Eight autoinjectors sat in their foam like rounds in a magazine. She carried them to the bed and lined them up in a row and looked at them until they were only a procedure.

Then she closed her eyes and found the Seed and pushed. The light through the hospital gown went red. It had never been red. Blue was the whole history of it, blue at the warehouse, blue over the bridge, blue in her sleep. The cold stayed the same cold. Only the direction was new.

The first dose went into her thigh. The room dropped into slow motion around her, the alarms stretching into long low notes, and the cold poured outward from the Seed into every corridor of her.

“She’s accelerating!” The shout arrived pulled out of shape, low and long.

“She’s giving herself the three weeks!”

The second dose, the third. Her cells took the payment as fast as she could offer it, and the red haze came up at the edges of the room like weather.

Ryu had the tablet up against the glass of her speed, writing in block capitals:

RATIO = 1:2000

His fingers counted along with it, too slow and hopelessly behind her, and his pen tapped a metronome she heard as if from underwater. The fourth dose went in, and the alarms stretched past hearing.

TOO FAST = CELLS BURN

Somewhere beyond the red, Ryu went clinical. “Energy expenditure is exceeding regeneration. It’s a net loss.”

The fifth. The taps were fading steadily now, wall by wall, a man knocking from farther and farther away.

“It’s not like there’s a dial I can turn!” The shout went out of her into air gone thick. “Or a needle gauge to monitor!”

TRY 1:1500

The sixth dose, the seventh. The haze stopped being haze and became the color of the room, and somewhere out past it the tapping no longer mattered.

“Even at perfect ratio, she’s only getting fifty percent max,” Ryu said. He was talking to Bourn now. “It’s a zero-sum problem.”

SLOW DOWN

She never read it. The eighth dose went in, and the world came back at full speed, sound first, then the shaking in her hands. Fifteen seconds had passed on the wall clock.

“Did it work?” Ahdia said.

Ryu was already at the screen. “First few integrated. Last ones barely worked. Maybe thirty percent total regeneration.”

“Is that enough?” Bourn asked.

“I don’t know.”

Ahdia stood up in the hospital gown. Her legs held, barely, and she let nobody see what that cost.

“It has to be.”

She reached for the Seed and was gone.

The monitors flatlined over an empty bed. The cabinet hung open, its foam stripped bare. Ryu looked at Bourn, and Bourn looked at the place where a patient had been, and then they both turned to the feeds, because the feeds were where she would land, and waiting was the only procedure left.

The FAERIS drone’s light came first, dropping down through the smoke over the intersection, white and perfectly steady. Then space folded. It creased at the middle of Main Street, held for a breath and opened. Ahdia stood in the intersection. Hospital gown, bare feet on broken glass, hair loose, hands spread from her sides, and through the thin fabric the light of the Seed was blue again.

Kain froze mid-step at twenty-three feet, fist raised. His other hand stopped closed around the teenagers it had caught, and they hung inside the cage of his fingers, alive. The four giants in torn CCPD uniforms froze with him. Edgar with Victor in his grip. Ogden, his fist an inch from Leah. Philips and Orlansky mid-charge, boot soles off the ground.

Everything else kept moving. The smoke moved, the sirens moved, loose paper moved along the street, and the civilians ran the moment they understood that running worked again, pouring away down the side streets without looking back. The Go Squad did not run. Ben pulled Leah up off the asphalt without taking his eyes from the intersection. Victor stayed against a wall, an arm across his ribs. Tess stayed on her knees where Ogden’s shadow had been. Ruth stood beside them, the bruise across her throat gone visibly darker.

“Is that—” Ben said.

“Ahdia,” Ruth whispered.

The name barely crossed the ruin of her throat, and it landed in front of four people who had grieved that name for somebody else’s sake. Nobody asked her to repeat it. What they saw, and could not stop seeing, was this: the girl who should be dead, small at the center of a wrecked street, holding five giants still. Her arms shook. A thread of blood ran from one nostril, and she did not wipe it. The blue light beat through the gown with her heart, and every line of her body reported what the stillness was costing, and she paid it standing, and nothing she held moved.
