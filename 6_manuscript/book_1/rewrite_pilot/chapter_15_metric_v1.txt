“What conditions?” Bourn asked.

Ahdia set the empty water bottle down and squared it to the table’s edge. That bought her two seconds, and two seconds was evidently all the rehearsal she was going to get.

“You’ve been watching Caledonia. You know what it’s like down there. Police state. Whitford’s thugs beating people for existing while Black. Kain in bed with all of it. People are terrified.”

“We’re aware of the sociopolitical climate,” Bourn said, in the tone of a form acknowledging receipt.

“The Go Squad is the only thing giving them hope,” Ahdia continued. “Real people standing up to corruption. Not some government agency. Not some shadowy organization with acronyms. Just . . . people. Who decided they’d had enough.”

“They’ve become symbols.”

“Exactly. And if Caledonia finds out it was all me—that my brother’s team doesn’t actually have powers—that hope dies. They become frauds. The city goes back to accepting that nothing can change.”

Bourn considered that for a long moment, without any visible change of expression. “You want us to help you maintain a deception so a handful of parkour vigilantes can continue street-level operations against corrupt municipal authorities.”

“Yes.”

“And you believe this matters.”

“It matters to them. That’s enough.”

The pause ran long enough that Ahdia braced for the no.

“The sociopolitical stability of authoritarian regimes,” Bourn said, “has strategic implications. Populations under systematic oppression become . . . vulnerable to external influences. Maintaining indigenous resistance movements can prevent larger destabilization events.”

Ahdia waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it. She had asked this woman to protect her brother’s hope, and the woman had agreed because hope was a stabilizing asset, and the agreement was somehow colder than a refusal would have been. “So . . . yes?”

“We’ll provide support,” Bourn said, and the terms came out in a list she had evidently already drafted. “FAERIS surveillance of Go Squad operations. Real-time threat assessment. Translocation to intervention points. Extraction afterward. You provide tactical assistance during frozen time. They maintain belief in their capabilities. Caledonia maintains hope. We gather operational data on your power usage and limitations.”

“Everyone wins,” Mack added.

“Except I’m still dying in eighteen months.”

Mack looked into his coffee instead of at her. Nobody in the room offered a correction.

“Which brings us to our research division,” Bourn said, already up and moving for the door. “Follow me.”

---

The route ran along a railed walkway above the command floor, past the wall of live feeds with its storms and sinkholes and burning things in patient rotation. Side corridors branched off at intervals, every one of them labeled and every label an acronym. Ahdia watched the feeds slide by underneath her sneakers. “How many of these places are being watched?” Ahdia asked, keeping her eyes on the slow parade of disasters below.

“Currently? 247 active monitoring sites,” Bourn said, without slowing down or consulting anything. “Down from 890 during the Cold War. Cataclysm activity has decreased since the 1960s.”

“Why?”

“Unknown. Working theory: dimensional barriers strengthened after atmospheric nuclear testing stopped. Or testing weakened barriers and they’re slowly recovering. Or complete coincidence. We don’t have enough data.”

She offered all three theories with identical investment, and Ahdia was beginning to understand that Bourn kept her beliefs like other people kept spare batteries, interchangeable and in a drawer.

The walkway ended at a heavy door stenciled in yellow:

RESEARCH DIVISION - AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY

Beyond it the facility changed dialect entirely: whiteboards instead of status boards, cables duct-taped across the floor in bundles, equipment she couldn’t name crowded against equipment she couldn’t guess at. In the middle of it all, a man in a wrinkled button-down was wheeling his chair between two monitors, loudly arguing both sides. “—can’t be right, the temporal variance should be inverse not proportional unless the field radius affects decay rate which would mean—”

He looked up, saw Bourn, and rode the chair directly into a filing cabinet. “Director! I didn’t—sorry, I was just—the readings from the warehouse event were—”

“Dr. Ryu,” Bourn said. “This is Ahdia Bacchus. Our Temporalist.”

He came upright fast enough to knock his glasses crooked. Japanese and somewhere in his late twenties, roughly her height, wearing the look of a man thirty hours past his last sleep and running on enthusiasm instead.

“Yes! I know! I mean, I’ve been studying your—not YOU, obviously, that would be—I mean the data from your power usage, which is fascinating, not that you’re just data, you’re a person, obviously a person, a very—” He ran out of runway and pushed the glasses back up his nose. “Sorry. I talk too much when I’m excited. Also when I’m nervous. Also in general.”

“Relax. I understood maybe forty percent of that, which is my baseline for most conversations.”

“Forty percent? That’s actually above average! Most people stop me at ten. My last roommate used to throw pillows at me when I—” Bourn’s attention shifted onto him by a single degree, which was apparently enough. “Sorry. Doing it again.”

“Dr. Ryu will be your handler,” Bourn said. “He manages FAERIS operations and will serve as your tactical support during missions.”

“I’m your guy in the chair,” Ryu said. “That’s a Spider-Man reference. You probably don’t—”

“I know,” Ahdia said. “I’ve seen Homecoming like six times.”

“Seven for me. Well, eight if you count the time I fell asleep and had to rewatch—” He caught the Director’s stillness at the edge of his vision. “Right. Professional. I can be professional.”

“Dr. Ryu has been monitoring your power usage since the warehouse incident. He’ll brief you on what we know, what we don’t know, and begin baseline testing to understand your capabilities and limitations.”

“I’ve prepared a presentation,” Ryu said, visibly rallying. “It’s only forty-seven slides. I cut it down from ninety.”

“We’ll skip the presentation,” Bourn said, already turning to go. “Run the physical assessments first. I want baseline biometrics before she uses powers again.”

“Right. Yes. Testing. I can do testing.”

Bourn was gone with a nod to Ryu, and Mack followed her out, giving Ahdia a look on the way that might have been sympathy or might have been a warning about the slides. The lab door sealed behind them, and just that fast the entire federal apparatus shrank down to one over-caffeinated scientist bouncing slightly on his heels. “Medical bay is this way.”

---

The medical bay was white and quiet, and the machine in the middle of it looked like a dentist’s chair and a tanning bed had pooled their ambitions. Screens ringed it on articulated arms. Ryu moved between them, waking each one with a tap.

“Okay,” Ryu said, cracking his knuckles at the console. “So. Baseline biometrics. We need to understand what the Hyper Seed bonding did to your physiology before we can figure out how to slow the degradation. Or stop it. Ideally stop it.” He patted the chair hopefully. “If you could just—”

“What exactly are you looking for?”

“Everything?” He spread his hands to indicate the size of everything. “Heart rate, blood pressure, cellular composition, temporal field resonance, dimensional signature, neurological patterns—basically if it can be measured, I’m measuring it.”

“That sounds deeply invasive.”

“It’s non-invasive! Completely! The scanners are passive. You just sit there. Well, lie there. It works better if you lie flat.”

A changing room hid behind a curtain in the corner, gray scrubs folded on its shelf in a stack of guessed sizes that included exactly hers. She changed and came back and lay down on the not-quite-dentist-chair, which reclined beneath her with a hum.

“There we go. Comfortable?”

“Not remotely.”

“Great! That’s—I mean, sorry. Do you need a pillow or—”

“I’m fine. Let’s just get this over with.”

The scanner arm made its first slow pass, and data climbed the screens while Ryu leaned into them, fingers drumming the edge of the console as he read. “Okay,” Ryu muttered, half to the screens. “Heart rate elevated but that’s probably stress from translocation. Blood pressure . . . hmm. Cellular degradation markers are . . . oh.” The drumming stopped.

“‘Oh’ what?”

“Sorry. Just—the Seed integration is more thorough than I expected. It’s not just sitting in your chest like a foreign object. It’s connected to your cardiovascular system, your nervous system, your skeletal structure. It’s . . .” He turned one of the screens so she could see it. Her body rendered in gray sections with fine bright threads running through the gray. They followed her ribs and wrapped the long bones of her arms. They gathered at the center of her chest, thick around a point of light.

“It’s replacing parts of you. Slowly.”

She looked at the threads.

“Replacing me with what?”

“With itself.” He kept his eyes on the screen. “Every time you use your powers, it spreads a little more. Converts your biological tissue into something else. Something that can channel temporal energy.”

“So I’m turning into a Hyper Seed.”

“Essentially. The process is slow—eighteen months at current usage rates before you’re more Seed than human. But . . .” He stopped. His hands lay flat on the console now, holding still on purpose. “The tissue it converts isn’t dead. It’s still you. It still functions. It’s just . . . different.”

“Can you stop it?”

“I don’t know. Maybe? CADENS has been studying Temporalists for decades, but most of them died before we could collect meaningful data. You’re the first one we’ve had access to this early in the bonding process.”

She watched the threads. He watched her.

“But I’m going to try. That’s what all this is for—understanding the mechanism so we can intervene.”

“And if you can’t?”

“Then we make the eighteen months count.”

The scanner arm made its second pass. She lay flat and let it read her.

---

The full battery took another hour. She was back in her own clothes, sitting on the edge of the chair, by the time he had finished making the screens agree with each other.

“Okay,” he said, wheeling his stool around to face her. “Good news and bad news. Good news: your baseline physiology is adapting remarkably well to the Seed integration. Your body isn’t rejecting it, which is—well, you’re alive, so obviously it’s not rejecting it, but I mean at the cellular level there’s no immune response, no inflammation, no—sorry. Rambling.”

“And the bad news?”

“The temporal field generation is inefficient. You’re burning way more energy than you need to for the same effect. It’s like—” He looked around the medical bay for an analogy and located one somewhere behind her. “Like running a car engine at maximum RPM to go thirty miles per hour. You’re getting where you need to go, but you’re destroying the engine in the process.”

“Can you fix it?”

“Maybe. If we can teach you to control the field more precisely. Right now you’re doing everything by instinct—which is impressive, don’t get me wrong—but instinct isn’t efficient. It’s like the difference between flailing in water and actually swimming. Both keep you afloat, but one uses way less energy.”

Ahdia thought about the fire stair and the trick with her own burning legs, and kept the thought where it was.

“I want to run some controlled tests. See if we can refine your technique. But first—” He was already moving, waving her up and out into the corridor, talking happily the whole way about interface design and video game HUDs while she let it wash over her like weather. Two doors down he badged them into a long room lined with racks. “We need to get you equipped.”

---

The armory ran deep, racks down both sides and a firing range opening off the far end. Front and center, on a mannequin, stood a suit: matte black from collar to boot, armor plates laid over flexible fabric in overlapping segments. It had very clearly been waiting for her. “CADENS is really going all-in on the superhero aesthetic, huh?” Ahdia said.

“It’s designed specifically for you. Well, for Temporalists. The fabric is—” He stopped, visibly upgraded his plan, and crossed to a rack drawer. “Here, let me show you something. Cover your ears.”

“What?”

He came back holding a handgun. “Trust me. Cover your ears.”

She got her hands up just before he shot the mannequin in the sternum. The report slammed around the racks, a spark jumped off the plate, and the round went whining away into the range. The mannequin rocked briefly on its stand and settled, and the suit carried no mark that mattered. Her ears rang anyway.

“Bulletproof,” Ryu said, setting the gun down gingerly with both hands, a man returning a borrowed thing. “The armor plates, anyway. The fabric itself is impact-resistant but not bullet-stopping. It will, however, handle the temporal shear when you freeze time. Normal clothes deteriorate—fibers can’t handle the stress. This can.” He peeled the suit off the mannequin and held it out. “Temperature regulation, sensor-transparent for our monitoring equipment, and surprisingly light. Try it on.”

“Changing room?”

“Same one as before.”

The suit fit her. Every measurement was right, down to the length of her arms and the width of her shoulders, and nobody at CADENS had ever taken a tape to her, which meant they had extrapolated her dimensions from surveillance footage. She filed that under efficient and creepy in that order, and walked back out to the armory.

“Okay,” he said. He said it to a point slightly to the left of her, then found something urgent to check at a console. When he came back he was carrying a visor, holding it out with both hands. “This is your HUD. Augmented reality display. Put it on.”

She put it on. The armory grew a second layer: range markers and air temperature, a compass ribbon, small patient readouts attaching themselves to everything she looked at. “Whoa.”

“Right?” He bounced once on his heels. “I designed the interface. Video game HUDs, like I said. You can track FAERIS-monitored individuals, see threat assessments, magnify your vision up to 20x, access tactical data during operations. It’s all gesture and eye-tracking controlled. Very intuitive.” He tapped his own temple. “It’s also field-hardened like the suit—ordinary electronics stop dead inside your bubble, which is how I ended up watching you climb thirty-four floors instead of taking the elevator.”

She looked at him. The display obligingly tagged him:

[DR. RYU MATSUDA - CADENS PERSONNEL - CLEARANCE LEVEL 7 - HEART RATE: 82 BPM]

When she had first put the visor on, the last line had read 78. “I can see your heart rate went up.”

“Yeah, well, I get excited about tech.”

“I’m also detecting,” Ahdia said, “elevated levels of awkwardness. It’s over 9,000.”

“Did you just—did you make a Dragon Ball Z reference?”

“I contain multitudes.”

He looked like a man whose birthday had arrived early.

“Also I watched a lot of anime at 4 AM when sleep wouldn’t come.”

“Okay, I’m raising your clearance level.”

She raised an eyebrow at him.

“Just kidding, I don’t have that authority. But we need to test the gear with your powers. The display should stay functional even in frozen time. I’m also going to test something experimental—the HUD’s AR communication protocol. Step into the testing circle.”

The testing circle turned out to be a ring of yellow paint on the floor near the range, and she stepped into it.

“Now freeze time,” Ryu said, backing up two steps and straightening his glasses. “And don’t freak out when you see me.”

She froze it. The room’s hum cut out, and Ryu stopped mid-fidget with one hand still at his glasses. Then light gathered itself beside the console, and a second Ryu stood there, edges faintly luminous, grinning at her through the stillness.

“If you can see this, wave your hand,” AR-Ryu said.

She waved.

“Excellent! Okay, this is going to sound weird, but I’m not actually me right now. I’m a cybernetic mapping of my brain running through the HUD’s processors. The system operates on picosecond framerates, so it can function even in your temporal field. I can talk to you, answer questions, provide tactical support—and when you unfreeze, everything we discuss gets uploaded to real-me’s brain instantly. He’ll—I’ll—remember this conversation like it just happened. Which, from my perspective, it did. Or will. Time travel grammar is confusing.”

“This is deeply weird.”

“Oh absolutely. But functional! Try asking me something only I would know.”

She glanced at the real Ryu fixed mid-adjustment, then back at the glowing one. “What show did I reference earlier?”

“Dragon Ball Z. Over 9,000 joke. See? I have all of real-Ryu’s memories up to the moment you froze time.”

She looked at it a moment longer. It stood there glowing, patiently pleased with itself. “Okay, you can unfreeze now. Watch what happens.”

She let the stillness go. The hum returned, and the real Ryu finished adjusting his glasses and then caught up with himself all at once. “Did the AR work? Did it—oh, you asked about Dragon Ball Z. Yeah, I remember that now. It’s like I was there, but also I wasn’t? This tech is so cool.”

“That,” Ahdia said, “is the weirdest thing that’s happened to me. And I have a cosmic artifact in my chest.”

“Fair. But it means you’ll never be alone during operations. I’ll always be there. Sort of.” He checked the console readouts, nodding at each in turn. “Everything else maintained integrity too—suit, HUD, communication. You could hold a freeze for hours and we could have full conversations.”

He looked up from the console, straight on. “How does it feel?”

“I feel like I’m either cosplaying,” she said, “or I look like I’m about to brood on a rooftop.”

“You’re equipped,” Ryu corrected. “There’s a difference.”

“Does it have to be black?”

“Just for dinner,” he replied, without looking up from the console, and it sat there being a joke or possibly not, and she decided not to audit it.

He strapped a heavy watch onto her wrist next, cinching the band with tech-support briskness. “This is your panic button. If something goes wrong—if you’re injured, if you’re stuck, if you just need immediate extraction—you press this and the nearest FAERIS unit will paint you for translocation. Emergency protocol. Bourn authorized it specifically for you.”

“Anything else?”

“One more thing. It’s—you don’t have to take it if you don’t want to. But . . .” He took a flat case from the end of the rack and held it a moment before he offered it. “For anonymity. I know you’re invisible during operations, but in case someone sees you before you freeze time, or after you unfreeze—”

She opened it. “A mask.”

“A mask,” he confirmed. “I know it’s very comic book, but—”

“I’ll take it.”

It was a black domino, light as paper and shaped for the top half of a face. She put it on and turned to the mirror at the end of the racks. “I look ridiculous,” she said.

“You look dangerous,” Ryu corrected. “In a good way. In a ‘don’t mess with me because I can stop time and you’ll never see it coming’ way.”

The woman in the mirror stood straight, armored and masked and built for a job. She looked capable. She looked above all like someone who wasn’t Ahdia, and Ahdia had no complaints about the casting. “What now?” she asked.

“Now,” Ryu said, “we test your temporal control. See if we can make you more efficient. Buy you more than eighteen months.”

---

The training room was mostly empty space under a high ceiling: padded floor, a pendulum hanging on a long cable from a gantry, and a ball suspended from a rig beside a wall display. Ryu set the pendulum swinging and set the wall timer running, red digits climbing. “Okay,” Ryu said, walking backward toward the console. “I want you to freeze time, but this time, try to feel what you’re doing. Where the energy is coming from, where it’s going. The more aware you are of the mechanism, the more control you’ll have.”

She reached for the cold. The pendulum halted mid-arc. The ball hung dead on its cable. The timer held at 00:03.87 and declined to move again, and for once she stood inside the stillness and paid attention to it instead of spending it: the cold flowing outward from the center of her, the far edge of the field somewhere beyond the walls and wastefully wide. She let go.

“Good,” he said, eyes on his tablet. “Now try to shrink the field. Make it smaller. Just the room instead of the whole facility.”

She froze it again. This time she went hunting for the edge. It was there, as the burn in her legs had been on the fire stair, a thing she could set her attention against and move. She hauled it inward until it sat just outside the walls and released.

“Perfect!” The tablet came up like a trophy. “You just reduced energy expenditure by thirty percent. Try it again, even smaller.”

She did it again. The edge drew in closer, snug against the room, and now she could feel the whole shape of the thing, a bubble she wore instead of a weather system she dragged. “I felt it,” she said. “I could feel the shape of the field. Like it was part of me.”

“You just cut your energy usage in half. If you can maintain that level of control during field operations, you’ve bought yourself another year. Maybe more.”

Her legs were shaking, and she sat down on the padded floor before they could decide the matter for her. Another year. She turned the number over carefully, looking for the catch. “Really?”

“Really.” He crouched to her level, tablet against his knees. “You’re a remarkably fast learner. Most Temporalists took years to achieve this level of control. You did it in an hour.”

“I watch a lot of training montages.”

“Well, it’s working.” He stood. She got herself up in stages. “You should rest. Tomorrow we’ll start coordinating with FAERIS, practicing translocation protocols, running through intervention scenarios. But for now—you’ve earned a break.”

“Where am I supposed to sleep?”

“Right. Quarters.” He led her out into a corridor where the lights had gone over to a dimmer cycle, evening by facility decree. “Basic but functional. Probably better than the penthouse you’ve been hiding in for months.”

“Hey. My penthouse has excellent TV.”

“Our quarters have TV too. Also running water, a bed that’s not a couch, and food that didn’t come from a gas station.”

“Sold.”

They walked. Somewhere below them the command floor went on tracking every wrong thing on the planet, while up here it was quiet enough to hear the air system breathe.

“Oh, almost forgot.” He stopped and dug in a pocket and came out with a small flat case.

“What’s this?”

She opened it. Inside was a silver crest — a shield with a crack down its center — CADENS embossed across the top, and below it a single word: AUERBACH

“Your codename. If you choose to accept it.” She looked at the word. “Everyone operational gets one—Howitzer, Greyhound, Mercury. Director Bourn picked yours herself. But if you want something else—”

“Auerbach,” she said. The word meant nothing she could trace. It was not an acronym she could unpack, or a joke she could catch. Bourn had chosen it, and Bourn explained nothing, and somehow that made it easier to hold. It was a name with no history attached. She could be the history herself.

“You don’t have to use it,” Ryu said. “We can come up with something else. Chronos, Tempus, um . . . Freeze Frame? That one’s terrible, sorry—”

“No.” She closed the case and kept it in her hand. “Auerbach is fine. It’s . . . it’s good.”

“Yeah?”

“Yeah.”

The corridor ended at a door stenciled TEMPORARY QUARTERS 7.

“This is you,” he said, opening the door with a tap of his badge. “Door locks from inside, bathroom has toiletries, there’s a tablet on the desk with facility maps and protocols. If you need anything, the comm system is—” He caught himself. “Actually, you probably just want to sleep.”

“Desperately.”

“Right. Sleep. Good. I’ll—I’ll let you do that.” He got three steps down the corridor and turned around. “Hey. For what it’s worth? You’re doing really well. I know this is all overwhelming—new powers, new organization, dying slowly—but you’re handling it better than I would.”

“You don’t know how I’m handling it.”

“I’ve been watching the surveillance footage,” he said. “I know how you were handling it before. This is better.”

Then he was gone around the corner before she could build an answer. She stood in the doorway a moment, holding the case and not building one.

The room was small and clean and belonged to no one, and it held a bed, a desk, a chair, and a lock she controlled. She sat down on the bed, and the bed was softer than her couch, a finding she was uniquely qualified to certify. She set the case on the nightstand where she would see it first.

The day had held one deal, one handler, one suit, one mask, one panic button, one codename, thirty percent and then half, another year and maybe more. She had spent the whole day doing something, and the something was slowly turning her into a cosmic artifact, but it was doing, and doing was new.

Small victories. She was asleep before the lights finished dimming.
