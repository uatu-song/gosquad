Night Knight jumped where he stood. Gravity handed him back the usual couple of feet. The team watched him expectantly anyway. He shook his head and reset into an athletic crouch. He focused on some point above the rafters and jumped again, straining for it. He came down on the same mat, the same man, disappointed.

“I swear, it was like . . .” He carved a long flat arc through the air with one hand. “Fffffwwwweeeuuuuhhhh!”

Firas watched from a plyo box by the office door. He had picked the box because sitting on it looked voluntary, and because standing for a whole meeting was past him, since Ruth had ordered bed and he had bargained her down to this, seated, one morning only.

“I thought I was going to get arrested,” Night Knight said, dropping his arms. “I was on the rooftops when on one particular jump, I just frickin’ flew. Well, it was more like a super-jump if I’m being honest, not like Superman flying. But it was like a quarter-mile, based on how many blocks I covered in a single bound!”

Ruth stood against the doorframe with her arms folded. “Firas, I know it sounds crazy. I was there and I’m still struggling to believe it.” She had walked him through the bridge part twice already, slowly, in the cadence she saved for bad labs: Gloom Girl mid-air over the river, then gone between one blink and the next. Then a body dropping out of an empty sky four blocks inland, unhurt, and a third telling that had not made any of it smaller.

“Look, look, look!” Sable had found the remote and was aiming it at the wall TV, urgently thumbing the volume up.

“. . . reports of a burglar and alleged assault perpetrator jumping then . . .” The helicopter footage was grainy and wide, a small figure balanced on the bridge rail. “. . . vanishing into thin air. The police are withholding speculation but experts think this is the work of a master illusionist.”

The anchor handed off to an ad break, and Harding Kain filled the screen in a three-piece suit, heavy rings catching the studio light, promising a country that worked again. Ruth crossed the mat and put the screen dark with her thumb.

“It probably has something to do with what happened that night at the warehouse. We all teleported out of there, out of range of the RPG. We were all dead if not for that.”

Gloom Girl’s hand shot up. “Maybe I did it!” She sat cross-legged on a crash mat with her hood up. “I mean . . . I don’t remember doing it, but I don’t remember doing it last night, either. Maybe my powers are panic-based?”

“None of us can do it again,” Battlea put in, rotating her left shoulder, testing where last night had left its mark. “We’ve been trying.”

Firas leaned forward on the box. “Let’s . . . table that for now. What was with the police presence last night?”

“Yeah, that was extra as hell,” Gloom Girl agreed, tugging the hood lower over her eyes.

“If what you all are telling me is true . . .” He heard himself dole the rest of it out in pieces, each piece heavier to lift than the last. “. . . and you all can’t control your . . . ‘powers’ . . . we may have to discuss . . . disbanding.”

“Disbanding?” Night Knight echoed.

“It’s for everyone’s safety,” Firas told them, and even he could hear how little that weighed.

“No offense, jefe,” Sable offered. “If any one of us cared that much about our personal safety, we wouldn’t be going out every night dressed like urban ninjas looking for trouble.”

“We’re not quitting,” Battlea said.

“Look,” Firas said. He pushed himself upright off the box, one hand flat on it for the launch, because some sentences could not be delivered sitting down. “What do you think? I started this. I trained you all. And guess what? That makes me responsible for you all. And last night, it could have ended in the worst ways,” he went on, his eyes on Gloom Girl. “We have to consider ending it before it comes to that.”

“Even if we disband,” Gloom Girl pointed out. “You know we’re still going out on our own. We’re safer together.”

He ran that one four different ways, hunting for the counter, and there wasn’t one, so he sat back down.

“My question is,” Night Knight said. “How did the cops know where we were all going to be? It seems like all of us faced down a whole precinct each. They kettled us in three different areas of the city at the same time. What are the odds?”

“What are you saying, Night Knight?” Firas asked.

“I’m saying . . . who among us . . . leaked?” He let each pause sit there and do its work.

Every eye in the room found Gloom Girl. And settled there.

“What?”

“Gloomy,” Ruth said. “We know.”

“That’s not fair. I don’t know a thing about any of you.”

“No one should,” Firas answered. “That’s how I recruited you all. Individually. But you’re the most public figure in the room.”

“I don’t even talk to my dad anymore. Do you guys know that?” She looked from face to face, demanding a flicker of surprise, and got none.

“We do,” Ruth told her. “Of course. But that doesn’t mean your father, the Caledonia Police Chief, doesn’t have the means to track you anyway.”

“I . . . I don’t know,” Gloom Girl managed, crossing her arms and turning her face toward the climbing wall.

The silence stretched thin, and Sable was the one who paid to break it.

“I’m a director at a non-profit,” he said. “My day job. We do community organizing for things like food drives and consumer advocacy. My name is Victor. Victor Hernandez.”

Four years of masks, handed over in one plain sentence, and Battlea straightened up beside him before it could cool.

“I’m Leah Turner. I’m a barista at the Bean Post. I’m also the jammer on the Caledonia Roller Derby team.”

“Ben. Bukowski. I’m a . . .” Night Knight hunted for a dignified way into it.

“Personal trainer,” the whole room answered at once.

“That obvious, huh?” He grinned sheepishly at the floor.

“I’m Ruth Carter. I’m a doctor,” Ruth said, crossing to the crash mat and crouching to Gloom Girl’s level. “We trust you, Tess. That’s not what Night Knight . . . Ben was saying.”

“It’s about time you all did this,” Firas allowed. “It was starting to get weird.”

That bought the first laugh of the morning, a small one. Tess did not join it; she unfolded her arms and got to her feet.

“There’s something else I need to say.” She took a breath. “I’ve been . . . posting about us. On social media. The Go Squad stuff, the videos, the hashtags—that’s me. I started it.” She pressed on before anyone in the room could load a reply. “I know you said stay secret, Firas, but people needed to see. Someone was fighting back. Caledonia needed that hope.”

“Tess—” Firas lifted a hand.

She rode straight over him. “I’m sorry I didn’t tell you earlier. But it’s working. People believe in us. They actually have hope that things can change.” Her chin came up. “And now with these . . . powers? We could actually be what I’ve been telling them we are. Real heroes.”

Battlea reached over and squeezed her shoulder. “People did need to see. You weren’t wrong about that.”

“Though it does mean we’re kind of . . . public now. That’s more exposure than we planned.” Night Knight shifted his weight uncomfortably.

“Exposure that’s giving people hope,” Ruth countered. “That matters.”

Firas looked around the room and understood the vote had ended before it opened. “Just . . . be careful what you post,” he said at last. “If we have powers now, we’re in a different category. Bigger risks.”

“I know,” Tess promised. “I will be.”

The room loosened by a degree, but Ruth did not.

“Firas,” Ruth said. “What about Ahdia?”

The dagger sat on the filing cabinet just inside his office door, where he had set it that morning, squarely in his line of sight, so that he would stop waiting for the phone to ring. He looked at it instead of at her.

“She didn’t make it out,” Firas said. “The warehouse. She was . . . she didn’t make it.”

“I’m sorry, man,” Night Knight offered.

Something rose in Firas then, fast and wordless, like water coming under a door, and he did the only thing he ever did with a thing that rose. He outran it.

“Honestly? Maybe it’s for the best.” The crack in the middle of the sentence surprised him, and he kept going over it. “My sister was already dead inside anyway. All she did was sit in that penthouse watching TV eight hours a day and eight hours a night, living on raspberry freezes and gummy bears. She gave up on life years ago.” He dragged the heel of his hand across his eyes and one tear got loose and headed down his cheek. “At least this way it was quick.”

The tear stopped halfway down.

So did everything else. The dust in the window light hung like stars, Ruth was fixed mid-breath, and Sable’s hand had stopped short of his own jaw. Nothing in the room needed to move, so nothing did.

Behind the big blue foam vault at the far end of the activity area, Ahdia Bacchus got to her feet.

She had heard every word. Eight hours a day and eight hours a night. He had the numbers right, and the rest she was not going to sort now, or possibly ever.

She crossed the floor between the mats. Her sneakers made no sound, none, not even to her. She kept her hands in close and touched nothing, because the room had the exact look of a paused frame, and the one rule everybody knows about a paused frame is that you don’t smudge the screen.

She stopped in front of her brother. The tear hung on his cheek, mid-fall, lit from the side. He had called her dead inside with wet eyes, and both of those were his, and neither one canceled the other. She shook her head at him. Then she stuck her tongue out, the full childhood version, all the way.

“Very mature, Ms. Bacchus.”

“What the—” She spun around.

It hovered at eye level, small and sleek and metallic, not quite committed to being real, its edges a shade too clean for the room around it. She knew it. It had hung in the warehouse smoke, and waited beyond her penthouse window at three in the morning, and found her twice in the hours she spent teaching herself what she could do now. A light on its face swept her from head to foot.

“Wait—”

The light climbed past white, and space folded inward.

---

The tear finished its fall. Nobody had spoken since he said it, and the silence in the room had a current running through it now.

“Wait, what?” Ruth cut through it. “What did you just say?”

For half a second Firas would have sworn somebody had been standing in front of him, near enough to touch. He looked past Ruth. The blue vault at the far end, the empty mats, nobody at all. Grief, he decided, inventing company for him, and he put it down and left it down.

“Did you seriously just say ‘maybe it’s for the best’ about your sister dying?” The quiet in the question was worse than any volume Ruth owned.

“I didn’t—” He got two words out.

“She was shot protecting people she didn’t even know!” Ruth took a step toward him. “She died in that warehouse trying to help us, and you just—”

“Ruth—”

“‘Already dead inside’?” Gloom Girl had gone small on her mat, hood forward, knees up. “That’s what you think about people who are depressed?”

“No, I didn’t mean—” The edges of the room went white on him.

“Then what did you mean?” Battlea’s arms were crossed again. “Because it sounded like you meant exactly that.”

“I was grieving, I didn’t—” He reached in every direction and found no handholds anywhere.

“Grieving doesn’t make you cruel,” Night Knight said, with no heat in it at all, which made it land harder. “That was cruel, man.”

He had meant every word, and he had not meant one of them, and both things stayed. On the filing cabinet the dagger lay where he had put it, meaning what he had put it there to mean.

“I don’t—” The crack went all the way through this time. “I don’t know how to do this. I don’t know how to—”

His legs gave out the rest of it, and he went down onto the plyo box hard enough to feel it in the wound, elbows on knees, face in his hands.

“You process it,” Ruth told him, sitting down beside him on the box, close, not touching. “You feel it. You don’t make it about whether she was good enough to deserve being alive.”

Nobody else moved for a while and the window light had gone back to ordinary, the dust all settled out of it. In the corner by the blue vault, the air stayed still, and stayed empty.
