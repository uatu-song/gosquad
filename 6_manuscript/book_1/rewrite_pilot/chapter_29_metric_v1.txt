The bank leaned another degree and went on making its sound, low under everything else on the street, a note with no end built into it.

The pull found her hair first. It lifted the loose ends off her shoulders and carried them forward across her face, gentle about it, and then less gentle. Her suit went next, every fold of it pointed one direction, the sleeves emptying toward the crater like sails.

Then the pavement gave up what was lying on it. Glass came off the road in a sheet and crossed the intersection at waist height. A parking sign tore out of its footing. Brick and grit and a torn strip of black uniform crossed the road and went into the point where Kain had compressed, turning two feet above the crater floor, and nothing came out the other side of it.

Her body went third, tipped at the hip and the shoulder, her weight sliding off her heels and onto the balls of her feet without anybody asking her.

“Everyone back!”

“Fall back! NOW!”

Mack had his people moving before the second word finished. Twenty of them came off the crater rim in one piece, backward, weapons up, boots working against a floor that wanted them the other way. Nobody ran. They gave ground like men who had practiced giving ground, backward and in order, and not one of them turned around to look at it.

Ruth had her arm.

“We need to move. Can you—”

The rest of it went under the bank. The upper floors let their windows go in order, one row and then the row beneath it, and the glass did not fall. It flew, all of it, sideways, into the point. The steel behind the glass made a sound she felt in her teeth. Then the whole face of the building came off vertical and stayed there, leaning, working out how it wanted to do this.

Ten stories of it, and every person on the street standing underneath.

She could see all of them from where she was. Leah had Victor half off the pavement with his arm across her shoulders. Night Knight was coming down the front of the pharmacy with one elbow locked against his ribs. Gloom Girl held position by the overturned bus, arms out from her sides, leaning back against the drag like somebody on the deck of a boat.

“Go,” she said, and got both hands into Ruth’s harness.

“What?”

“No. We’re not—”

“GO!”

She shoved, and there was nothing kind in it, no measure in it at all. Everything left in her shoulders went into Ruth, and Ruth went back three steps and sat down hard in the glass.

“Get them out! All of them!”

“No. Don’t you—”

Ahdia turned around and walked at the crater.

“Stop her!”

Ruth came off the glass in a dead run and got four strides. Then her feet stopped being hers. The pull took her at the hip and turned her, and she went down and caught the base of a parking meter with both arms and stayed there. Leah made it as far as the light pole and stopped for the same reason. To cross the last of that ground either of them would have had to let go of what they were holding on to.

“AHDIA!”

“Don’t you DARE do this!”

The walking got harder, not easier, and that was the part she had not planned for. The current wanted all of her at once. She would not go all at once, so every foot of it was work, the soles of her bare feet dragging over grit and broken glass, the cold coming up through them, the suit hammering flat against her body.

At ten feet the ground under her opened in a spiderweb, and she planted herself in the middle of it and stopped.

Arms wide. Right hand out at the point, left hand back at the city behind her.

She went down into the cold place behind her sternum and asked for the red.

It came out of her right palm and into the core of the thing, and it was not a beam and it was not a wave, it was permission. Whatever the thing at the center of the crater was doing, it could do it now. Whatever it had left to burn, it could burn all of it in one breath and be finished with the street.

The point brightened, and the ground under her jumped hard enough to take her off her heels.

It was not enough. It was drinking the road and it would take the bank next, and the red on its own was going to need longer than the building had.

So she opened her left hand and gave the city the other direction.

The blue went out behind her and wrapped forward, and she felt it close over the crater like a palm coming down over a struck match. The pull she had used her whole life, running as a shell around the acceleration, holding the edge of the thing still while the inside of it raced.

Both directions at once, through one body, in opposite hands.

The Seed could do it. That was the thing nobody had tested and nobody had priced. The Seed could do it, and she was only the wire it came through, and the wire was not rated for this.

Her skin opened. The fissures came up both forearms and across her chest under the suit, splits with edges to them, and what showed inside was not blood. It was white. It had structure in it, and it went down further than a body goes.

She heard herself screaming and could not feel it in her throat.

Her vision came apart into panes, and at the edge of every pane there was a corridor with doors in it. She shut her eyes and refused it. It stayed where it was, behind her eyes, waiting.

Then there were hands over her hands.

Warm ones, human ones, and she knew before she turned her head, because there was one person on this street who would cross the last ten feet of it to hold on to her instead of pull.

“I’m not losing you again!”

He had both palms over her knuckles, pushing her hands out further into the shapes she was holding them in, and he had nothing on him. No Seed. No field. Skin, and everything he had.

The cracks came up his forearms, the same splits, the same white underneath.

“No—”

“You can’t—no Seed—you’ll—”

He did not move, and nothing in his hands changed.

“Let go!”

“Firas, let GO!”

“No.”

“We do this together.”

He said it without strain, and that was how she knew the argument was already over, because he had decided it somewhere back before he ever touched her, and he had never once reopened a thing he had decided.

Her left hand knew what to do before the rest of her caught up. It went to her hip, to the thing that had been riding there since the medical bay, the one she had faked, the one that had gone into her palm instead of her leg. It came free into her fingers. It was for him. It had always been for him.

She could not turn her hand over.

The fingers were going. She could see the crater through them, and the injector sat in a hand that was in more places than one, falling and floating and lying still, all of it at the same time, and no version of her hand could close on every version of it.

“Firas—”

Then the street was gone.

The corridor was not bleeding in at the edges any more. It was all of it, floor and ceiling and both directions, and the doors ran away from her past where seeing stopped, and every one of them was lit a different color.

She looked for her hands and there were no hands. There was light in the shape of where she had been, and inside the light there was structure, depth going down through her that had nothing to do with meat, time laid open along it, older versions of her overlapping the present one like frames of film stacked out of order.

No mouth. No lungs. Nothing to scream with and nothing that needed to.

The doors were lives, and she knew it without being told. Behind one of them was an apartment she never left, a couch, a television, forty years of that and nobody hurt by it. Behind another was a warehouse floor and her own body cooling on it. Behind another, close, standing open a crack: Firas alive, and no Ahdia anywhere in it at all.

Something turned its attention on her.

It was at the far end of the corridor and it was directly in front of her, and both of those were true. It had never been human. It had never been anything that had to work its way up to being what it was.

It looked at her, and she looked back, and there was recognition in it going both directions, arriving with no words attached to it.

She had questions, all of them, everything she had wanted to ask since the warehouse.

It acknowledged her. Then it took its attention elsewhere, and whether that was dismissal or whether it was permission she could not tell, and nothing in the corridor was going to tell her.

Then something took hold of her and put her back.

It was violent, and it arrived all at once: she was torn open and reassembled inside the same instant, the corridor shattering, dimensions folding down and down, everything she had been able to see crammed back into three directions and one clock.

Back into a body. Back into flesh, which hurt, and weighed something, and had edges.

Air went into lungs she had forgotten she owned. Pavement came up under her knees, cold and solid and stupid with reality.

Firas was inches from her face.

There was a sharp pain low in her abdomen, bright and specific, and she looked down. The autoinjector stood against her with his thumb still on the plunger.

He looked up at her, still smiling, and there was nothing in his face that was fighting anything.

Ruth had both hands locked on the arm that had come back far enough to hold, her face pulled out of shape with it, screaming something the roar took before it got anywhere. Behind Ruth: Victor with both arms around Ruth’s waist and all his weight back. Leah holding Victor. Ben anchoring Leah, boots braced, shoulders down. Tess at the end of it with her arms around Ben and her legs jammed against the overturned bus.

All of them hauling. All of them on the wrong side of the pull and refusing to be on any other side of it.

They had come for her, all of them, and nobody had asked her first.

“NO!”

She got a hand on his wrist and tried to tear it off the injector, and she tried to stand, and the chain let her do neither.

“Ahdia, we can’t—the pull—”

He was still smiling.

His mouth moved. The roar took every sound out of it, and she was close enough to see his teeth and not close enough to hear one syllable, so she took the words off his lips instead.

“Oh hey,” his lips said.

“I knew you were in there somewhere.”

“Firas, please—”

The cracks went up his neck and across his jaw and the light came through them from inside, more of it every second and less of him.

Nothing dragged him. He moved, and the space he was standing in shifted over and took him along with it, and his feet never left the ground so much as the ground stopped being where he was.

He kept his eyes on her the whole way.

At the edge he looked back once.

Then he went in, and the light and the energy and all of it, everything that had ever been her brother, went down into a space smaller than a fist and was absorbed.

Gone.

The point pulsed once more, brighter than the street could stand, and then it was not there. The red had finished what it was doing. Whatever had been in the middle of it to burn was burned.

Air came in from every direction at once to fill the hole where the thing had been, and the pressure hit like a wall going past, and everything the intersection had put in the air came down and clattered and rolled and stopped.

The bank groaned. It held. Then there was no sound anywhere.

The chain came apart. The line of them went down at once, people who had been pulling against something that stopped existing, and they landed in a heap in the glass and lay there breathing.

Ruth had not let go of her arm. She had not let go of it once, and she used it now to drag Ahdia into the middle of the pile, into the elbows and the knees and the shaking, and somebody’s hand closed on the back of her neck and stayed.

Ahdia sat in the center of all of them and let them hold on.

Across the intersection, where the crater had been, there was nothing. No body. No mark on the ground except the scorch, and the scorch was going pale at the edges already.

She was alive because he decided she would be.

She never got to say goodbye.
