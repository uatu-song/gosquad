Star Trek had prepared her for a shimmer and a polite chime. Star Trek had lied. Every atom she owned screamed at once that “here” had been a lie all along, and the lie was being edited out from under her, cell by cell, each one getting the memo at a different moment. No travel happened. It was more like being recast mid-episode. Reality changed its mind about her address, and the only calm thing anywhere in her was the Hyper Seed, sitting where her heart should be, reading the correction the way she used to read subtitles.

She reassembled in the dark, on cold concrete, on her hands and knees, and threw up. She did it again, thoroughly. Then she stayed down until her arms agreed to hold her.

The dark turned out to be the shadow of something large and humming. Ahdia spat, wiped her mouth on her sleeve, and made herself look up.

Gravel under her palms. A low parapet. A sky the exact color of dishwater. The humming thing was an air-conditioning unit she knew personally, because Firas had once spent an entire dinner mourning the three grand it cost to replace after Night Knight tried a wall run on it. The Academy roof, then: she was sitting directly above the room she had just left, close enough that her own funeral was probably still in session under her sneakers.

Two strangers stood near the roof edge, unbothered by the drop behind them. The woman was silver-gray and somewhere past fifty-five, dressed for a conference room that billed by the hour, and she studied Ahdia the way people study lab results, patiently and from above. The man beside her had a military posture his civilian clothes could not talk him out of. He carried two paper coffee cups.

“Apologies for the disorientation,” the woman said, in the practiced tone of an airline announcing a delay it had caused. “Translocation affects everyone that way. It doesn’t improve with repetition.”

Something to look forward to, then.

“Ms. Bacchus,” the woman went on. “I’m Director Harriet Bourn. CADENS. This is Colonel Bentley Mack.” She let a pause open up, one you could have parked a car in. “We need to talk.”

“You’ve been watching me.” The words came out scraped raw, as if she had spent the trip between there and here screaming.

“Yes.”

“The whole time.”

“Yes.”

“Since the warehouse.”

“Before that, actually. We were monitoring Kain. You became relevant when you bonded with the Hyper Seed.”

Relevant. She filed the word away for some colder hour with the TV on. “Where’s my brother?”

“Downstairs. With his team. Time resumed normally after we extracted you.” Nothing in Bourn’s face moved while she delivered any of this. “They’re processing what he said about you.”

Processing. A generous word for a room where “already dead inside” was still circling the mats with “maybe for the best” flying wing behind it. Her stomach rolled again. She decided, officially, that it was still the translocation.

“You should sit,” Bourn told her.

A folding chair stood open on the gravel. Ahdia was fairly sure it had not existed four seconds ago. Naturally, these people owned footage of her with her “tongue out” at her frozen brother. Somewhere a log entry read “throwing up on gravel,” timestamped and cross-referenced. They had known she would need a chair before she did. She sat, mostly because her legs voted early.

Mack crossed the roof and held out one of the cups. “It’s terrible,” he said. “But it’s caffeinated.”

The cup was warm and real and stupidly ordinary. Her hands closed around it the way disaster-movie survivors close around blankets.

“What do you know about CADENS?” Bourn asked.

“Literally nothing. Except you have creepy drones and you kidnapped me with physics.”

“We didn’t kidnap you. We relocated you for a conversation.”

“That’s just kidnapping with better PR.”

Mack’s mouth moved a quarter of an inch. On him that apparently counted as applause.

“CADENS,” Bourn said, “is the Cataclysm Activity Detection and Engagement Network Sentry. We monitor and respond to cosmic-level threats. Events that fall outside normal understanding. Dimensional incursions. Reality fractures. Artifacts like the Hyper Seed.”

“So you’re like . . . government X-Files.”

“Something like that.”

“And you want something from me.”

“We want to show you what we know. Then we’ll discuss what happens next.”

The air above Bourn’s shoulder rippled, and the drone was simply there, the way a fish is there once the water settles. It was the same one, and its surface slid like oil on water, colors moving across it without ever choosing one. Ahdia’s grip tightened on the cup.

“What is that thing?”

“Field-Adaptive Entanglement Reconnaissance and Infiltration System,” Bourn answered, each syllable worn smooth from years of use. “FAERIS for short. Advanced surveillance and translocation technology. This particular unit has been tracking you since the warehouse.”

“Tracking me.”

“Among other things.” That was Mack, into his coffee.

Bourn’s eyes stayed level, and somehow that was worse than any amount of volume. “You’ve been operating under the assumption that you were hidden. Training in secret. Helping your brother’s team without them knowing. We need you to understand: you were never hidden. We’ve been watching since the beginning.”

All that sneaking around she had been so proud of. Invisibility was the one thing she had been certain she owned, and it had been a set the whole time, and she had been the show without ever once getting to read the script.

“FAERIS,” Bourn said. “Warehouse incident. Night of August 14th. Begin playback.”

The drone brightened. Light with shape in it rose off its shell. The warehouse assembled itself above the gravel in translucent gray-blue, rafters and catwalks and crates, correct down to the smell her memory supplied for free. And there, easing along a catwalk with a wooden plank held like a sword nobody had issued her, went a small determined idiot in pajamas.

“Wait,” Ahdia blurted. “Is that . . . what I was wearing?”

“Business casual pajamas,” Mack supplied. “And a wooden plank.”

“We had three drones positioned around the warehouse,” Bourn continued. “Monitoring Kain’s activity. You and Dr. Carter were . . . unexpected variables.”

The footage ran on. Ruth arrived in the little gray world, fluid as water finding a drain. Then the gunman. Then the muzzle flash, her own past knees buckling, a dark stain opening across a tiny pajama shirt. Ahdia’s hand went flat against her own chest, against unscarred skin, checking. Nothing there now. It didn’t help as much as advertised.

“FAERIS,” Bourn prompted. “Her vitals at impact.”

“Heart rate dropping. Blood pressure 60 over 40. Survival probability: 3.7%.”

“You should have died,” Mack told her, with the flat courtesy of a man reporting weather. “We thought you did.”

So she had now watched her own death scene in dailies, with the crew, and the coffee trembled slightly in her hands, and she let it.

The rescue ran compressed: smoke, goggles, small gray figures moving in choreography she recognized from underneath. Kain shouldered the launcher personally. The rocket left the tube.

“Then this happened,” Bourn said, and the little world stopped. The rocket hung mid-air, exhaust sculpted behind it. On the warehouse floor, the dying girl stood up.

Ahdia came halfway out of the chair. “What the hell?”

“FAERIS,” Bourn said. “Time dilation parameters.”

“Gravitational anomalies detected across 47-meter radius,” the drone reported. “Subject vital signs: heart rate zero, respiration zero, body temperature dropping. Subject remains ambulatory.”

Heart rate zero. She had walked around clinically dead and improvised. She added it to the list of words that would need a colder hour.

The display slid to the crate. Her past self reached in. The thing that refused to be three-dimensional folded into a point of light, and the light ran up the tiny arm and sank into the tiny chest, and the figure convulsed and dropped. On the roof, one full second late, Ahdia’s whole body flinched in sympathy.

“What happened there?” she asked, though part of her had voted against knowing.

“We’re not sure,” Bourn admitted, and seemed to dislike the taste of it. “Our sensors detected massive energy discharge. Visual distortion. Something that might have been communication, but we couldn’t capture it. You were unconscious for 2.3 seconds. What did you experience?”

Ahdia looked into her coffee for a long moment. “I saw . . . things. Images. It was like having a whole encyclopedia downloaded into my brain in two seconds except I couldn’t read any of it. Just flashes. Something massive—I can’t even describe it, like trying to describe a color that doesn’t exist. A moon forming. Earth, but wrong, like from space billions of years ago. And . . .”

The end of that sentence went somewhere she couldn’t follow. She let it go. “This sounds insane.”

“Ms. Bacchus, you bonded with an interdimensional artifact while dying. ‘Insane’ is baseline.”

“I felt like I was being watched. By something huge. And then it was just . . . over.” Gooseflesh climbed her arms, and the terrible coffee did nothing against it. “When I came to, the bullet wound was healed. I knew I could freeze time. I didn’t know how I knew. I just did.”

“That’s when we knew,” Bourn went on. “Previous Temporalists took decades to achieve initial bonding. You did it while dying. In less time than it takes to blink.”

“How many?” Ahdia asked, and Bourn tilted her head a single degree. “Previous Temporalists. How many were there?”

“Records suggest seven over the past three millennia. None in the last four hundred years. We thought the lineage was extinct.”

Seven people in three thousand years, and the universe had cast a woman who once cried at a yogurt commercial. Somebody upstairs needed to fire their casting director.

“You saved him,” Mack put in. “Kain. You could’ve left him in the blast. No one would’ve known.”

On the display, her past self hauled Kain’s bulk clear of the frozen fireball, bound him methodically with rope and zip-ties, then went back for the thugs, one dragged armful at a time.

“I thought about it,” Ahdia said. “But that would make me a murderer. I already had enough therapy topics without adding that one.”

Mack’s almost-smile made a second appearance.

“We thought you’d be trapped,” Bourn added. “Ancient texts suggest Temporalists aged decades during their first freeze. They’d enter ‘The Unmoving’ and couldn’t release it. By the time they figured out how, they’d lived years in frozen time. Aged that amount in a single instant when they released.”

The tiny her sat down on the dock edge, hunched, lips moving around something no sensor had caught. Then fire jumped, smoke tore sideways, and the figure ran.

“How long was I in there?”

“FAERIS?”

“Duration: 47 minutes, 18 seconds subjective time. Real-time passage: 0.00 seconds.”

“You can measure that? To the second?”

“FAERIS tracks temporal variance to the millisecond,” Mack said. “The fastest learning curve we’ve ever recorded.”

“That’s when we made our decision,” Bourn said. “You bonded with the most dangerous artifact on the planet. Demonstrated unprecedented learning speed. And chose to save lives over tactical advantage.” She let the list settle. “We needed to know if you were an asset or a threat.”

“So you kept watching.”

“Yes.”

“Your training period was . . . informative,” Bourn resumed, as though there had been no break at all.

The display had rebuilt itself into her own building, thirty-four translucent floors of it, a tiny figure grinding up the stairwell with the enthusiasm of a condemned prisoner.

“You could’ve frozen time and walked up without effort,” Mack observed.

“I didn’t want to use it,” Ahdia said. “I had this feeling—instinct, maybe—that using it was costing me something. I thought maybe I could . . . not do that.”

“That lasted about six hours,” Bourn noted.

“Emotional trigger,” Ahdia agreed. “I had to get angry.”

“And then calm down to release it,” Bourn said. “We watched you practice. FAERIS, how many successful freeze-release cycles in the first training session?”

“Seventeen successful cycles,” the drone answered. “Subject endurance increasing. Duration extended from 6.4 seconds to six hours continuous freeze within 48-hour period.”

Ahdia lowered the cup. “Six hours?” On the display a mug hung over a frozen kitchen counter while a tiny her circled it, hands on hips, learning her own physics like a toddler god. “I thought it was like . . . a few minutes.”

“Time gets weird when you’re the one freezing it,” Mack allowed.

“Then you got ambitious,” Mack went on.

“Oh no,” she muttered.

“You were gathering intelligence on your brother’s team,” Bourn said. “Learning their capabilities. Weaknesses. Resources.”

Apartments scrolled past in gray miniature, rooms she had drifted through while their owners stood frozen elsewhere, learning who these people were from bookshelves and medicine cabinets and unwashed dishes.

“I was trying to understand them,” she protested. “I wanted to help but I didn’t know how—”

“We know.” Bourn let the interruption finish dying. “You were training yourself for tactical support operations.”

The display settled on a punching bag folding itself gently around nothing, then leaping off its chain into a wall. She pointed at the display before the pride underneath could embarrass her. “How hard did I hit that?”

Bourn glanced at the drone. “FAERIS?”

“Force amplification ratio: 1:47,” the drone reported. “Subject’s gentle contact translated to 47 times normal impact force upon temporal release.”

“Forty-seven times,” she repeated. “I barely touched it.”

“By the end of two days,” Bourn said, “you could hold a freeze for thirty minutes. Move through secured locations without leaving traces. Amplify force with precision control. You went from ‘can barely unfreeze’ to ‘tactical asset’ in forty-eight hours.” She paused. “Then the police ambush happened.”

The display went dark. The coffee had gone cold against her palms. Ahdia registered that she was angry roughly four seconds after the anger arrived, which was about her average.

“You’ve been watching me break into people’s apartments,” she said flatly. “Training. Experimenting. And you just . . . took notes?”

“We needed to understand what you were capable of,” Bourn answered. “And what you would choose to do with those capabilities.”

“So this was a test.”

“Everything is a test, Ms. Bacchus. You just didn’t know you were taking it.”

Every private fumble, every pep talk delivered to an empty penthouse, every practice run that ended with her on the floor — all of it existed somewhere now, timestamped and threat-assessed. She kept her face still and drank the cold coffee anyway, slowly, because the alternative was letting them log the reaction too.

“The force amplification,” Mack said. “Did you figure that out intentionally or was it an accident?”

“Accident,” Ahdia admitted. “I dropped a book while everything was frozen. When I unfroze, it shot across the room like I’d thrown it. So I tested it. Pushed things gently in frozen time, watched them fly when time resumed.”

“Forty-seven times amplification,” Bourn repeated. “That’s significant. A gentle push becomes a knockout blow. A tap becomes a battering ram.”

“I was trying not to kill anyone,” Ahdia insisted. “I thought if I could help them without them knowing, maybe—”

Maybe what. The sentence ran out of road and left her standing at the end of it, and she honestly did not know what had been waiting there. Maybe they would finally be proud of her at the second funeral, which sounded worse laid out flat.

“You wanted to help,” Mack offered.

“I wanted to not be useless,” Ahdia said. “For once.”

The roof went quiet except for the AC unit and its three-thousand-dollar hum. The confession hung there like a boom mic dropped into the frame.

“Your psychological profile,” Bourn said, “suggests significant depression, social isolation, lack of direction. Multiple therapists over the past decade. No employment history longer than three months. Estrangement from family members.”

“Wow. You really did your homework.”

“We needed to know if you’d be reliable.”

“And?”

“You climbed thirty-four flights of stairs because you didn’t want to waste life force helping yourself,” Bourn said. “Then you spent that life force helping people who didn’t know you existed. Broke into their homes not to steal, but to understand them. To prepare to protect them.” Her cadence never changed. “Your profile says you’re unmotivated. Unreliable. Can’t commit to anything. But in forty-eight hours, you taught yourself capabilities that took previous Temporalists decades. And you used them to save lives.”

Ahdia looked at the gravel between her sneakers while the kind words floated there with nowhere to land. Insults she could have returned fire on all day; she had a whole arsenal racked and oiled. “So what does that make me?” She made herself finish it. “Asset or threat?”

“That,” Bourn said, “depends on what you do next.”

The drone’s shell brightened again, and light gathered itself over the gravel.

“The police ambush,” Bourn said, as the light sharpened into a street she recognized. “That’s when we knew for certain.”
