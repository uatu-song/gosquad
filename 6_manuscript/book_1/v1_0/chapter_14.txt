Chapter 14

“Knew what for certain?”

Bourn stood. She rose like an executive ending a meeting, as if the chair had been her idea.

“I’d rather show you the rest at our facility. The rooftop drone projection has limitations. And we should get you off this roof before your brother’s team finishes their discussion downstairs.”

“Your facility.”

“CADENS operations center. Secure. Climate controlled. Better coffee.”

Mack’s face registered a brief professional dissent on the coffee point.

“Do I have a choice?”

“You always have a choice, Ms. Bacchus.” Bourn paused long enough for that to finish being technically true. “But if you want answers about what we know, what’s happening with Kain and the police, and what your parents were actually doing in Montana—you’ll come with us.”

Montana. Nineteen years of no rangers, no wreckage, no theory that survived daylight, and this woman had dropped the word into a sentence as casually as a room number. CADENS knew something about her parents. Bourn had just turned that something into bait and she knew it and Ahdia knew she knew it. She hated that it worked. It worked completely.

“Fine,” Ahdia said, standing before her legs could be consulted. “But if I throw up on your fancy facility floor—”

“We have protocols,” Mack said.

Somehow that was scarier than anything else on the roof. The drone drifted closer and its light climbed all three of them head to foot, brightening toward business.

“Oh no,” Ahdia managed. “Not again—”

“Deep breath,” Bourn advised. “It doesn’t help, but people feel better if they try.”

She tried. Space inverted anyway.

* * *

The second trip was worse, second-bee-sting worse: this time she knew what the universe planned to do with her and dread made a lousy anesthetic. Reality pulled her file, crossed out “rooftop” and penciled in “somewhere else” instead and every cell she owned received the correction at a slightly different moment. Star Trek still owed her an apology.

She reassembled on her hands and knees on concrete polished to such a corporate shine that she got to watch her own reflection throw up. They both did a thorough job.

“Protocol Sigma,” Mack told his radio. “Command center, level three. Standard cleanup.”

A janitor appeared unbothered inside the minute, steering a mop cart around her with the serenity of a man doing a familiar chore. Her vomit had a protocol. This happened here often enough to need a name and she was too busy shaking to decide how she felt about being a recurring event.

Once she could lift her head the room was every underground command center she had ever watched from her couch, except real and humming. Holographic displays hung in the air with nothing holding them up. People in tactical gear crossed paths with people in lab coats, and nobody saluted anybody. One whole wall ran live feeds: a storm rotating off some coastline, a sinkhole collecting a crowd, something burning under a headline in a language she couldn’t read.

“Welcome to CADENS,” Bourn said. “This is where we actually work.”

“You said better coffee.”

“I said we had coffee. Better is relative.” Bourn was already moving. “Conference room this way. The footage is already queued.”

The conference room offered a long table, a wall-sized display, and chairs with opinions about posture. The drone floated in after them and settled at head height like a housecat claiming a shelf.

“Water?” Mack held one out and she drained half the bottle in one pull, and sitting stopped being optional.

“The police ambush. Three simultaneous engagements across the city. Every member of the Go Squad kettled by coordinated police response. We were monitoring their communications. They knew exactly where your brother’s team would be.”

“How?”

“That’s what we wanted to know.” Bourn tipped her head toward the drone. “Show her.” She looked back at Ahdia. “Before the ambush makes sense, you need to see what you were doing that night.”

* * *

Gray light rose off the drone’s shell and a street she knew assembled itself over the table, and then the conference room stopped existing, because memory has better production values than any hologram.

The night of the warehouse. Hours after the crate, after the light, after the ice moved into her chest and unpacked. She ran for home, ten blocks uphill at a dead sprint, because the alternative was waiting at the docks to explain the artifact-shaped hole in somebody’s inventory. Home meant the couch. The couch was where she intended to have her breakdown, in comfort, with the TV on.

The weirdness arrived on an installment plan.

First a dog hung mid-bark, silent, all four paws off the ground and staying there. She filed it under adrenaline. A man stood checking a very expensive watch whose numbers never moved, and she filed that under broken watch and rich guy and not her problem. On the next corner a couple had their argument on pause, the woman’s finger parked an inch from the man’s sternum, neither of them blinking. A taxi idled at a green light with its driver slumped over the wheel and she nearly stopped for him, and some deep animal instinct voted no and she kept running.

And always behind her, strictly peripheral, the world kept twitching back on. The dog landed. Horns resumed. People snapped their heads down at their phones, hunting for the thirty seconds they could swear they’d just lost. She was carrying a bubble of stopped time the size of a modest living room and didn’t know it, and everyone she passed got their evening docked half a minute for standing too close.

Her working theory was hallucination. Her backup theory was that she was the Flash now, which genuinely troubled her for half a block on jurisdictional grounds, because the Flash was DC and the X-Men were Marvel, and whichever universe had drafted her hadn’t sent any paperwork.

“Wait, I was freezing time while running? I didn’t even know I was doing that.”

The conference room reassembled around her question. Bourn glanced at the drone.

“FAERIS,” she prompted. “Duration of unconscious freeze during subject’s return trajectory.”

“23 minutes continuous,” the drone reported. “Radius: approximately 6.1 meters. Subject maintaining temporal field unconsciously while in motion.”

“Twenty-three minutes,” Bourn repeated. “Maintaining a time freeze while running at full speed. Without even realizing it.” From her, flat delivery was what awe looked like.

The gray light swelled again, and Ahdia let it pull her back under.

Her building. The doorman stood frozen mid-wave and she waved back out of reflex, a courtesy she could feel guilty about at her leisure. Inside she jabbed the elevator call button and got nothing, jabbed it again in the special rhythm people reserve for elevators and the button stayed dark while the truth put itself together in pieces she did not want. Electricity was a thing that happened over time. Time, at present, was her fault. She lived on the thirty-fourth floor.

“You’ve got to be kidding me.”

The stairwell door accepted her without comment and shut behind her onto perfect dark—the emergency lights were electricity too, and electricity was hers now. The first eight floors went fine anyway, hand over hand on the railing, which she would later understand was the cardio equivalent of the first eight seconds of a fall. By ten her thighs had opened negotiations. By twelve she was hauling herself up the railing hand over hand, past the door she took for fifteen, where a sign she couldn’t see went on advertising the residents’ gym she had never once visited, information her body received as pure sarcasm regardless. By fifteen she was dying, not decoratively but actually dying: a heart attack at twenty-seven built from years of gas-station food and twelve-hour television days. Her heart had given up on rhythm. A hummingbird in a jar.

On the landing of twenty-two, folded over the railing, she had the idea that saved her life and complicated everything after it. The bubble had an edge; she had felt it dragging behind her all night like a bridal train made of stopped clocks. A thing with an edge had a size. A thing with a size could shrink. Just her, maybe. Just her heart. She pressed a hand over the cold spot where the Seed sat and asked her own heart, silently and sincerely, to slow down. Please. Just a little.

The cold answered. Her pulse stuttered, then settled into a calm she had done nothing to deserve.

“Oh,” she said as the stairwell swam gently sideways. “Oh that’s—”

She hadn’t fixed anything, was the thing. Heart calm, legs suffocating: she had redistributed the crisis and called it a fix. Silver linings. Floors twenty-three through thirty-four happened on willpower and railing and her own voice out loud in the dark. At the top she released her heart like a held breath, and her pulse came slamming back with interest, every postponed beat delivered at once, worse for the delay. She sat on the top step until her legs re-enlisted.

Her key found the lock on the third try. The penthouse was dark and the TV was off, and the TV was never off; the wrongness of that reached her before the shape on the couch did. Firas lay on his side, one arm trailing to the floor, still in yesterday’s clothes.

He wasn’t breathing.

“Firas?”

It came out at a pitch she didn’t recognize as hers. She crossed the room with no memory of crossing it.

“Firas!”

Nothing moved. Her brain, helpful to the end, laid out the math. She had run home inside a bubble of stopped time. She had carried it through her own front door. She had parked it on her brother, and her stupid cosmic time powers had done to Firas what a shotgun couldn’t and she had killed the only—

The clock. The wall clock above the TV, the one that ran six minutes fast on purpose, held its second hand still.

“Oh you’ve got to be kidding me.”

Apparently panic pulled the same trigger as everything else. Her fear for him had frozen him, frozen the room and the clock and the air, and kneeling over her maybe-dead brother she could not work out how to let go. She tried the mental equivalent of Ctrl-Z. She tried unfreeze, undo, cancel, whatever the opposite of a scream was. She ordered the clock to move and the clock declined, and the ice in her chest sat there, patient and bright, waiting for something she didn’t know how to hand over.

“Please,” she whispered, and the word carried none of the command she’d been attempting, which turned out to be the trick. “Please please please I can’t—I can’t do this again, I can’t lose—”

The clock ticked.

Firas pulled in a breath, long and rude, and let it go. Asleep. He was asleep on her couch, dead to the world in the ordinary reversible way, and she knelt there with her hands hovering over him, relearning how to exist.

So that was the lesson, delivered at the maximum available cost because that was the only tuition she ever paid: emotion pulls the trigger. Calm releases it. A device keyed to panic and unlocked by serenity, issued to a nervous system already running hot. What could possibly go wrong.

Firas mumbled something with no consonants in it and rolled over into the cushions.

She stood up on legs with strong opinions and went to the kitchen, and there on the counter sat that morning’s coffee mug, an inch of cold coffee poured by a woman who had never frozen a city block or a heart or a brother. She had to learn to control this thing. Because next time he startled her, dropped a glass or banged a cabinet or laughed too suddenly behind her, the freeze might not ask permission and might not let go, and she could freeze him to death for the crime of saying good morning. She stood in the dark kitchen a long while, staring at the mug.

* * *

The mug had survived the night, and the next afternoon Ahdia came back for it, rested and reasonably caffeinated and ready to negotiate.

“Freeze,” she whispered at it.

The mug declined.

She squared her shoulders and worked down the full menu. “Stop. Pause. Time out. Za Warudo!”

The last one honestly felt the most promising, since her only working reference for stopped time was an anime villain, and the universe had already shown a taste for her references. The mug sat there. She tried the Force-choke hand next, fingers clawed at empty air, elbow cocked, and held the pose long enough to catch her reflection in the microwave door and feel as stupid as she looked.

Under the old bullet wound the Seed hummed along, a cold ember, awake and interested and useless. Beneath the hum ran the instinct that had ridden with her since the docks: this costs you. She didn’t know what it took from her; she only knew the feel of something running somewhere out of sight.

She dumped yesterday’s coffee and brewed a fresh cup, reasoning that if she meant to spend the afternoon threatening a beverage, the beverage should be worth drinking. Then she threatened it for another hour. Commands. Gestures. Meaningful eye contact. Nothing worked, and the not-working stacked up in her until it hit the floor of her patience and went through.

“Come ON!” Her palm cracked down on the counter—

—and the world stopped.

The steam over the fresh cup hung mid-curl, a white ribbon parked in the air. The stove clock said 2:47 PM and went on saying it. The refrigerator hum was gone, and the silence where it had lived had a texture, thick and total. Dust hung in the window light and did not fall.

“Oh,” she said.

Nothing on Earth moved except her.

“Oh shit.”

She tried to un-slam the counter. She tried the commands again in reverse order, then in a calm indoor voice, then in the wheedling tone she saved for dying electronics. The freeze ignored all of it as the mug had ignored her. Panic arrived punctually, because the first freeze, at the docks, had ended on its own timetable, like weather, and she’d had no vote. This one might never end at all: the steam might never land, and the clock might read 2:47 PM for the rest of a very long life, hers, alone in a world-sized diorama.

The panic crested, broke, and rolled back out, and it dragged the anger with it, and for one hollow second she was empty of everything but tired.

Time snapped back, the steam finished its curl, and the refrigerator resumed its low argument with itself. The clock still said 2:47 PM. The entire terror had cost the world nothing.

She stood very still and did the math, because math was a thing you could hold. Mad pulled the trigger. Calm released it, or whatever hollowed-out cousin of calm she had just fallen into.

She practiced until midnight. Getting angry on purpose turned out to be the easy half, since she owned years of material; letting go on purpose was the work. The holds ran five to ten seconds, seventeen freezes by the end of it, each release slightly less of an accident, and when she quit she felt scraped hollow, and the cold spot under the old wound ached like a pulled tooth.

* * *

The theory arrived with the next morning’s coffee, fully formed: buy time by spending time. If frozen seconds cost the world nothing, then frozen time was free practice space, a private gym the size of the universe, and she was apparently the only member.

By midmorning she could hold past a minute, and shortly after that she got scientific about it. She froze the kitchen and lobbed a pillow across it, and inside the stillness the pillow flew and flopped as pillows do, obeying her private physics without complaint. When she released, nothing dramatic happened at all; the pillow was somewhere new, with no history of having traveled.

Then the variation. She threw the next one hard and let the freeze go while it was still mid-flight.

The pillow crossed the living room like it had been fired out of something military, cracked into the wall, and brought the framed photo down with it in a burst of glass.

“Oh shit!” She was already moving toward the wreckage. “Yes!”

Both reactions were true, and neither one canceled the other. She swept up the glass and logged the finding underneath it: whatever went in gentle came out enormous. A fingertip push inside the freeze arrived, in real time, as a shove from something far bigger than her. She spent the afternoon calibrating on furniture that couldn’t sue, cushions and cereal boxes and one long-suffering ottoman, until she could dose it, tap to thump to launch.

She worked it out by the clocks: an hour inside ran to three minutes outside, which she wrote down and declined to understand. By day’s end she had banked something near six frozen hours, she could drop into the stillness at will and hold it for minutes at a stretch, and the cold under the old wound had crossed the border from temperature into pain. Her sleeve cuffs had gone soft and pale too, the fibers furred like rope just before it parts, and she could not name a thing she had done to them. She filed it under later and kept going. The meter she couldn’t see ran on.

* * *

What she did with the stillness next was go visiting.

She told herself it was reconnaissance, which was true, and that it was for their sake, which was also true, and that it had nothing to do with wanting to stand inside other people’s lives for a while, which made her two for three.

Ruth first, obviously, because Ruth was the only one of them she actually knew. The lobby guard was frozen mid-yawn when she walked in, a yawn wide enough to count fillings by, and she went past it at her leisure. Upstairs, the apartment door was unlocked. The woman fought crime on a volunteer basis and did not lock her front door, and Ahdia stood in the hallway a moment feeling offended on her behalf. Inside: The New England Journal of Medicine on the nightstand, bristling with sticky notes, a trauma surgery text underneath it. Tactical gear hung behind the winter coats, squared away with terrible neatness. The fridge wore photographs of Ruth and Firas, years of them, and Ahdia lifted one free, held it, and set it back precisely where it had been, corner to corner, and did that until her hands were steady and nobody living could have said she’d come through.

The tiny studio across town had its fire-escape window cracked two inches, so she went in that way. Its tenant was asleep on the futon in her work clothes. The pharmacy bottle on the bathroom sink read WHITFORD, TESS, and under the name, Sertraline 100mg. Ahdia knew that one; it hadn’t done much for her either. Another depressed girl making rent, sleeping where she landed. Recognition, not pity. She eased the window back to its two inches on her way out.

The community center said “nonprofit” from every surface, in the dialect that translates to “we’re one grant away from closing.” She came in past a rubber doorstop wedged for the evening crowd, past a janitor frozen mid-mop, past flyers for a food drive and ESL classes taped up at heights suggesting the ladder had been optional. The donation jar by the front desk held six dollars, all of it coins. In the back office a framed wedding photo stood on the desk, and the woman’s face in it had been scratched out, gouged deep enough to mark the glass. Ahdia didn’t touch that one; she left it where it lived and went back out through the propped door.

The last apartment belonged to a barista, per the name tag on the dresser: the Bean Post, then LEAH, then a smiley face added in Sharpie. The helmet on the wall hook was stickered over every inch and carried a star for jammer, a fact Ahdia possessed for one reason: that roller derby movie, 3 AM, a box of Thin Mints, no witnesses. A punching bag hung in the corner, held together with duct tape and spite. On the shelf sat anger-management books, and under them workbooks with actual handwriting in the margins, page after page of somebody doing the assignment anyway.

She froze the room, set two fingers against the bag, pushed like you’d test a door, and released from across the apartment. The bag went off. It swung like it had taken a full baseball swing, the chain snapped taut, and a ceiling bolt bent out of true.

“Oops,” she told the empty apartment.

By the end of the second day she could hold a freeze for thirty minutes, come and go without leaving a fingerprint of herself anywhere, and dose force from a nudge to a wrecking ball. She carried out one other finding, the kind she hadn’t gone looking for. Sticky notes, six dollars in coins, duct tape, handwriting in a workbook: none of these people were special because of what their bodies could suddenly do. They were special because they kept showing up.

* * *

Two nights after the warehouse, she learned what the practice had been for.

The Frederick Douglass Bridge, from her side of it: floodlights, a helicopter hammering the air white, and Gloom Girl up on the rail with the whole department closing in. Then she was off the rail, one middle finger raised on the way down.

Ahdia froze the night with her still in the air above the river.

She was most of the bridge away when it happened, so she sprinted the deck flat out in the stillness, hooked one arm through the cables, and hauled a frozen, folded woman in out of the dark, which mostly took nerve. The carry was the education: four blocks inland with a grown adult over her shoulders, then up a fire stair toward a roof where Ruth stood fixed at the parapet, binoculars up. Halfway up the climb her lungs went from burning to bargaining, and her body remembered the stairwell, the trick with the heart, how narrowly that trick had missed being her last idea. Wrong dial. She didn’t need the whole engine slowed, only the burn. So she put her attention into her own screaming legs as she’d put two fingers on a punching bag, and asked time to run cold there, and only there.

“Oh,” she said.

The burn in her legs folded up obediently and went quiet mid-stride.

“Oh that’s way better.”

She topped the climb sweating through everything she wore, because apparently effort still had to go somewhere no matter what the muscles agreed to.

“For such cosmic power,” she said, “it sure is a sweaty one.”

She left Gloom Girl hanging a foot above the rooftop, feet down, arms arranged, close enough that Ruth’s binoculars would be beside the point. Then she crossed three rooftops and let the night go. Somewhere behind her a girl dropped out of an empty sky, unhurt, and the story would assemble itself without help from anyone. Teleportation. The vanishing girl. Let them have it.

A low whistle brought the conference room back, and the whistle was Mack’s.

“She figured out selective time dilation. Her own muscles. Mid-climb.”

Ahdia’s hands had found the water bottle without consulting her. “Is that . . . hard to do?”

“FAERIS,” Bourn said. “Previous Temporalist development timeline for selective biological manipulation.”

“Average development time: 12 to 47 years post-bonding,” the drone reported.

“You did it in under an hour,” Bourn told her. She let the number finish landing before she went on. “That’s what made us decide you were worth recruiting. You problem-solve with cosmic abilities the way most people problem-solve with everyday tools.”

It was a compliment shaped like a lab result, and Ahdia filed it away to have feelings about later.

The display split into three feeds, three gray miniatures of one long night.

“We had eyes on all three ambush sites,” Bourn said. She nodded at the feeds in turn. “Ruth on the Frederick Douglass Bridge. Battlea and Sable at the construction site in Little Poland. Gloom Girl and Night Knight near the waterfront.”

In one feed a police dog hung mid-lunge and then was elsewhere, aimed at nothing, while a small gray Ahdia leaned her weight into other people’s punches half a second before they landed. In the second she watched herself gather Gloom Girl out of the air like fruit off a high branch. In the third, Night Knight left a rooftop at a doomed angle, and a hand closed around his ankle in the stillness and moved him two degrees.

“Coordinated perfectly,” Mack said, and he wasn’t praising anyone. “Someone fed Chief Whitford their locations. All three teams kettled at the same time.”

“Then you showed up,” Bourn resumed.

“Three simultaneous interventions within twenty minutes,” she added. On the middle feed the river hung in gray pleats below the bridge. “Ensuring no one died—police included.”

Mack turned to the drone. “FAERIS,” he said. “Total power usage duration for police ambush intervention.”

“Six hours continuous temporal manipulation across multiple sites,” the drone answered. It paused, for effect or for math. “Force amplification applied 47 times. Life force expenditure: 23% of total reserves.”

The blood left her face and took its time coming back.

“Twenty-three percent? In one night?”

Nobody in the room softened it for her, and she sat back slowly until the chair took her weight.

“I wanted them to be okay. I wanted them to think they could do this without me.”

“You gave them confidence,” Mack offered. “Made them believe in themselves.”

“While exhausting yourself,” Bourn said.

The display had moved on to an alley, where a small gray figure slid down a wall between dumpsters, folded up, and cried into her sleeve while the timestamp in the corner kept counting anyway.

“Ten minutes before you could stand,” Mack murmured. “You limped home. Took eight ibuprofen. Passed out on the couch.”

“And woke up the next day to do it again,” Bourn finished.

The feed changed again and gave her the Academy, the day after: the whole floor stopped at midmorning, Firas caught mid-sentence beside a mat, Victor frozen nodding along. A small gray Ahdia leaned in and stuck her tongue out at her brother, the full childhood version. Then the figure stumbled. A hand went flat against her chest, and even in grayscale the color went out of her.

In the conference room the gray light lay across her hands, and Ahdia leaned forward into it.

“I could feel it. Like something was burning through me. Every time I used the powers, it got colder.”

“Colder?” Bourn asked.

“The Seed. In my chest. When I freeze time, it’s cold. Really cold. But after—when I let go—it feels like it’s taking something from me. Like I’m emptying out.”

“That’s when you realized,” Mack prompted.

“That the power wasn’t free,” Ahdia said. Her eyes stayed on the small gray figure. “That it was costing me. I didn’t know how much or what exactly, but I could feel it. Like . . . like running on a battery that doesn’t recharge.”

Bourn turned to the drone. “FAERIS, bio-sign analysis from that session.”

“Cumulative degradation detected across all power usage instances. Pattern consistent with permanent cellular depletion, not temporary fatigue.”

“Permanent,” Ahdia repeated.

On the display, the gray Ahdia sat down on the Academy floor and stayed there a while. Then she stood, looked for a long moment at her brother, still frozen in the middle of whatever he was telling Victor, unfroze the room, and walked away before he could turn around.

* * *

The display went dark. For the first time since the roof, nothing was playing anywhere, and the quiet had intentions.

“That was two days ago,” Bourn said. It didn’t sound like an accusation. That made it worse. “You figured out your powers were draining you. That you couldn’t keep using them without consequence. And then you hid behind a foam obstacle to stick your tongue out at your brother.”

“I was processing,” Ahdia managed.

“You were regressing,” Bourn corrected. There was no cruelty anywhere in it. “After everything you’d accomplished—saving your brother’s team twice, mastering abilities that should’ve taken decades, preventing multiple deaths—you heard what he really thought of you.”

Her hands, she noticed from a slight distance, were still shaking. She flattened them on the table and reached for the one question that outranked the rest.

“My parents,” she said. The words needed a running start. “You’ve been monitoring this thing—the Hyper Seed. Did they . . . was their work in Montana connected to this?”

“Your parents,” Bourn said, and for the first time she seemed to weigh each word before spending it, “were working with CADENS when they disappeared in Montana. Or rather, working adjacent to us. They were tracking reports of a Hyper Seed manifestation. We were monitoring from a distance.”

“They died looking for this thing? For the thing that’s inside me now?”

“They died protecting it,” Mack answered. “From people who wanted to use it. People like Kain.”

“The Montana excavation wasn’t an accident,” Bourn told her. “Your parents discovered the Seed’s location. Someone else did too. There was a confrontation. An explosion. By the time our team arrived, your parents were gone. The Seed was gone. We thought it was destroyed.”

“Until Kain had it,” Ahdia finished.

“Until Kain had it,” Bourn agreed.

The echo sat on the table between them, and Bourn collected it before it could cool. “We don’t know how he acquired it. But we’d been tracking him for months before the warehouse. When you bonded with it—when you became it—you inherited your parents’ legacy.”

“Lucky me.”

Bourn rose and crossed to the long interior window, the one that looked down into the well of the facility: levels of it descending, screens tracking storms and sinkholes and every other way a planet can go wrong, people moving between them with somewhere to be.

“Our analysis suggests you’re running on a finite energy source,” she said to the glass.

Then she turned around, and whatever was coming had enough mass that the room felt it before she spoke. Mack went still. The drone’s light held where it was, and the only sound left was the building keeping itself alive.

“The Hyper Seed bonding replaced approximately half your life force with . . . something else. Every time you use your powers, you’re draining what remains. At a survivable rate of expenditure—short freezes, small corrections, nothing heroic—you have maybe eighteen months. The ambush was not a survivable rate. That one night cost you a quarter of everything you had left. Four more like it and we are not having this conversation.”

“Eighteen months,” Ahdia said. The number came out flat, from somewhere far away.

“Unless we can find another way,” Mack put in. “CADENS has been studying Temporalists for decades. We have resources, research, ancient texts. Maybe there’s a way to slow the drain, or restore what’s been lost.”

Bourn sat. “But we need your cooperation. We need you to work with us, not hide from us. We need you to trust us.”

“Trust the shadow government that’s been spying on me?”

“Trust the organization that knew you were at critical energy expenditure and intervened before you killed yourself helping people who don’t even know you exist,” Bourn shot back. Then the edge went out of her, all at once. “We extracted you from that academy because if we’d waited any longer, you would’ve spent the rest of your life—however short—watching your brother mourn you while you hid in the shadows.”

The drone hummed at its shelf height while Ahdia looked at her hands until she could ask it.

“So what do you want from me?”

“First, we want you to stop killing yourself. Second, we want you to let us help you figure out how to survive this. Third—” Bourn let the third one stand in line. “Kain is still out there. Chief Whitford is still hunting your brother’s team. And you’re the only one who can help them without exposing what they really are.”

“No pressure,” Ahdia muttered.

“Exactly the right amount of pressure,” Bourn returned. “The question is: are you going to keep hiding and burning through your life force alone? Or are you going to work with us?”

“Work with you how?”

“Training. Research. Mission support.” Bourn stood. “Kain is escalating. Chief Whitford is escalating. We need eyes on both of them, and you’re the only asset who can operate in ways they can’t detect or counter. In exchange, we give you every resource we have to extend your life expectancy beyond eighteen months.”

“You want me to spy for you.”

“We want you to stop Kain from acquiring more artifacts. Stop Whitford from weaponizing the police against enhanced individuals. And let us study your capabilities so we can understand what’s coming.” Bourn opened one hand. “Your personal life—your brother, his team, whatever family drama you’re processing—that’s yours to handle or not handle. We don’t care. We care about preventing the next cataclysm.”

Mack leaned forward. “But we do need an answer. Are you in or not?”

“What happens if I say no?”

“We keep monitoring,” Bourn said, unhurried. “We intervene when you’re about to do something that gets you killed. We track Kain and Whitford without your help. And when whatever cosmic threat is coming finally arrives, we hope we’re prepared enough without a Temporalist on our side.” She let that settle before she spent the rest. “But you keep burning through your energy alone. And you die in eighteen months, give or take. Alone.”

There it was. Alone was not a threat you could aim at her; alone was the apartment she had lived in for years. She thought of the alley and the sleeve. She thought of the thirty-four flights she had climbed on ruined legs rather than spend one minute of herself on her own behalf, and of every person she had saved who would never know she existed, who would never get the chance to show up for her as they kept showing up for each other.

“I’m in,” she said.

Nobody moved. The drone’s light held on her, recording, presumably for some future footage session she would also get to sit through.

“But I have conditions.”
