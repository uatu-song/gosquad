Epilogue

“—and I want to assure the good people of Caledonia that I was nowhere near downtown during this tragic incident.”

The lights were low and warm, and set where he had asked, no shadow under the eyes. Grief photographs badly. Steadiness photographs well, and steadiness in a man who has been attacked photographs better than anything money can otherwise buy.

“We believe this was an attempt by criminal elements to impersonate me and destabilize my campaign.”

He let a beat go, because the room wanted one. “The authorities are investigating. I have full confidence in our law enforcement and our intelligence agencies to get to the bottom of this terrorist attack.”

Thirty cameras. The polling would come in overnight and it would be good, and the good would be worth more than the mansion, which was insured.

“That’s all for tonight. Thank you.”

The aides took him off the riser and down a hall and into a service elevator, and the doors closed on the noise and cut it off like a switch. At the bottom of the building a man in a gray uniform looked at a screen and stepped aside.

“Clear,” one of the aides said into his earpiece.

Then a second elevator, which went down. Past the garage. Past the mechanical floors with their pipes and their painted numbers. Past four levels that appear on no drawing filed with any office in the city. Forty-seven seconds, lobby to bottom. He had counted it once, back when this part still made him nervous, and had not needed the number since.

The corridor at the bottom was seamless white, with no doors in it, and the sensors sat in the walls where the seams would have been. His shoes made no sound. He had priced this corridor. It came in over. Worth it.

The tank room hummed at a pitch you felt in your teeth. Rows of them, glass and fluid and a soft green light coming up from below, and in every one of them a body floating with its chin down and its hands open. A boy of nine. A young man with hair. Himself at forty, at fifty, at the weight he carried now and at a weight he had not been in twenty years. All of them asleep. All of them paid for.

Halfway down the second row a cylinder stood open and dry, the cradle empty, the number stenciled at eye height. Forty-seven. An acceptable loss. The accounting on it was clean.

“You look tired,” a woman’s voice said.

She was standing at the end of the row and she had not been there a moment ago. White coat, no badge on it, no age on her at all, and the proportions of her a degree off in a direction he had long ago stopped trying to put a word to.

“Forty-seven was our best integration,” he said.

“The Tamois Heart had fully bonded. Years of work. Gone.”

“And yet you’re here.”

“That’s the point of redundancy.”

“I know the point.”

He looked at the empty cylinder and not at her. “I’m living it.”

“Are you?”

She came down the row without hurrying. “Forty-seven thought he was special too. Thought the Heart made him more than a vessel. Look where that got him.”

“How long until we can reintegrate a Heart?”

“We have three more in storage. We can begin the bonding process within a week.”

“But that’s not what you should be worried about.”

“What should I be worried about?”

“Agent Auerbach saw Bellatrix.”

The hum went on around them. He took his hands out of his pockets and put them back.

“During the singularity?”

“According to the readings CADENS picked up, yes. She transcended. Briefly. Made contact with higher-dimensional space.”

“She saw the hallway.”

“That’s not possible. The Seed was dormant, we made sure—”

“Nothing is ever certain with the Seeds. That’s why they were scattered in the first place. To see what would happen. Who would find them. What they’d do with them.”

She stopped in front of the empty tank and looked into it as though something were still in there.

“Bellatrix doesn’t interfere. She observes. But she acknowledged Auerbach. That means something.”

“What does it mean?”

“It means the girl is more important than we thought. It means her death might have consequences we can’t predict.”

“It means we proceed carefully.”

He had a file on the girl. It was thin and it was cheap and it had a line through it as of yesterday afternoon.

“The plan was always to eliminate her once the Seed went dormant.”

“Plans change.”

She said it as an item already decided in a room he had not been invited into. “Focus on the election. Win the presidency. That’s your role. Let me worry about the girl.”

The door took her and closed, and the corridor beyond it made no sound.

He stood where he was, in the hum, among the rows of himself, every one of them asleep and waiting to be worn.
